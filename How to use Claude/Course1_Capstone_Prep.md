# Course1_Capstone_Prep

*A pre-Module-16 readiness guide for Course 1 — AI Essentials. Read this once around Module 9, then revisit it after Module 12 and again before you start Module 16. It does not replace Module 16; it makes Module 16 doable without panic.*

---

## Purpose of the capstone

The capstone is the place where the separate skills you built across fifteen modules stop being separate. Until Module 16, you have practiced prompt control, verification, audience fit, learning support, responsible use, privacy, research, workflows, decision support, team coordination, and reusable systems as distinct disciplines. The capstone asks you to take one realistic task you actually do — or could plausibly do — and run it end to end using those skills together.

It is not an exam. It is not a creative project. It is a working demonstration of an AI-supported workflow that you would be willing to show to a manager, a client, an instructor, or a future collaborator without having to explain away weak parts.

The capstone matters because most failures in real AI use are not failures of any single skill. They are failures of integration: a strong prompt with no verification, a clean workflow with sensitive data leaking through it, a confident decision memo with no record of what was actually checked. Module 16 is where you prove the parts hold together.

## What the capstone proves

A completed capstone proves five things, and only those five things. It is useful to be honest about this so you do not over-claim later.

It proves you can frame a real problem in terms of audience, task, constraints, success criteria, and risks — not as a vague intention. It proves you can design a workflow that uses AI for the steps where AI helps and keeps humans on the steps where judgment, verification, or accountability belong. It proves you can write, review, and reuse the prompts that workflow depends on. It proves you can run that workflow inside reasonable safety and privacy limits. It proves you can critique your own output and revise it when the critique is fair.

It does not prove domain expertise. A strong capstone in customer communication does not make you a customer-experience specialist. A strong capstone in study workflows does not make you a learning scientist. The capstone certifies AI-supported practice, not the underlying field. Saying that out loud now will save you from over-stating it on a CV later.

## When to start preparing

You can start light preparation as early as Module 9, when responsibility and accountability come into focus. Module 9 is also where the course shifts from foundational skill-building into applied work, and it is the first natural moment to ask, "What task would I want to demonstrate at the end of this?"

Active preparation begins around Module 12, once you have practiced workflow design. By Module 12 you have enough material to sketch a candidate capstone task — not commit to it, but list two or three options.

Final preparation happens during Module 15. Module 15 is the bridge into the capstone by design: the prompt pack you build there is the same pack you extend in Module 16. If you arrive at Module 16 with a finished, tested prompt pack and a chosen task, the capstone becomes a focused two-to-four-day effort instead of a scramble.

If you are reading this for the first time inside Module 14 or 15, you are not late. You are on the standard path. If you are reading this for the first time inside Module 16, slow down and complete this guide before starting the capstone work itself.

## How to choose a capstone task

Choosing the task is the single most important decision in Module 16, and it is one of the most common places learners go wrong. A weak choice cannot be rescued by strong execution. The criteria below filter out most bad choices before you spend time on them.

**Realistic.** The task must be one you actually do, plausibly will do, or could clearly explain to someone who does. A capstone built on a hypothetical job you do not have, or a project you have never touched, tends to drift into vague generality. If you can describe the audience, the input, and the desired output in concrete terms, the task is realistic enough.

**Useful.** The output of the workflow should produce something a person would actually use, not just something that proves you used AI. A weekly study summary you would re-read, a customer-response template you would actually send, a research brief that informs a decision you are facing — these are useful. A "demo report on a topic" that no one will ever read is not.

**Bounded.** The task must be small enough to complete end to end in two to four focused days. If you need a week of background research, ten interviews, or a rebuild of an existing system, the scope is wrong. A good capstone has one clear input source, one or two AI-assisted stages, one human review step, and one finished output. Anything larger should be cut down before you start.

**Reviewable.** You — or another reasonable person — must be able to tell whether the output is good. If you cannot describe what "strong evidence" looks like for this task, you cannot grade it, and neither can the rubric. A task whose quality depends on subjective preference alone ("write something nice") is not reviewable. A task whose quality depends on accuracy, audience fit, completeness, or fit-to-purpose is reviewable.

**Not too sensitive.** The task should not require you to put real customer data, real medical or legal records, real student records, or any other restricted-tier information into an AI tool. If your strongest candidate task is sensitive, you have two options: redesign the workflow to use abstracted or synthetic inputs, or pick a different task. Module 10 trained you to recognize this; the capstone is where you act on that training.

**Connected to your learner pathway.** Course 1 maps to five Jifunze pathways. Your capstone should produce evidence that matters for the pathway you intend to enter next. A Digital Work Starter learner builds workplace-credible artifacts. A Remote Work and Freelancing learner builds something a first client could see. A Small Business and Entrepreneurship learner builds an operations or customer-communication workflow. An AI Productivity Professional learner builds a prompt-pack-driven, reusable, agent-aware workflow. A Junior Tech Builder learner builds something that demonstrates disciplined AI integration. If your task does not produce evidence relevant to your pathway, ask what task would.

A task that satisfies all six criteria is rarely the most exciting candidate on your list. That is normal. Pick the disciplined option.

## Capstone task examples

The examples below are illustrative, not prescriptive. They show what a credible capstone looks like in five common contexts. If yours resembles one of these in shape — bounded, reviewable, useful — you are in the right zone.

**Student or study workflow.** A learner preparing for a specific certification or final assessment in two to three weeks. Capstone: an AI-supported study workflow that takes a syllabus or notes as input, produces a weekly study plan, generates retrieval-practice question sets at the end of each week, and includes a weak-area repair loop. Output: the workflow itself, the prompt pack that runs it, one full weekly cycle of evidence (plan, questions, repair notes), and a self-critique on whether the workflow actually helped retention versus just felt productive.

**Office or admin workflow.** An office assistant or operations coordinator who runs a recurring task — a weekly internal recap, monthly reporting, meeting-notes-to-actions, or onboarding checklists. Capstone: an AI-supported workflow that takes the standard inputs (raw meeting notes, raw weekly events, a draft report) and produces the standard output the role already requires, with explicit review and verification steps and a documented privacy boundary on what data may be pasted in. Output: the workflow, the prompt pack, one fully run cycle, and a one-page "how a colleague would use this" handoff note.

**Small business customer communication workflow.** A small-business owner or customer-service operator who handles repetitive customer messages — order confirmations, delay updates, refund explanations, follow-ups, common questions. Capstone: an AI-supported customer-message workflow with a standard prompt set, a tone and voice guideline, a privacy boundary on what customer data may be included in prompts, and a human-review step before any message is sent. Output: the workflow, the prompt pack, three sample message types fully written and reviewed, and a short note on which message types the workflow handles well and which still need a human draft.

**Freelance or client workflow.** A freelance writer, designer, analyst, or consultant who takes a typical client brief and produces a typical client deliverable. Capstone: an AI-supported intake-to-draft workflow with stages for understanding the brief, drafting, internal review, client-ready presentation, and revision. Output: the workflow, the prompt pack, one fully run cycle on a real or realistic brief, a disclosure statement the freelancer would include with the deliverable, and a self-critique on what the workflow improved versus what it did not.

**Research or reporting workflow.** A learner researching a defined question for a report, briefing, or recommendation — a market scan, a competitor comparison, a policy summary, a literature note. Capstone: an AI-supported research workflow that takes a scoped question and a small set of sources as input, produces a synthesis brief with explicit uncertainty labels and source attribution, and ends with a human-owned conclusion paragraph that does not over-claim. Output: the workflow, the prompt pack, one full brief, the verification log behind it, and a short note on what the brief does and does not establish.

If your candidate task does not fit any of these shapes, that is acceptable — but check it carefully against the six criteria above. The most common failure mode is choosing a task that is interesting but not bounded.

## Scope checklist

Before you start writing the capstone, run your task through these checks. If any answer is no, fix the scope before continuing — not after.

Can you describe the task in one sentence that names the audience, the input, and the output. Can you complete the full end-to-end workflow in two to four days of focused work, not weeks. Can you point to the source material the workflow needs, and is that source material already accessible to you. Are the AI-assisted steps clearly separable from the human steps, with at least one human review step before the output is finalized. Does the workflow produce a finished output, not just intermediate artifacts. Can you tell whether the finished output is good, using criteria you can write down. Is the workflow reusable — could you, or someone else, run it again next week on a fresh input without rebuilding it.

If all seven are yes, the scope is correct. If any are no, the most common fix is to make the task smaller. Cut the audience from "everyone" to one specific person or group. Cut the input from "all my notes" to one document. Cut the output from "a full report" to "one section of a report." A well-scoped capstone is small enough that the integration is the hard part — not the volume of work.

## Safety and privacy check

The capstone must run inside the safety floor you built in Module 10. This is the single most common place learners cut corners under time pressure, and it is the single most common place a strong technical capstone is undermined.

Walk through the workflow in your head and answer the following.

What data does the workflow need as input. Classify it against your Module 10 four-tier table — safe, caution, restricted, or never-enter. If any input falls in restricted or never-enter, redesign the workflow to use an abstracted, summarized, or synthetic version of that input. Do not proceed with the original.

What data does the workflow produce as output. Could the output, if shared, expose information about a real person, a real customer, a real client, or a real organization in a way they have not consented to. If yes, abstract the output, anonymize identifying details, or use synthetic stand-ins for the capstone submission.

Who is the audience for the output. If the workflow produces something intended for a real recipient — a customer, a manager, an instructor — confirm that you would be willing to send that exact output. If not, the workflow is not finished.

What disclosure does the output need. If the output will be shared with anyone, prepare a short disclosure statement adapted from your Module 9 accountability statement: what AI was used, what was reviewed, what the human (you) is accountable for. Include this in the capstone submission even if no one outside the course will read it. The disclosure habit matters more than this specific instance.

If the workflow involves a sector with specific data rules — healthcare, education, finance, HR — name the rules and confirm the workflow operates within them. If you cannot confirm that, change the workflow.

A capstone that ignores this check can still score well on prompt design and workflow logic and still fail the overall standard. Safety is not extra credit; it is a baseline.

## Self-critique preparation

Module 16 asks you to critique your own work. For a self-learner, this is the hardest part of the capstone. The default failure mode is being too easy on yourself. The opposite failure mode — being so harsh nothing is ever finished — is rarer but real.

Three habits make self-critique work.

The first is the twenty-four-hour rule. After you finish the workflow and the output, walk away for a day. Do not look at the work. When you return, read the output as if a colleague had produced it and asked you to review. Most weaknesses become visible inside the first ten minutes of fresh-eyes review and stay invisible in the moment of writing.

The second is the rubric-as-checklist habit. The Module 16 rubric scores problem framing, prompt and workflow design, verification and review, safety and privacy, reusability, and reflection. Before self-critique, write down what strong evidence and weak evidence look like for each criterion in the context of your specific task. Do not import generic standards; describe your task specifically. Then grade your own work against your own descriptions. This catches the common error of grading the workflow against an easier standard than the rubric uses.

The third is the named-reader test. Pick one specific real person — a former colleague, a peer from this course, a mentor, a relative who works in a relevant field — and write down what they would say if they reviewed your capstone. Imagine the comment they would actually make, not the comment you wish they would make. This works because most learners can be honest about a named reader's standards even when they cannot be honest about their own.

If, after running these three habits, you still believe the capstone is strong, it is probably strong. If any of the three surfaces a real problem, fix the problem before submitting. Self-critique that produces no changes is usually self-critique that did not happen.

## How to know you are ready for Module 16

You are ready for Module 16 when the following are all true.

You have completed Modules 1 through 15 at the 80% checkpoint threshold. You have all module artifacts saved in one folder, especially the Module 15 prompt pack, the Module 12 workflow map, the Module 10 classification table, and the Module 6 verification habit. You can describe a candidate capstone task in one sentence that names the audience, the input, and the output. The task passes all six selection criteria above. The task passes the seven-question scope checklist. The data the workflow needs is in the safe or caution tier of your Module 10 classification, or you have a clear plan to abstract higher-tier inputs. You have at least two and ideally three to four days of focused, uninterrupted work available — capstones done in scattered fifteen-minute slots tend to lose coherence. You have a way to walk away for twenty-four hours mid-process, so the self-critique step has fresh eyes to work with.

If any of these are not true, do not start Module 16 yet. Going back to fix one missing piece is much faster than going forward and discovering it later.

If all of them are true, you are ready. Module 16 will not be easy, but it will be doable, and the work you produce will be worth showing.
