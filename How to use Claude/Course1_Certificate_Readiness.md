# Course1_Certificate_Readiness

*Course 1 — AI Essentials. This document defines what it means to be certificate-ready in Course 1, what evidence the learner must produce, and what the certificate does and does not claim. It is a standards document, not a marketing page.*

---

## What certificate readiness means

Certificate readiness in Course 1 means the learner has worked through all sixteen modules of AI Essentials, passed every checkpoint, produced every required artifact, completed the capstone, and demonstrated — in their own words and their own work — that they can use AI tools competently, responsibly, and with verification discipline in everyday tasks.

It is a statement about practice, not promise. The learner has done the work. They have written prompts, compared outputs, caught hallucinations, classified sensitive information, designed workflows, drafted decision memos, and packaged what works into a reusable toolkit. A reviewer looking at the portfolio can see that the learner can think clearly about what AI is, what it is not, when to trust it, and when to slow down.

Certificate readiness also means the learner has built a personal posture toward AI — one that includes a mental model, a verification habit, a privacy floor, a disclosure practice, and a way of explaining their AI use to other people. The certificate marks the moment that posture becomes visible in finished work.

It is the entry point to applied AI fluency. It is not the end of the learning curve.

## What it does not mean

The certificate is issued by Jifunze on the basis of demonstrated coursework. It is not accreditation from a regulator, university, or government body. No external authority has reviewed or signed off on it.

It is not a job guarantee. Completing Course 1 does not entitle the learner to employment, an interview, a contract, a freelance client, a promotion, or any specific income outcome. Hiring decisions belong to employers and clients, who weigh many factors the certificate does not address.

It is not proof of domain expertise. The course teaches AI fluency that applies across writing, learning, research, decisions, workflows, and team use. It does not certify that the learner is a subject-matter expert in education, healthcare, law, finance, marketing, software, or any other field. A learner using AI to support work in a specialized domain still needs the domain knowledge that domain demands.

It is not an AI engineering or developer certification. Course 1 builds practical, end-user AI fluency. It does not certify the learner to build AI systems, train models, fine-tune models, deploy production AI, perform machine learning research, or operate AI infrastructure. Those are different disciplines with their own credentials and learning paths.

It is not a legal, financial, medical, or regulatory credential. Nothing in the certificate substitutes for licensed professional advice, sector-specific compliance training, or jurisdiction-specific data protection certification.

## Required completion criteria

To be certificate-ready, the learner must satisfy every item below. Partial completion does not qualify.

- All sixteen modules completed in full, including readings, worked examples, and practice activities.
- All sixteen module checkpoints passed at 80% or higher. Each checkpoint is six scored questions plus one applied scenario.
- All required portfolio artifacts completed for every module (see *Evidence required* below) and assembled into a single portfolio.
- The Module 16 capstone completed end to end: problem framing, workflow design, prompt set, review and verification, safety boundaries, presentation, self-critique, and reflection.
- A disclosure statement included with the portfolio that names how AI was used in producing the artifacts and the capstone.
- Revision completed where needed. Any checkpoint below 80% on first attempt, or any capstone criterion judged weak in self-critique, must be revised before the certificate is issued.

The standard is met when all six conditions are true at the same time. There is no waiver path.

## Skills demonstrated

A learner who reaches certificate readiness has demonstrated, through artifacts and checkpoints, the practical skills below. Each skill is anchored to specific module work, so the certificate is not a vague claim — a reviewer can trace the skill back to evidence.

- **AI mental model.** The learner can explain in plain language what AI is, what it is not, where its outputs come from, and the categories of task where it is and is not reliable.
- **Prompt control.** The learner can write prompts that specify task, context, constraints, format, and audience, and can show before/after improvements on real prompts.
- **Prompt engineering.** The learner can engineer prompts deliberately using a structured framework, design output formats, choose appropriate constraints, and recognize when added detail starts to hurt rather than help.
- **Iteration and comparison.** The learner can run a prompt, evaluate the output, revise the prompt, compare versions, and document why a final version was chosen.
- **Verification.** The learner can identify claims that need checking, separate known facts from assumptions, label confidence appropriately, and catch hallucinated citations, reasoning, or procedural steps.
- **Writing support.** The learner can use AI to adapt the same message for multiple audiences while preserving their own voice, and can apply a review model to check tone, accuracy, and fit.
- **Learning support.** The learner can use AI as a study aid without bypassing understanding, including teach-back practice, retrieval testing, and weak-area repair.
- **Responsible use.** The learner can map a use case to a stake level, apply appropriate guardrails, and write an accountability statement that names what the learner — not the tool — is responsible for.
- **Privacy boundaries.** The learner can classify information by sensitivity, rewrite prompts to expose only the minimum necessary detail, and identify scenarios where AI use should be paused or escalated.
- **Research synthesis.** The learner can scope a research question, summarize sources separately, compare and cluster themes, mark uncertainties, and produce an evidence-based brief without overclaiming.
- **Workflow design.** The learner can break a task into stages, identify dependencies, place review checkpoints, and document a reviewable standard operating procedure.
- **Decision support.** The learner can structure a decision with criteria, options, tradeoffs, and risks, use AI to surface alternatives and counter-arguments, and keep the final decision human-owned.
- **Team AI use.** The learner can identify coordination risks in shared AI work, draft a basic team AI use agreement, and design shared prompt assets — with a solo-learner alternative when no team is available.
- **Reusable prompt packs.** The learner can convert successful prompts into a reusable pack with purpose, inputs, examples, boundaries, review criteria, and version notes, and can test the pack against a fresh scenario.
- **Capstone workflow.** The learner can integrate the skills above into a single end-to-end AI-supported workflow, present it, critique it, and revise it.

## Evidence required

The certificate is granted on the basis of artifacts, not assertions. The portfolio must include the following items, one per module, plus the capstone bundle. Each artifact carries a one-paragraph context note written by the learner.

All artifacts use the canonical filename pattern `ModuleXX_ArtifactName_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`. Short forms (for example `MXX_Name`) are not accepted by the upload validator.

- **Module 1 artifact** — `Module01_AI_Use_Boundary_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: plain-language AI definition, capability and limitation table, personal use boundary.
- **Module 2 artifact** — `Module02_Responsible_Judgment_Checklist_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: myth correction worksheet, bias review checklist, judgment reflection.
- **Module 3 artifact** — `Module03_Prompt_Rewrite_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: before/after prompt rewrite, reusable prompt contract.
- **Module 4 artifact** — `Module04_Structured_Prompt_Template_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: structured prompt template, three reusable prompt patterns.
- **Module 5 artifact** — `Module05_Prompt_Version_Log_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: prompt version log, comparison matrix, final prompt with rationale.
- **Module 6 artifact** — `Module06_Claim_Verification_Table_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: claim-verification table, source-quality notes, revised answer with confidence labels.
- **Module 7 artifact** — `Module07_Audience_Fit_Communication_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: three audience-specific rewrites, tone rationale, one job-search artifact.
- **Module 8 artifact** — `Module08_AI_Learning_Repair_Plan_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: teach-back recording or transcript, practice question set, weak-area repair plan.
- **Module 9 artifact** — `Module09_Responsible_AI_Guardrails_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: use-case risk review, guardrail checklist, accountability statement.
- **Module 10 artifact** — `Module10_Privacy_Safety_Checklist_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: data classification table, safer prompt rewrite, operational safety checklist.
- **Module 11 artifact** — `Module11_Research_Synthesis_Brief_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: research scope brief, source comparison grid, evidence-based synthesis brief.
- **Module 12 artifact** — `Module12_Workflow_Agent_Readiness_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: workflow map, AI-assist decision table, reviewable SOP, agent-readiness assessment.
- **Module 13 artifact** — `Module13_Decision_Memo_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: decision criteria table, options and tradeoffs matrix, human-owned decision memo.
- **Module 14 artifact** — `Module14_Team_AI_Use_Agreement_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: team AI use agreement, responsibility map, shared prompt artifact — solo alternative permitted.
- **Module 15 artifact** — `Module15_Prompt_Pack_Playbook_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: prompt pack, usage and boundary notes, prompt-pack test log.
- **Module 16 capstone bundle** — `Module16_AI_Workflow_Capstone_[YourName].pdf` or `.docx`: end-to-end workflow portfolio, extended prompt pack, presentation, self-critique notes, one-page reflection.

Artifacts that exist as files (documents, slide decks, recordings) live in the portfolio. Artifacts that exist as practice (teach-back recordings, test logs) are evidenced by the learner's recorded output and the reflection that accompanies it.

## Capstone readiness

The capstone is the final demonstration that the certificate rests on. To be certificate-ready, the capstone must show the following — not in theory, but in finished work.

A clear problem framing that names the user, the task, the constraints, the success criteria, and the risks. A workflow design that breaks the task into stages, identifies which stages AI assists and which remain human, and places review checkpoints where they are actually needed. A prompt set written for each stage of the workflow, with the engineered structure visible. Sample outputs the learner actually produced, not described. The verification work performed: which claims were checked, which sources were used, which uncertainties were flagged. The safety boundaries applied: what data was excluded, what was generalized, what was escalated. A presentation — document or deck — that another person could follow without the learner present. A self-critique that names the weakest part of the work and what would be improved on a second pass. A reflection on what the learner now knows about their own AI use that they did not know at the start of Course 1.

The capstone must score at or above the rubric's "strong evidence" threshold on every criterion: problem framing, prompt and workflow design, verification and review, safety and privacy, reusability and transfer, reflection and improvement. A capstone that is strong on most criteria but weak on one is not certificate-ready until that criterion is revised.

## How learners can explain this certificate

The statement below is offered as a starting point for a profile, portfolio, or résumé entry. Learners should adapt the language to their voice and context, and should not embellish beyond what the certificate actually represents.

> *I completed Course 1 — AI Essentials with Jifunze. The course required sixteen module checkpoints at 80% or higher, sixteen portfolio artifacts, and an end-to-end capstone workflow with self-critique and revision. I can use AI tools to support writing, learning, research, decision-making, and workflow design while applying verification, privacy, and accountability standards. My portfolio shows the actual work, including the prompts I wrote, the outputs I reviewed, and the boundaries I applied. The certificate represents demonstrated practice in foundational AI fluency. It is not a credential in any specialized domain.*

Learners are encouraged to link to the portfolio rather than rely on the statement alone. The artifacts are stronger evidence than the description.

## Employer or client interpretation

A reviewer looking at a Course 1 certificate, paired with the portfolio it points to, can reasonably infer the following.

The learner has spent meaningful time with AI tools and has moved past casual or incidental use. They can write prompts deliberately rather than guess at them. They know the difference between an AI output and a verified output, and they take responsibility for the gap between the two. They will not paste sensitive information into a chat window without thinking about it first. They can produce written work in different registers and check whether their own voice survived the rewrite. They can use AI to support a decision without outsourcing the decision. They can describe a workflow well enough that someone else could repeat it. They can package a few useful prompts into a small toolkit and explain when to use it and when not to.

A reviewer cannot infer domain expertise, technical AI skills, performance under workplace pressure, communication skills outside of what the artifacts show, or fit for any specific role. Those need to be assessed through the reviewer's own process — interviews, references, work samples, trial tasks. The certificate gives the reviewer a credible signal that the learner has a foundation. It does not replace the reviewer's judgment on everything else.

For clients evaluating a freelancer who holds the certificate, the same applies. The portfolio shows that the freelancer can run AI-assisted work with verification and privacy discipline. Whether they can deliver a particular project is still a question the client answers through scoping, samples, and a small first engagement.

## Revision and retake rules

Course 1 is built around the assumption that learners will not pass everything on the first try, and that the path to certificate readiness includes deliberate revision. The rules below describe what happens when something falls short.

If a learner scores below 80% on a module checkpoint, they revisit the module sections that map to the missed questions, redo the relevant practice activity, and retake the checkpoint. Checkpoints can be retaken until passed; the highest passing score is recorded. There is no penalty for taking the checkpoint more than once. The intent is mastery, not speed.

If a portfolio artifact is incomplete or does not meet the acceptance criteria stated in the module's completion-evidence section, the learner returns to the module, redoes the artifact, and replaces the earlier version in the portfolio. Earlier drafts can be kept as appendices to show the revision trail, but the portfolio carries the final version.

If a capstone criterion is judged weak during self-critique — using the rubric's annotated examples of strong and weak evidence — the learner revises that section of the capstone before submitting the portfolio for certificate review. A capstone that is weak on more than one criterion typically benefits from a 24-hour pause and a fresh-eyes second pass before revision begins. The capstone is the centerpiece of the portfolio; rushing it weakens the whole.

If a learner repeatedly fails the same checkpoint, that is information, not failure. The most common cause is a gap in an earlier module that the current module assumes. The revision path is to step back one or two modules, redo the relevant practice, and return. Course 1 is sequenced so that earlier modules support later ones; a stuck point is usually a missing link, not an inability.

There is no time limit on the path to certificate readiness. A learner can pause the course and resume. The certificate is granted when the standard is met, not on a schedule.
