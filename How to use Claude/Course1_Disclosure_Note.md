# Course1_Disclosure_Note

*Course 1 — AI Essentials. A standard for disclosing AI assistance in your Course 1 artifacts and your Jifunze portfolio. This is not legal advice. It is a practical norm: enough to be honest, short enough to actually use.*

---

## Purpose of disclosure

Disclosure is how you tell a reader — a reviewer, a manager, a client, a teacher, an admissions officer — that AI was part of how a piece of work was made, what part it played, and what you did with what it produced. The point is not to confess. The point is to give the reader the context they need to trust your work and to know who is responsible for it.

A good disclosure does three things at once. It names the tool so the reader knows what kind of assistance was involved. It names the task so the reader knows where AI ended and your judgment began. It names the human review so the reader knows the work was checked, not just produced.

Disclosure protects three people. It protects the reader, who deserves an accurate picture of how the work was made. It protects you, because the alternative — silent AI use that is later discovered — damages credibility much more than honest use ever does. And it protects the people downstream who will rely on the work: the customer, the patient, the student, the colleague.

Course 1 treats disclosure as a normal professional habit, not a special event. Every artifact you build in this course is built with AI assistance, so every artifact will benefit from a short, plain disclosure attached to it.

---

## When disclosure is required

There are five settings where disclosure is not optional. In each of these, silent AI use creates real risk — to you, to the reader, or to the people the work affects.

**Course submissions.** Every artifact you submit for Course 1 carries a disclosure note. This is a Jifunze standard, not a personal choice. The reviewer needs to see what tool you used, what you asked it to do, and what you reviewed before submitting. This habit is also the reason the discipline becomes natural by the end of the course: you will have written sixteen of them.

**Portfolio artifacts.** Anything you publish in your Jifunze portfolio carries a disclosure. The portfolio is a professional document that people will read to evaluate your skills. They are entitled to know which work was AI-assisted and which was not, and what role the assistance played. Without disclosure, your strongest artifacts can be read as either fully manual (overstating your unaided skill) or fully AI-generated (understating your judgment). Disclosure prevents both misreadings.

**Employer or client work.** If you produce something for an employer, a client, or anyone paying for your work, AI assistance must be disclosed unless the employer or client has already stated in writing that disclosure is not required. The default is disclose. This applies whether the work is a draft email, a research brief, a slide deck, a customer message, or a strategy document. If your employer has an AI policy, follow that policy. If they do not, disclose.

**School and assessment settings.** Any academic, certification, or assessment context — coursework, take-home exams, application essays, scholarship submissions, professional certification work — requires disclosure unless the rules explicitly permit unattributed AI use. Most institutions now require disclosure. A small number forbid AI assistance entirely. Read the rules. If the rules are unclear, ask. If you cannot ask, disclose.

**Regulated or sensitive contexts.** Any work in a regulated or sensitive domain — healthcare, legal, financial, HR, child welfare, safeguarding, government, anything involving personal data of others — requires disclosure, and in many cases requires more: review by a qualified person before the work is acted on. AI assistance in these contexts is not just an authorship question; it is a safety and accountability question. Module 10 covers the privacy side of this in detail. The disclosure side: name the tool, name what it was used for, and name who reviewed the output before it was used.

## When disclosure may be optional but wise

Outside the five required settings above, disclosure is sometimes a judgment call. In a personal note, a draft you will keep to yourself, a casual message between friends, a list of ideas you generated for your own thinking — there is no obligation to disclose, because there is no reader who is making a trust decision about your work.

Even so, a short disclosure is often wise in cases where the work *might* be reused, forwarded, or re-published later. A draft becomes a published article. A personal study note becomes a teaching resource. A private prompt pack becomes a team asset. If the work has any chance of leaving your hands, disclosure now is cheaper than disclosure later.

A second wise case: when AI assistance changed something meaningful about the work. If AI surfaced an idea you would not have reached on your own, restructured an argument significantly, or generated a substantial portion of the language, a brief acknowledgement is honest even when no rule requires it. This is also a useful self-check: if you find yourself wanting to hide the AI involvement, that is usually a sign disclosure is warranted.

A third wise case: when the work is going to someone whose trust matters to you long-term — a mentor, a senior colleague, a co-founder. Front-foot disclosure builds trust. Discovered non-disclosure damages it.

## What disclosure should include

A useful disclosure has five parts. Each part is one short line. The full disclosure is rarely more than a short paragraph.

**Tool used.** Name the AI tool and, where it matters, the version or model. "Claude," "ChatGPT," "Gemini," "Claude Sonnet 4." Naming the tool is informative — different tools have different known strengths and known failure modes, and your reader may want that context.

**Task performed.** Name what AI did, in plain language. "Drafted the first version of the email." "Summarized the three source articles." "Generated three options for the opening paragraph." "Reviewed my draft for tone consistency." The task description should let the reader picture the boundary: where AI ended, where you began.

**Human review performed.** Name the review you did. "I checked the facts against the sources." "I rewrote the opening to match my voice." "I removed two claims I could not verify." "A colleague reviewed before send." Review is the part that turns AI output into your work. If review was minimal, say so. If a second person reviewed, name that.

**What was changed.** Name what you altered between the AI output and the final artifact. "I rewrote the second paragraph entirely." "I changed the tone from formal to warm." "I added the section on local context." "I removed the suggested statistics because I could not verify the source." Change-tracking, even at this rough level, tells the reader where your judgment shows up.

**What you remain responsible for.** Name what you, the human, still own. "I am responsible for the accuracy of the figures, the appropriateness of the tone, and the recommendation made." This part matters most in employer, client, regulated, and sensitive contexts. AI is not a co-author you can blame. The human who used the tool is the responsible party, and disclosure should make that clear rather than soften it.

## Simple disclosure template

Use this when you do not need anything fancier. Adapt the wording, keep the five parts.

> **AI assistance disclosure.** This artifact was produced with help from [tool, e.g., Claude]. AI was used to [task, e.g., draft the first version and suggest a structure]. I reviewed the output by [review, e.g., checking facts against the sources, comparing against my notes, and reading aloud for tone]. I changed [change, e.g., the opening paragraph, the order of the recommendations, and the closing line]. I am responsible for [responsibility, e.g., the accuracy of the claims, the appropriateness of the recommendation, and the final wording].

For most Course 1 portfolio artifacts, this template, filled in with one line per blank, is enough. It usually fits in three to five sentences.

## Short disclosure examples

These are illustrative, not prescriptive. Adapt to your real work.

**Writing support.**
> AI assistance: Claude was used to draft a first version of this customer email and suggest two alternative tones. I rewrote the greeting to match how I usually open with this customer, removed one sentence that overpromised, and verified the delivery date against our system. I am responsible for the commitment made and the customer relationship.

**Research summary.**
> AI assistance: ChatGPT was used to summarize the three source articles listed in the references and to cluster the themes. I checked the summaries against the originals, corrected one misattributed quote, and added the section on local context which the AI did not produce. I am responsible for the accuracy of the summary and the conclusions drawn.

**Prompt pack.**
> AI assistance: Claude was used to draft the first version of two of the five prompts in this pack and to suggest review criteria. I tested every prompt against three real scenarios, rewrote the prompts that produced inconsistent output, and added the boundary notes from my own experience. I am responsible for the quality and safe use of the prompts in this pack.

**Workflow SOP.**
> AI assistance: Claude was used to draft the stage map and the first version of the AI-assist decision table. I revised the stages to match how the work actually runs in our office, added the escalation step the AI omitted, and removed one suggested automation that would have removed a needed human review point. I am responsible for the operational fit of this SOP.

**Job-search document.**
> AI assistance: Claude was used to adapt my existing cover letter to the role posting and to suggest stronger opening lines. I rewrote the second paragraph to match my voice, removed two claims I could not back up with examples, and added the specific project the role description asked about. I am responsible for the accuracy of every claim made about my experience.

**Capstone project.**
> AI assistance: Claude was used throughout this capstone — to draft prompts, draft a first version of the workflow, summarize source material, and review my self-critique. The prompts in the prompt pack were tested by me against three scenarios; I rewrote the two that did not generalize. The workflow was adjusted after I walked through it manually with a real example. The reflection is my own, written without AI assistance. I am responsible for the design, the quality, and the safe use of the workflow described.

## What not to say

A weak disclosure is worse than no disclosure, because it pretends to give the reader information while actually hiding the picture. Avoid wording that is vague, evasive, or overstated.

**Vague.** "AI was used in the production of this work." This tells the reader nothing they could not have guessed. It does not name the tool, the task, the review, or the responsibility. It is a placeholder, not a disclosure.

**Evasive.** "AI may have been consulted at certain points." This phrasing is built to be deniable. If AI was used, say so. If it was not, do not gesture toward it. The point of disclosure is to be clear, not to leave wiggle room.

**Minimizing.** "AI was used briefly for minor edits" — when the AI in fact wrote most of the first draft. Understating the role of the tool is dishonest in the same way that overstating it would be. If you wrote the prompt and reviewed the output, you can take credit for the judgment without hiding the assistance.

**Overstating.** "This work was generated by AI" — when in fact you wrote most of it yourself and used AI for one paragraph. Overstating the AI role can sound modest, but it understates your own work and your own responsibility. Be accurate in both directions.

**Off-loading responsibility.** "Any errors are the AI's." No. Responsibility for the artifact stays with the human who produced it. Disclosure names the tool; it does not transfer accountability.

**Marketing.** "Crafted with cutting-edge AI." Disclosure is a clarity statement, not a sales line. Drop the adjectives.

**Boilerplate paste.** Copy-pasting the same disclosure paragraph across every artifact regardless of what AI actually did. Each artifact had a different real involvement; the disclosure should match.

## Learner responsibility statement

The following is a short statement you can include in the front matter of your Jifunze portfolio. It states the standard you hold yourself to across the artifacts inside. Adapt the wording to your own voice; keep the substance.

> **Responsibility statement.** The artifacts in this portfolio were produced with AI assistance, used as a tool under my direction. For each artifact, I am the person who chose the task, wrote or shaped the prompts, reviewed the output, and decided what to keep, change, or discard. I am responsible for the accuracy of the claims made, the appropriateness of the recommendations given, the safe handling of any information involved, and the quality of the work as a whole. Where individual artifacts include a more specific disclosure note, that note describes the particular AI involvement for that piece. Where they do not, this statement applies.

That statement, plus a per-artifact disclosure where the involvement was substantial, is the standard Course 1 expects. It is enough to be honest with any reader. It is short enough to actually use.
