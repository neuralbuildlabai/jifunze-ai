# Course1_Portfolio_Guide

*A practical guide for assembling the artifacts you produce across Course 1 — AI Essentials into portfolio-ready proof of disciplined, responsible AI practice. This guide does not rewrite module content. It tells you what to save, how to organize it, how to describe it, and how to share it.*

---

## Purpose of the portfolio

The portfolio is the working record of what you actually did in Course 1. Not what you read, not what you watched, not what you intended — what you produced, reviewed, and stand behind.

Three reasons to keep it.

The first reason is for yourself. A portfolio gives you something to point at the next time you wonder whether you have made progress. Sixteen modules go by quickly; the work blurs. A folder of dated artifacts with short context notes is the difference between "I took an AI course" and "here is the prompt pack I built, here is the safety checklist I follow, here is the decision memo I wrote when I had to choose between two real options."

The second reason is to support a Jifunze pathway. Each pathway — Digital Work Starter, AI Productivity Professional, Remote Work and Freelancing, Small Business and Entrepreneurship, Junior Tech Builder — values different artifacts. The portfolio lets you select the right ones to surface for the pathway you are pursuing without having to recreate the work.

The third reason is to support an external audience: an employer, a client, an instructor, an admissions reviewer, or a teammate. People reasonably want to see how you use AI before they trust you to use it on their work. A portfolio gives them a concrete answer that a certificate alone cannot.

What the portfolio is not. It is not a credential by itself. It is not proof that you can do every AI task well. It is not a guarantee of a job, a contract, or income. It is evidence of a starting point: that you can use AI productively, verify what it produces, protect sensitive information, and take responsibility for the outputs you ship.

---

## What portfolio-ready proof means

Portfolio-ready proof is work a stranger could understand, evaluate, and trust without you sitting next to them explaining it. It has four properties.

**It is concrete.** A portfolio-ready artifact shows actual work, not a description of work. A prompt pack with three real prompts and the outputs they produced is concrete. A paragraph that says "I learned how to write prompts" is not.

**It is contextualized.** A reviewer needs to know what task the artifact addresses, which AI tool you used, what the prompt was, what review you performed, and what you would still flag. Without context, even strong work looks generic. The context note (template below) is what makes the artifact legible.

**It shows human review.** AI output by itself is not proof of skill. AI output plus your review — what you accepted, what you changed, what you flagged as uncertain — is. The review is often the most important part of the artifact, because it shows the judgment you bring to the tool.

**It is honest about limits.** A portfolio that hides what AI did, or that overstates what the artifact demonstrates, fails on first contact with a careful reader. Strong portfolios disclose AI use plainly and describe the artifact's scope without inflation.

The shortest test: if you handed this artifact to a thoughtful person who has not taken the course, could they tell what you did, what AI did, and why the result is trustworthy? If yes, it is portfolio-ready. If not, the artifact needs a better context note, more visible review, or a tighter scope.

A few notes on saving these well.

Save the prompts, not only the outputs. The prompt is the most reusable asset in the artifact. An output without its prompt is hard to evaluate and impossible to reproduce.

Save at least one revision. The first version of an artifact is rarely the strongest. Keeping a "before" alongside the "after" makes your judgment visible.

If an artifact spans more than one file (for example, a prompt pack with separate example files), keep them in a single sub-folder named after the module rather than scattering them. The module folder structure below assumes this.

If you cannot share an artifact publicly because it contains sensitive material from your work or studies, save a redacted version for the portfolio and keep the full version privately. Note the redaction in the context note.

If you maintain the portfolio in a non-file environment (Notion, Google Drive, GitHub), keep the same numbered structure. The numbers preserve the course's intended progression and help a reviewer find their bearings quickly.

## How to write context notes

A context note sits next to the artifact and tells a reader, in fewer than 200 words, what they are looking at and how to evaluate it. Without it, even strong artifacts feel like fragments.

Use this template. Keep each line short.

**Context note template**

- **Task.** What were you trying to do? Describe the real task in one or two sentences. If the task came from your own work, study, or life, say so. If it was a course-supplied scenario, say that too.
- **AI tool used.** Name the tool and version (for example, "Claude Sonnet 4.6, web interface, accessed April 2026"). If you used multiple tools, name each and what each was used for.
- **Prompt used.** Either include the full prompt here or point to where it is saved in the same folder. The prompt is part of the artifact, not a footnote.
- **Human review performed.** What did you check, compare, or verify before keeping the output? List the specific review steps, not "I reviewed it carefully."
- **What changed after review.** Which parts of the AI output did you accept, edit, or reject? If you changed the prompt and re-ran it, say so.
- **What the artifact demonstrates.** In one sentence, what skill or judgment is this artifact evidence of? Tie it to the module's purpose. Be specific and avoid overclaiming — "shows I can scope a research question and label uncertainty in the synthesis" is stronger than "shows I am good at AI research."

Two short examples.

**Example A — Module 6 verification artifact.**
> Task: verify three claims an AI tool made about Kenya's data protection landscape, before using the summary in a study brief.
> AI tool used: Claude Sonnet 4.6 web, accessed 2026-03-12.
> Prompt used: see `prompt.md` in this folder.
> Human review performed: cross-checked each claim against the Kenya Data Protection Act 2019 text and the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner site; flagged the AI's date for the act as uncertain and looked it up.
> What changed: corrected the year of one referenced amendment; replaced one paraphrase with a direct quotation from the Act; added a "needs verification" label to one claim I could not confirm in the time available.
> What the artifact demonstrates: that I do not treat AI summaries as authoritative — I check, label uncertainty, and revise.

**Example B — Module 12 workflow artifact.**
> Task: design a monthly reporting workflow for a small bookshop owner so AI handles drafting and a human handles review.
> AI tool used: Claude Sonnet 4.6 web, accessed 2026-04-02.
> Prompt used: see `prompt.md`.
> Human review performed: walked through each stage with the owner; flagged two stages where the AI had assumed a level of data access that does not exist; added a review checkpoint between draft and send.
> What changed: removed the "auto-send" step; added an explicit human approval before any customer-facing message; tightened the SOP from eight stages to five.
> What the artifact demonstrates: that I can design a workflow that respects real authority and review boundaries rather than the ones the AI defaulted to.

A context note is short on purpose. If yours is running long, the artifact itself probably needs a tighter scope.

## How to package the final portfolio

There is no single right format. Pick the option that matches your audience, your comfort level, and the privacy of the material involved.

**PDF.** A single PDF (or a small set of PDFs) is the most portable option. It travels well by email, is easy to attach to a job application, and renders the same on every device. The downside is that it is a snapshot — updates require regenerating the file. Best for: applications, freelance proposals, printed handouts.

**Notion.** Good if you want a living portfolio with linked sub-pages and easy editing. Notion handles tables, embedded files, and shared links well. Make sure you understand which pages are public and which are private before sharing the link. Best for: ongoing personal portfolios that you update as your work evolves.

**Google Drive folder.** Useful if your audience is comfortable opening folders and you want to share original files (.docx, .xlsx, .md, .pdf) rather than a fixed export. Set folder permissions deliberately — view-only by default, with link sharing limited to specific people unless you want it fully public. Best for: instructors, employers who want original files, or collaborators who will leave comments.

**GitHub.** Strong fit for the Junior Tech Builder pathway and for anyone who wants to demonstrate technical literacy. A public repository with a clear README, the same numbered folder structure, and Markdown context notes is itself evidence of how you handle structured work. Avoid uploading sensitive material; use redacted versions. Best for: technical roles, builder-pathway portfolios, version-controlled iteration.

**Personal website.** A simple static site (a one-page summary plus links to artifacts) is professional and discoverable. Tools like a basic Hugo, Astro, or static-site template work; so does a single Notion page published as a website. Keep it light — a portfolio site does not need to be a design showcase. Best for: freelancers, founders, learners actively job-searching.

**Printed or emailed packet.** Some audiences (a local employer, a community mentor, a small-business client) will read a printed packet more readily than they will click a link. A printed packet can be a curated subset — the introduction, a pathway-focus note, three or four key artifacts, and the disclosure statement — rather than the full sixteen. Best for: in-person interviews, regional contexts where digital portfolio review is not the norm.

You can use more than one format. A common pattern: keep the master copy in Google Drive or GitHub, generate a PDF subset for applications, and maintain a one-page personal site as a public front door.

Whichever format you choose, three things must travel with the portfolio: a short learner introduction, the disclosure statement, and a note on which Jifunze pathway you are pursuing so the reader knows which artifacts to focus on.

## How to use the portfolio for pathways

Course 1 is foundational, so every Jifunze pathway passes through it — but each pathway weights the artifacts differently. When you present the portfolio to a pathway-aligned audience, surface the artifacts that pathway most cares about. Do not delete the others; just signal which to read first.

**Digital Work Starter.** This pathway is about being credible and safe in everyday digital-work tasks. Foreground Module 7 (audience-fit communication, including the workplace writing artifact), Module 9 (guardrail checklist and accountability statement), Module 10 (privacy and operational safety checklist), and Module 12 (the reviewable SOP). The story your portfolio tells: I can use AI productively in workplace communication and operational tasks while protecting information and respecting review.

**AI Productivity Professional.** This pathway is about disciplined, reusable, increasingly automated AI practice. Foreground Modules 3–5 (the prompt engineering and iteration work), Module 11 (synthesis brief), Module 12 (workflow and agent-readiness assessment), Module 15 (prompt pack), and Module 16 (capstone). The story: I treat prompting as a craft, I can design and document workflows, and I have judgment about when automation is and is not appropriate.

**Remote Work and Freelancing.** This pathway is about delivering AI-assisted work credibly to clients you do not see in person. Foreground Module 7 (especially the job-search and outreach artifact, which doubles as client-acquisition writing), Module 9 (accountability statement — clients want to know who is responsible for outputs), Module 10 (safety checklist — handling client data well), Module 14 (collaboration agreement adapted for client work), Module 15 (the prompt pack as a deliverable toolkit), and Module 16 (capstone as a showcase). The story: I can deliver AI-assisted work to a client with disclosure, safety discipline, and a documented process.

**Small Business and Entrepreneurship.** This pathway is about running AI-assisted operations in a small business without exposing the business to risk. Foreground Module 7 (customer-facing communication), Module 9 (founder accountability), Module 10 (business data handling), Module 11 (market and competitor synthesis), Module 12 (operational workflow and agent-readiness), and Module 13 (decision memo). The story: I can use AI to extend the capacity of a small business while keeping decisions, disclosure, and data within sensible bounds.

**Junior Tech Builder.** Course 1 is not a coding course, but the responsible-use foundation it establishes is what a builder needs before integrating AI into a product. Foreground Modules 3–5 (prompt engineering as the technical foundation for AI integration), Module 6 (verification — essential for shipping AI features), Module 10 (safety — essential for handling user data), Module 12 (workflow design and agent-readiness as design patterns), and Module 15 (the prompt pack as a personal asset library). The story: I have the responsible-use literacy that every later technical course assumes.

A short cover note in `00_About_This_Portfolio/pathway_focus.md` is the simplest way to signal this. Write three or four sentences naming your pathway, listing the four to six artifacts you want the reader to start with, and explaining why those particular artifacts matter for that pathway.

## Disclosure note

Disclosure is the act of being plainly clear about how AI was used in producing the work in your portfolio. It is not a confession and it is not a disclaimer; it is a basic professional courtesy that lets a reader judge the work accurately.

A reasonable default for Course 1 portfolios.

> Some or all of the artifacts in this portfolio were produced with the assistance of AI tools as part of Course 1 — AI Essentials. Each artifact includes a context note that names the tool used, the prompt, the human review performed, and what changed after review. The interpretation, judgment, edits, and final responsibility for these artifacts are mine.

Adapt the wording so it matches your audience and the truth of how you worked. If a particular artifact was produced without AI assistance, say so in its context note. If a particular artifact was almost entirely AI-generated and you only reviewed it lightly, say that too — over-claiming AI involvement is no better than hiding it.

Three situations to handle deliberately.

**Academic settings.** Some institutions and courses prohibit, restrict, or require disclosure of AI use. The portfolio's disclosure statement is not a substitute for the disclosure your instructor or institution requires on a specific assignment. Follow the rules of the assessor first, then add the portfolio-level disclosure on top.

**Employer or client work.** Many employers and clients have AI use policies, and some industries (legal, medical, financial, government) have additional restrictions. Disclose AI use according to the policy in force. If you do not know the policy, ask before using AI on the work, not after.

**Sensitive or redacted content.** If you have redacted real names, customer details, or other sensitive material from a portfolio artifact, note the redaction in the context note. A reader needs to know whether they are looking at the original work or a sanitized version.

The Course 1 standard is honesty over impressiveness. A portfolio that discloses AI use clearly is more credible than one that conceals it. Reviewers can tell the difference.

## Quality checklist before sharing

Run this checklist once when you finish assembling the portfolio, and again any time you update it before sharing with a new audience. The whole pass should take under an hour.

**Structure.**

- [ ] The folder structure follows the recommended numbering (00 through 16).
- [ ] Every module folder contains the artifact, the prompt(s) used to produce it, and a context note.
- [ ] The `00_About_This_Portfolio` folder contains a learner introduction, a disclosure statement, and a pathway focus note.

**Content per artifact.**

- [ ] The artifact is concrete: it shows actual work, not a description of work.
- [ ] The prompt is included or linked from the same folder.
- [ ] The context note follows the template (task, tool, prompt, review, what changed, what it demonstrates) and is under ~200 words.
- [ ] Human review is visible — what you checked, what you changed, and what you would still flag.
- [ ] Any redactions are noted.

**Honesty and disclosure.**

- [ ] The disclosure statement is present and reflects how you actually worked.
- [ ] No artifact overclaims its scope. If something demonstrates a starting-level skill, the context note says so.
- [ ] No artifact hides AI involvement that was meaningful to the output.

**Privacy and safety.**

- [ ] No customer, client, employer, classmate, or patient data appears in any artifact unless they have given explicit permission and the context allows it.
- [ ] No credentials, internal URLs, API keys, or other sensitive operational details appear anywhere in the portfolio.
- [ ] If the portfolio is hosted publicly (GitHub, personal site), folder and file permissions match your intent.

**Pathway alignment.**

- [ ] The pathway focus note names your target Jifunze pathway and the four to six artifacts a reviewer should start with.
- [ ] The artifacts named in the focus note are the ones that pathway most directly cares about (refer to the pathway section above).

**Readability.**

- [ ] A thoughtful person who has not taken Course 1 can open the portfolio and understand what each artifact is within thirty seconds, using the context notes alone.
- [ ] Filenames are predictable, dated where appropriate, and free of spaces or special characters that break links.
- [ ] The learner introduction is one page or shorter and avoids overpromising what the portfolio represents.

**Final check.**

- [ ] You have read the portfolio end to end at least once after completing it. If something feels weak, fix it before sharing — once it is in front of a reviewer, the time for revision has passed.

When every box is checked, the portfolio is ready to share. It is not a finished product — you will keep adding to it as you move through later Jifunze courses and into real work — but it is honest, organized, and trustworthy proof of the foundation you built in Course 1.
