# Course1_Self_Learner_Guide

*A working guide for completing Course 1 — AI Essentials without a live facilitator. Read this once before you start the course, then return to it whenever you feel unsure how to proceed.*

---

## Purpose of this guide

This guide exists because Course 1 is designed to be completed by self-paced learners. You may be a worker fitting study around a job, a student adding AI literacy to your other coursework, a freelancer learning a skill you intend to use with clients, an entrepreneur building operating habits for a small business, or a member of an informal study group. None of those situations include a facilitator standing next to you.

The course content is written to teach you directly. This guide sits one level above the content: it explains how to move through the course, how to pace yourself, what to do when you get stuck, how to verify your own understanding, how to save portfolio evidence as you go, and how to behave with integrity around AI use while studying AI. If you follow this guide, you will not need a live facilitator to complete Course 1 to a credible standard.

This guide does not promise jobs, income, or formal accreditation. It promises that if you do the work described here, you will leave Course 1 with a portfolio of artifacts that demonstrate competent, responsible, and reviewable AI practice — which is exactly what Course 1 is designed to produce.

## What this course helps you build

Course 1 is foundational. By the end, you should be able to use AI tools deliberately and safely across a range of everyday tasks: writing for different audiences, learning new material, doing source-aware research, supporting decisions, mapping workflows, and packaging what works into reusable assets. You should also know what AI does badly, when not to use it, and how to keep human judgment in charge of consequential work.

Concretely, you build three things across the sixteen modules:

A working mental model of what AI is and is not, so that you stop expecting the wrong things from the tool and start using it in ways that match its real strengths and weaknesses.

A practical skill set in prompting, verification, communication fit, learning, research, decision support, workflow design, privacy, and reusable systems — each anchored to a concrete artifact you produce.

A portfolio of sixteen module artifacts plus a capstone, all in a single folder, that another person could open and read to evaluate your AI fluency. The capstone is the centrepiece, but the earlier artifacts are the foundation it stands on.

The course is not a coding course, and it is not a course in any single AI tool. It teaches the underlying habits and judgment, then lets you apply them to whichever assistant you use.

## How to move through the course

The course has sixteen modules. They are sequenced so that each module assumes the modules before it. Module 1 sets the mental model; Modules 2 through 6 build prompting and verification discipline; Modules 7 and 8 apply that discipline to writing and learning; Module 9 establishes responsibility; Module 10 sets safety and privacy boundaries before high-volume application work; Modules 11 through 14 apply AI to research, workflows, decisions, and team contexts; Module 15 packages everything you have built into a reusable toolkit; and Module 16 is the capstone where you combine the toolkit into a single end-to-end workflow.

Work through the modules in order. Do not skip ahead. If you find a module easy, you can move faster, but you should still do the practice activities and produce the artifact, because later modules will assume that artifact exists.

Each module has the same internal shape. Read the core lesson, study the worked examples, complete the practice tasks, answer the pause-and-check questions, take the checkpoint quiz, and produce the portfolio artifact. Then move on. This rhythm matters more than your speed.

You can pause between modules without losing momentum, but try not to let more than a few days pass between module completion and the next module's start. Long gaps cause you to forget the prompt patterns and verification habits you just built.

## Recommended pace

The course is designed for a total of **32 to 45 hours of focused work**, depending on your starting level. A complete beginner with no prior AI exposure will be at the upper end. A learner who already uses AI casually will be closer to the lower end. The capstone alone takes roughly six to eight hours.

The recommended pace is **three to five weeks** of part-time study. Three weeks is the fast lane and assumes ten to fifteen focused hours per week. Five weeks is the steadier pace at six to nine hours per week. Either pace works. What does not work is trying to do the whole course in one weekend or letting it stretch past two months — the first compresses learning into shallow exposure, the second loses continuity between modules.

A useful weekly distribution is roughly:

- **Reading the core lesson:** about 25 to 30 percent of module time. This is foundational; do not rush it. If a module estimates two hours, expect to spend thirty to forty minutes on the core lesson.
- **Studying the worked examples:** about 15 to 20 percent. Read each example slowly, with the prompts and outputs visible. The examples are where the abstract teaching becomes concrete.
- **Completing the practice tasks:** about 25 to 30 percent. This is the largest chunk after reading. Do every practice task; do not skip the ones that feel familiar.
- **Answering pause-and-check questions and the checkpoint quiz:** about 10 to 15 percent. The pause-and-check questions are short and embedded; the checkpoint quiz at the end is six scored questions plus one applied scenario. Pass threshold is at least 6 of 8 correct.
- **Producing and saving the portfolio artifact:** about 15 to 20 percent. Some modules' artifacts are fast (a checklist, a table). Others are slower (a prompt pack, a synthesis brief). Budget more time on the application modules from Module 7 onward.

If a module is taking more than 50 percent longer than its estimate, that is a signal to slow down rather than speed up: you may be missing a prerequisite, or the artifact may need a clearer scope. Re-read the module overview and ask yourself which specific task is taking the time.

## How to use AI while learning AI

You will be using AI tools throughout this course. That is the point. But you are also being assessed on whether you can use those tools responsibly, so the way you use them while studying matters.

The honest principle is this: use AI to deepen your understanding, not to skip it. Asking an AI to summarize a module before you read it makes the lesson harder to absorb. Asking it to explain a concept you have already read once, in different words, can help. Asking it to grade your own answers without you trying first turns the course into AI doing your homework. Asking it to compare your answer to a reference answer, after you have written your own, is fine and useful.

Use AI freely for: testing your prompts on real tasks (the entire course depends on this), generating practice scenarios when you want extra repetitions, explaining a concept in a different style than the module uses, comparing two of your own draft answers, and stress-testing an artifact by asking the AI to critique it.

Be careful with: asking the AI to write your portfolio artifact for you, asking it to answer a checkpoint question before you have attempted it, asking it for "the right answer" on judgment-driven questions where the course is teaching you to make the call, and pasting whole module sections in and asking for a summary instead of reading.

The boundary is judgment. If the activity exists to build your judgment, do it yourself first and use AI as a second opinion. If the activity exists to produce output, AI assistance is part of the skill being taught — but the artifact must still reflect your work and your thinking, not the AI's unedited output.

You will explicitly study these distinctions in Module 8 (learning with AI), Module 9 (responsibility), and Module 10 (privacy and safe operation). Use those modules to refine the habits you start practising today.

## How to complete a module

Each module follows the same workflow. Treat this as your standard checklist.

**Read the core lesson.** Read the whole core lesson before you do anything else. Resist the urge to jump to the practice activities. The core lesson is where the concepts and vocabulary live; if you skip it, the practice will feel like guessing.

**Study the worked examples.** Each module has fully written worked examples that show prompts, outputs, and analysis. Read them slowly. When an example shows a "weak prompt versus strengthened prompt" comparison, look at exactly what changed and why. The worked examples are how the lesson becomes a working skill rather than a vocabulary list.

**Complete the practice tasks.** Each module has practice tasks with supplied materials. Open the supplied materials, do the task, and write down your output. Do not just imagine the answer. Practice produces the muscle that the checkpoint will test.

**Answer the pause-and-check questions.** These are short questions embedded in the module that ask you to re-state a concept or apply it to a tiny scenario. Answer them out loud or in writing before you read on. They exist to keep you active rather than passive.

**Complete the checkpoint quiz.** Each module ends with six scored questions plus one applied scenario. The pass threshold is at least 6 of 8 correct. If you score below that, do not move on; instead, identify which questions you got wrong, return to the relevant section, and re-attempt the failed questions after a short break. Treat the quiz as a learning tool, not just a gate.

**Produce the portfolio artifact.** Every module has a defined artifact — a checklist, a rewrite, a brief, a workflow map, a prompt pack section, a decision memo. Produce yours using the module's specification. The artifact should be tied to a real task you do or a realistic scenario, not a generic example. Date it. Keep it short, complete, and reviewable by another person.

**Save the artifact for the capstone.** Save the artifact into your portfolio folder using the naming pattern below (see *How to save portfolio evidence*). The capstone in Module 16 will draw on these earlier artifacts directly — particularly the Module 15 prompt pack — so saving them well now saves you significant work later.

When you finish all seven steps, you have completed the module. Run the self-check at the end of this guide before moving to the next one.

## What to do when you get stuck

Getting stuck is part of learning. The question is what to do when it happens, since you do not have a facilitator to ask. Use these self-remediation steps in order.

**First, name what is stuck.** Write down a single sentence: "I do not understand X" or "My output for Y does not match the worked example because Z." Vague stuckness is hard to fix; named stuckness usually has an obvious next step.

**Re-read the relevant section.** Go back to the part of the core lesson that introduces the concept. Read it once more, slowly, with a pen. About half the time, the second reading resolves the confusion because you now know what to look for.

**Re-study the closest worked example.** Worked examples are the bridge between concept and skill. If a practice task is not making sense, the corresponding worked example almost always shows you the missing move.

**Try the task with a smaller scope.** If the practice task is too large, do a smaller version of it first. If you are asked to rewrite a 200-word prompt, rewrite a 50-word version. Build confidence on the smaller scope and then scale up.

**Use AI as a second opinion, not a first answer.** Ask the AI to explain the concept in a different way, or to critique your draft answer, or to give you a parallel example you can compare. Do not ask it for the answer to a checkpoint question; that defeats the point of the checkpoint.

**Take a deliberate break.** Sometimes stuckness is fatigue. A 24-hour gap, with the question loosely in the back of your mind, often produces a clean answer the next morning. The capstone module has a built-in 24-hour rule for self-critique; the same logic applies to ordinary stuckness.

**Return with a fresh attempt.** When you come back, attempt the task again from scratch rather than reading your previous attempt. A blank-page restart often shortcuts the original confusion.

If you remain stuck after these steps, mark the question, complete what you can, and continue. Some confusions resolve later when a downstream module re-frames the concept. Note the gap honestly in your portfolio rather than pretending it is not there.

## How to know you understand

Reading material and feeling clear about it is not the same as understanding it. Use these four self-checks before you decide a module is done.

**Teach-back.** Close the module. From memory, explain the main idea of the module out loud or in writing in five to seven sentences. If you cannot, you do not yet understand it; re-read the section that defeats you. This technique is taught directly in Module 8 and is one of the strongest tests of real comprehension.

**Retrieval.** Without looking at the material, write down the module's key vocabulary and definitions, the worked-example pattern, and the steps of any framework introduced. Then check your list against the module. The gap between what you remembered and what was actually there shows you exactly what needs another pass.

**Output comparison.** Compare your practice-task output to the worked example or model answer. Do not look for word-for-word match; look for structural match. Did you cover the same elements? Did you miss a constraint? Did you over-include something the worked example deliberately left out? Write a one-sentence diagnosis.

**Artifact review.** Open your saved portfolio artifact for the module. Imagine you are someone else opening this file with no context. Can they tell what task it solves? Can they see the prompt, the output, the review? Is the artifact dated and named clearly? An artifact that fails this test is an understanding gap, not a formatting gap; the missing context usually reflects a missing concept.

If all four checks pass, you understand the module well enough to move on. If one check fails, return to the relevant section before you start the next module.

## How to save portfolio evidence

A portfolio is only useful if it is organized. Decide on the folder structure now, before you start Module 1, and stick to it.

Create a single folder called `Course1_Portfolio`. Inside it, create one subfolder per module, named with a leading zero so that they sort correctly:

```
Course1_Portfolio/
  Module_01_AI_Mental_Model/
  Module_02_Myths_Bias_Judgment/
  Module_03_Prompts_As_Control/
  Module_04_Prompt_Engineering_Structure/
  Module_05_Prompt_Iteration/
  Module_06_Verification/
  Module_07_Writing_Communication/
  Module_08_Learning_Study/
  Module_09_Responsibility/
  Module_10_Privacy_Safety/
  Module_11_Research_Synthesis/
  Module_12_Workflows_Agents/
  Module_13_Decision_Support/
  Module_14_Teams/
  Module_15_Reusable_Systems/
  Module_16_Capstone/
```

Inside each module folder, save the artifact using a consistent filename pattern:

```
M{NN}_{ArtifactName}_{YYYY-MM-DD}_v{N}.{ext}
```

For example: `M03_Prompt_Rewrite_2026-05-04_v1.md`, `M07_Audience_Rewrites_2026-05-12_v2.docx`, `M15_Prompt_Pack_2026-06-08_v1.md`.

Keep the file extension that fits the artifact: `.md` for text-heavy artifacts, `.pdf` for finished documents you would share with another person, `.xlsx` for tables, `.docx` for prose documents. Do not store artifacts only in chat history with an AI tool; copy them out into a real file. Chat history is not portfolio evidence.

Add a short context note at the top of each artifact: one paragraph explaining what task this solved, what you used AI for, and what you reviewed. This note is what a reader uses to evaluate the artifact, and it is also what you will draw on when you assemble the final portfolio after Module 16.

Version your artifacts. If you revise an artifact during the capstone, save the revised file with `_v2` rather than overwriting `_v1`. The version history is part of the evidence.

## How to prepare for the capstone from the beginning

The capstone is Module 16, but you start preparing for it in Module 1. The capstone is an end-to-end AI-supported workflow that consumes earlier artifacts directly, especially:

The **Module 3 and 4 prompt rewrites and templates**, which become reusable patterns inside your capstone workflow.

The **Module 5 prompt iteration log**, which gives you a working method for refining capstone prompts.

The **Module 6 verification table**, which becomes the verification step inside the capstone workflow.

The **Module 9 accountability statement and Module 10 safety checklist**, which set the responsibility and privacy boundaries the capstone must respect.

The **Module 12 workflow map and SOP**, which are the structural skeleton of the capstone workflow.

The **Module 15 prompt pack**, which is the most direct input to the capstone. Module 15 is explicitly designed as the bridge into Module 16: the prompts you package there become the prompts your capstone workflow runs on.

Practically, this means three habits from Module 1 onwards:

Choose a real task. When a module asks you to apply a skill to a scenario, choose a scenario from your actual work, study, or life rather than a generic placeholder. The same task — for example, "preparing for a certification exam," "writing customer responses for a small business," or "scoping a research question for a project" — can be revisited across many modules. By Module 15, you will have a portfolio that already wraps around one realistic use case, and your capstone will largely be the integration step rather than a fresh start.

Save artifacts in reviewable form. An artifact saved as a clean, named file with a context paragraph is capstone-ready. An artifact saved as scattered notes is not.

Read the Module 16 specification early. You do not have to read the whole capstone module at the start of the course, but skim its rubric and submission requirements after you finish Module 1 so you know what is being built up. Re-skim it after Module 9 and again after Module 14. By Module 15, you should know the rubric well enough to anticipate it.

If you do these three things consistently, the capstone becomes a synthesis exercise on top of work you have already done — which is exactly what it is designed to be.

## Learner integrity and disclosure

Course 1 takes responsibility seriously. You are studying responsible AI use, and the course expects you to practise it in your own work, including your coursework and your portfolio.

The general standard is honesty about how AI was involved. When you produce a portfolio artifact with AI assistance — which is true for almost every artifact in this course, by design — say so clearly in the artifact's context note. A simple form is sufficient:

"AI assistance: I used [tool name and approximate version] to [generate first drafts / restructure my notes / propose alternative phrasings / produce comparison tables / etc.]. I reviewed every output for accuracy, edited where needed, and accept responsibility for the final content."

Disclose more, not less, when:

- You are submitting work to a school, employer, certification body, or assessor that has its own AI-use policy. Follow that policy first. If the policy is silent, default to disclosure.
- The artifact will be shown to a client or collaborator who has the right to know how it was produced.
- The artifact involves judgment, recommendations, or content where the audience would reasonably want to weigh how much of the work is yours versus the tool's.

You do not need to itemize every chat exchange. You do need to be accurate about the role AI played and the role you played. Module 9 expands this into a full guardrail and accountability practice, and Module 10 adds the privacy dimension. Until then, the working rule is: if a reader of your artifact would be surprised to learn AI was used, you have under-disclosed.

A separate point of integrity: do not pass off AI-written checkpoint answers as your own thinking. Checkpoints are the only place in the course where the work is supposed to come entirely from you. AI-graded help on checkpoints undermines the only signal the course has about whether you actually understood the module.

## Suggested weekly plan

Two plans below — a five-week plan for steadier study and a three-week plan for faster study. Both produce the same outcome. Choose based on your weekly time budget, not your ambition.

### Five-week plan (six to nine hours per week)

**Week 1 — Mental model and judgment (Modules 1–2).**
Read the core lesson and worked examples for Module 1; complete practice and produce the personal AI use boundary artifact. Then do Module 2 the same way. End the week by reviewing your two artifacts together and confirming you can teach the mental model back from memory.

**Week 2 — Prompting and verification (Modules 3–6).**
The densest week. Move through Modules 3, 4, 5, and 6 in order. Spend extra time on Module 5's iteration log and Module 6's verification table — they are skills you will use in every later module. Save all four artifacts.

**Week 3 — Communication, learning, and responsibility (Modules 7–9).**
Apply prompting and verification to writing (Module 7), learning (Module 8), and responsibility (Module 9). Module 7 includes a job-search-style artifact; Module 8 includes a teach-back; Module 9 includes a personal accountability statement. End the week with all three artifacts saved.

**Week 4 — Privacy, research, workflows, decisions (Modules 10–13).**
Module 10 first; do not skip it or rush it, because the safety habits set the floor for the rest. Then research synthesis (Module 11), workflows and agents (Module 12), and decision support (Module 13). Save four artifacts. Skim the Module 16 capstone specification at the end of this week.

**Week 5 — Teams, reusable systems, capstone (Modules 14–16).**
Team agreement (Module 14), prompt pack (Module 15), then the capstone (Module 16). The capstone is the largest single piece of work in the course; budget six to eight hours for it. Produce the end-to-end workflow, the extended prompt pack, the presentation document, the self-critique, and the reflection. Use Module 16's 24-hour self-critique rule before you call the capstone complete. Assemble the final portfolio.

### Three-week plan (ten to fifteen hours per week)

**Week 1 — Foundations and craft (Modules 1–6).**
Mental model, judgment, prompting, verification — all six modules in one week. This requires roughly two hours per module on average plus the artifact work. Pace yourself; the prompting modules in the middle (3–5) are where most of the time goes.

**Week 2 — Application (Modules 7–13).**
Communication, learning, responsibility, privacy, research, workflows, decisions. Seven modules. The application modules each include a specific artifact tied to a realistic task. If you are using a single recurring use case across the course (recommended), the artifacts will compose into a coherent set by the end of the week.

**Week 3 — Integration and capstone (Modules 14–16).**
Teams, reusable systems, capstone, portfolio assembly. Module 15 is the most important preparation for the capstone; do not shortcut it. The capstone takes the largest single block of time in the course; protect that block.

Either plan works as long as you do not let modules pile up unfinished. A clean rule: never start a new module until the previous module's artifact is saved.

## Self-check before moving to the next module

Run this short check at the end of every module. If any item is "no," return to the module before you continue.

- I read the entire core lesson, not just skimmed it.
- I read every worked example with the prompts and outputs visible.
- I completed every practice task and saved my outputs.
- I answered the pause-and-check questions before reading on.
- I scored at least 6 of 8 on the checkpoint quiz, or I returned and re-attempted the failed questions.
- I produced the portfolio artifact this module asks for, tied to a realistic task.
- The artifact is saved in the correct module folder, with the correct filename pattern, with a context note that includes an AI-disclosure line.
- I can teach back the module's main idea in five to seven sentences from memory.
- I know how this module's artifact connects forward — to the next module and to the capstone.
- I have noted any unresolved confusions in writing rather than skipping past them.

When all ten items are "yes," the module is complete. Move to the next module promptly while the material is still fresh.
