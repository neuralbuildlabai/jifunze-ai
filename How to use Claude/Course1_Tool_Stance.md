# Course1_Tool_Stance

*Course 1 — AI Essentials. Tool stance and beginner setup guide. This document does not teach module content. It tells learners which AI tools they may use for the course, how to set them up responsibly, and what to expect from free versus paid tiers without making the course depend on any single vendor.*

---

## Purpose of this guide

This guide answers the practical questions a learner has before they touch their first AI tool for this course: *Which one am I supposed to use? Do I have to pay? Is it safe to type things into it? What if my answer doesn't match someone else's?*

It is written so a learner can complete every module of Course 1 with a free account on any mainstream AI assistant, on a basic laptop or a mid-range phone, on a normal internet connection. It is also written so the course does not lock learners into one company's product. AI tools change quickly. The thinking the course teaches — clear instructions, verification, judgment, privacy discipline — does not change. The tool you use to practice that thinking is replaceable.

Read this guide once before you start Module 1. Refer back to it when you set up a new tool, when you hit a usage limit, when you are unsure whether to paste something into an AI, or when you are asked to disclose how you used AI in a piece of work.

---

## Tool-neutral course stance

Course 1 is tool-neutral. You may complete the entire course using any of the following:

- **ChatGPT** (OpenAI)
- **Claude** (Anthropic)
- **Gemini** (Google)
- **Copilot** (Microsoft)
- **Another mainstream AI assistant** approved by your facilitator, employer, or school — for example Meta AI, Mistral's Le Chat, DeepSeek, or a local-language assistant offered by your country or institution.

The course examples are written in a way that should work on any of these tools. When the course shows a prompt and an output, treat the output as one realistic example, not the one correct answer. Your tool may produce a different but equally valid response. That is normal and is addressed below.

If you are studying as part of an organization, school, or workplace that has approved only certain tools, use the approved tool. Do not bring an unapproved tool into work or school just because the course mentions it.

You do not need more than one tool to complete the course. Using one tool well is more useful than skimming across several. If you want to compare two tools on the same prompt, that is a useful exercise — there is a practice activity in Module 5 that uses comparison directly — but it is not required.

## Recommended learner setup

This is a one-time setup. Do it before Module 1 so you don't lose time during a lesson.

**Account creation.** Go to the official website of the tool you have chosen. Type the address yourself; do not click on an ad result. Sign up with a real email address you control. If the tool offers sign-up through a Google, Microsoft, or Apple account, that is acceptable and often easier to manage, but use an account that is yours, not a shared family or workplace account.

**Email confirmation.** Most tools send a confirmation email. Open it and click the verification link before you try to log in. If the email does not arrive within a few minutes, check your spam folder and confirm you typed the address correctly. An unverified account will sometimes appear to work and then fail later in the course when you try to use a feature.

**Password and MFA basics.** Use a password you do not use anywhere else. A password manager is the easiest way to do this; if you do not use one, write the password down somewhere physical and private rather than reusing an old password. Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) if the tool offers it. MFA usually means a code from an authenticator app or an SMS to your phone. This matters because anyone who reaches your AI account can read your past conversations, which may include things you typed without thinking.

**Privacy settings.** Most major AI tools have a settings page that controls (a) whether your conversations are used to train the model and (b) how long conversations are stored. Open that page once. Read the options. Choose what you are comfortable with. If you are unsure, the safer default is: do not allow training on your conversations, and do not store conversations longer than you need them. You can change these later. The course does not require any specific setting.

**Conversation history and memory.** Some tools save every conversation by default. Some tools also have a "memory" feature that lets the assistant remember facts about you across conversations (your name, your job, your preferences). Decide whether you want this on. Memory is convenient but it means the tool builds a profile of you over time. If you turn memory on, periodically review what it has remembered and delete entries you do not want kept. If you are using a shared computer, turn memory off.

**Avoiding sensitive data.** Before your first prompt, decide what you will *not* type into the tool. The list is in the section **What not to paste into AI tools** below. Read it before you start Module 1, not after.

**Mobile versus desktop.** Either works for this course. A desktop or laptop is more comfortable for the writing-heavy modules (Modules 3, 4, 7, 11, 16). A phone is fine for shorter practice activities. If you are working primarily on a phone, install the tool's official app from the official app store rather than using the browser version; the app handles long sessions better.

## Free versus paid tools

Most major AI tools have a free tier and one or more paid tiers. The course is designed so that **the free tier of any mainstream tool is enough**. This section explains the differences in general terms so you can make an informed choice if you are considering paying. It does not recommend that you pay.

**Usage limits.** Free tiers usually cap how many messages you can send in a given window (per hour, per day, or per month). When you hit the cap, the tool will pause you, sometimes for a few hours, sometimes until the next day. Paid tiers raise or remove this cap. For this course, the practical impact is that on a heavy practice day you may run out of free messages mid-activity. The fix is to pause and continue later, not to pay.

**Model quality.** Free tiers often give you access to a smaller or older model; paid tiers give you access to the company's most capable model. The difference is real but is smaller than marketing material suggests. For Course 1's purposes — learning to instruct, verify, and judge — the free model is sufficient. You will not fail a practice activity because you used the free model.

**File upload limits.** Free tiers often limit how many files you can upload, how large each file can be, and what file types are accepted. Paid tiers allow more and larger uploads. Course 1 does not require you to upload large files. If a practice activity asks you to work with a document, you can paste the relevant portion as text instead of uploading.

**Context length.** "Context length" is how much text the tool can hold in its working memory at once — the prompt plus the conversation so far plus any attached files. Free tiers have shorter context lengths. In practice this means very long conversations may lose track of what was said earlier. The fix during the course is to start a fresh conversation for a new task instead of running everything in one long thread.

**Tool integrations.** Paid tiers often add integrations: connecting to your email, your calendar, your files, your code editor, or external services. Course 1 does not require any integration. Modules 12 and 15 discuss workflows and reusable systems; you can complete both without paid integrations.

**Privacy and admin controls.** Paid business or enterprise tiers usually offer stronger privacy guarantees (your data is not used for training by default, admins can manage retention, audit logs exist). If you are using AI inside a workplace, your employer's tier matters more than your personal tier. If you are a personal learner, the free tier's standard privacy settings, configured as described above, are enough for the course.

**Bottom line.** The course is built to be completed on a free tier. Pay only if you have an independent reason — usage volume in your job, a specific feature you need, or organizational policy. Do not pay because the course implied you should. It does not.

## What to do if your tool gives a different answer

You will sometimes follow a prompt from this course and get an output that does not look like the example output in the lesson. This is expected. There are three reasons.

**Different model.** The course was written using one or two specific models at one point in time. You may be using a different model from a different company, or a newer or older version of the same model. Different models phrase things differently, choose different examples, and weight instructions slightly differently.

**Non-deterministic generation.** Even the same model, given the same prompt, will not always produce the same output. There is some randomness in how AI text is generated. Run the same prompt twice and you may see two different responses, both reasonable.

**Tool-specific behavior.** Some tools format responses with bullet points by default. Some default to longer answers. Some refuse certain requests that other tools answer. Some have safety filters tuned differently. None of this means your tool is broken.

**What this means for the course.** Treat the example outputs in lessons as *one plausible answer*, not *the* answer. The skill the course teaches is not memorizing AI outputs. It is judging them. When your output differs from the lesson's:

1. Compare the two outputs against the *purpose* of the prompt, not against each other. Does your output do the job?
2. If your output is worse, that is feedback about your prompt or about your tool's defaults — both fixable. Adjust the prompt. Add a constraint. Try again.
3. If your output is different but still good, accept it. Submit yours.
4. If you cannot tell whether your output is good, that is exactly the situation Module 6 (verification) trains you for. Verify the claims in the output against a trusted source before relying on the output.

This is also why the course does not grade you on producing a specific AI output. It grades you on the prompt you wrote, the verification you did, and the judgment you applied.

## What not to paste into AI tools

Treat your AI tool the way you would treat a stranger you met at a bus stop who happens to be useful at writing emails. They can help you. You still do not hand them your passport, your bank statements, or your employer's confidential files.

Do not paste any of the following into any AI tool, free or paid, for this course or otherwise:

- **Passwords, API keys, recovery codes, or PINs.** Not even to ask the AI to "format them nicely." Once typed, you do not control where they go.
- **Government or institutional ID numbers** — national ID, passport, KRA PIN, Social Security or equivalent, driver's license, student ID number tied to your record.
- **Banking and financial details** — account numbers, M-Pesa transaction details with phone numbers attached, full card numbers, mobile money PINs.
- **Other people's personal data** — clients', customers', students', or colleagues' names combined with any sensitive attribute (medical condition, salary, performance issue, immigration status, contact details). If you would not forward an email about a person to an unrelated third party, do not paste it into an AI either.
- **Health information** — your medical records, a relative's diagnosis, mental health notes, anything you would expect a clinic to keep confidential.
- **HR material** — disciplinary letters, internal grievances, salary spreadsheets, candidate evaluations, anything written under the assumption that only HR will read it.
- **Legal material under privilege** — communications with a lawyer, draft contracts marked confidential, settlement terms, evidence in a live matter.
- **Confidential employer documents** — internal strategy, unreleased product plans, customer lists, source code your employer treats as proprietary, board materials, anything labeled internal-only or covered by an NDA.
- **School exam answers, take-home test responses, or assignments where the rules forbid AI assistance.** If your school's rule is "no AI," respect that rule. The course's stance on academic integrity is simple: follow your institution's rules. The course will not help you violate them.

If you need to work on something sensitive — for example, you are studying Module 7 (writing) and you want to draft a real performance review you actually have to write at work — replace the sensitive details with generic placeholders before you paste. Use "Employee A" instead of the person's name. Use "the project" instead of the project name. Use "the client" instead of the client. Then put the real names back in your final document, locally, after you copy the AI's output out of the tool.

If you are unsure whether something counts as sensitive, the rule is: when in doubt, leave it out. Module 10 covers this in depth.

## How to save prompts and outputs for the course

Most modules ask you to produce a portfolio artifact — a worked example, a checklist, a rewrite, a reflection. You also need to keep a record of the prompts you used and the outputs you got, both for your own future reference and because Module 5 (iteration) and Module 15 (prompt packs) build directly on what you saved earlier.

**Folder structure.** Create one folder for the course on your device or in your cloud storage. Inside it, create a subfolder per module. A simple structure:

```
Jifunze_Course1/
    Module01/
    Module02/
    Module03/
    ...
    Module16_Capstone/
```

**Filename format.** Use a format that sorts cleanly and tells you what is inside without opening the file. The course uses this pattern:

```
Module03_Prompt_Rewrite_YourName.docx
Module06_Verification_Table_YourName.pdf
Module15_Prompt_Pack_YourName.docx
```

If you produce more than one version of the same artifact (which you will, especially in Modules 3–5), add a date or version suffix:

```
Module03_Prompt_Rewrite_YourName_v1.docx
Module03_Prompt_Rewrite_YourName_v2.docx
```

**What to save inside each artifact.** For prompt-related work, save the prompt itself, the AI output, your analysis, and your improved version, in that order. Do not save only the final output — the course's portfolio review depends on seeing your prompt work, not just the AI's writing. The Capstone (Module 16) will ask you to assemble selected artifacts from across the course; if you skip the saving step, you will recreate work later under time pressure.

**Where to keep it.** Local device, a personal cloud drive, or a USB stick — whatever is reliable and private. Do not save course artifacts that contain personal reflections inside a shared work drive your employer can read.

## How to disclose tool use

Many institutions, employers, and publications now ask you to disclose when AI helped produce a piece of work. Even when not asked, a short disclosure is a habit worth building. It protects you against the accusation that you hid AI involvement, and it tells the reader how much weight to give the work.

A disclosure does not need to be long. A single sentence or short paragraph is enough. Use a template like this:

> *"I used [tool name] to [what you used it for — for example: brainstorm an outline, draft an initial version, suggest alternative phrasings, check the structure of an argument]. I [reviewed / edited / verified / rewrote] the output before submitting. The final [decisions / claims / recommendations] are mine."*

A worked example:

> *"I used ChatGPT (free tier) to draft an initial version of this customer email and to suggest a more polite opening line. I rewrote the body myself, verified the delivery dates against our internal tracker, and replaced the AI's generic apology with our actual policy. The final wording is mine."*

A different worked example:

> *"I used Claude to brainstorm possible structures for this report and to check whether my conclusion logically followed from the evidence section. The writing is mine. The data was collected by me from [source]."*

The important elements are: which tool, what you used it for, what you did to the output afterwards, and who is accountable for the final result. If your school, employer, or publisher has a specific disclosure format, use theirs instead. If they do not, the template above is enough for most situations Course 1 prepares you for.

Modules 9 and 14 return to disclosure in the context of accountability and team use.

## Kenya and global learner note

The course is written from a Kenyan base with global learners in mind. AI access is not the same everywhere. A few practical considerations.

**Bandwidth.** AI tools work over text, which is light, but the web interfaces sometimes load heavy front-end code. If your connection is slow, the official mobile apps usually run leaner than the browser versions. If you are on metered mobile data, write longer prompts in a notes app first, then paste them into the tool — this avoids losing work to a connection drop mid-typing.

**Device.** A mid-range Android phone or a basic laptop is enough. The course does not require a powerful machine. Voice input, available on most tools' mobile apps, is a reasonable alternative if typing on a phone is slow for you.

**Payment.** Some paid AI tiers do not accept M-Pesa, Kenyan-issued cards, or cards from certain countries directly. Some require a billing address in a specific region. The course does not require a paid tier, so this is not a blocker. If you decide independently that you want a paid tier and your card is not accepted, options that often work include: a virtual card from a Kenyan or pan-African fintech, a regional payment provider, or, where available, paying through your employer or school. Do not give your card details to a third party who promises to "subscribe on your behalf."

**Account region and language.** Some tools restrict accounts by country. If a tool is not available in your country, choose a different tool from the list above rather than using a workaround that violates the tool's terms. Most tools accept English prompts and produce English output well; many also handle Swahili, French, Amharic, Arabic, Hausa, and other widely-used languages with varying quality. If your tool answers a non-English prompt poorly, the issue is the tool, not your language. Try a different tool or write the prompt in English and translate the output yourself.

**Power and reliability.** If power is unreliable where you study, save your work after each prompt-output cycle, not at the end of a long session. Most tools save conversations automatically when you are logged in, but a draft you typed and did not yet send is lost when the tab closes.

**Cost discipline.** Course 1 is built to be free. If at any point the course material implies you must spend money to continue, that is a mistake in the material. Report it through Jifunze's feedback channel and continue with the free path.

## Updating this guide

AI tools change. Companies launch new tiers, retire old models, change privacy defaults, change pricing, change which countries they serve, and add or remove features. This guide will be out of date in some specific detail within months of being published. The structure is meant to outlast the details.

This document will be reviewed at least once per course revision cycle. When you encounter a detail in this guide that no longer matches reality — a tool that has shut down, a setting that has moved, a feature that is now free or now paid — that is feedback worth sending. The principles in this guide (tool neutrality, free-tier sufficiency, privacy discipline, verification, disclosure) are not expected to change.

If you are revisiting this course a year or more after first reading this guide, treat the tool-specific details as guidance to verify, and the stance and habits as the part to keep.
