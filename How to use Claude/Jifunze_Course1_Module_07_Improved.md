## Module purpose

Most adult AI use, in offices, schools, businesses, and personal life, is writing-related. Drafting emails. Replying to customers. Adapting an announcement for parents and for staff. Cleaning up a message that came out too harsh. Writing a cover letter. Sending a follow-up after a missed deadline. These are the moments where AI is genuinely helpful — and the moments where bad AI use shows up most often as polished, generic, slightly-off messages that the recipient quietly distrusts.

This module gives you the discipline to use AI for writing without losing the things that make a message yours: accuracy, voice, audience fit, format suitability, and your own responsibility for what is sent. It also turns one of your strongest learning artifacts so far — your prompt skill from Modules 3–5 and your verification habit from Module 6 — into a daily writing capability.

## Core lesson

### AI can improve writing, but it does not own the message

When you ask AI to write or rewrite something, you are still the author. The tool produces draft language. You decide what to send. This is not a small distinction. The reader of an email does not see the AI; they see you. The customer who receives a reply about a delayed M-Pesa refund does not care which model wrote the apology; they care whether the commitment is real and whether it sounds like the company they pay. The hiring manager reading your cover letter is reading *you*, not the assistant you used. AI helps you write more, faster, and often more clearly. It does not transfer ownership of the message.

This frame matters because the most common AI writing mistake is silent: the learner sends a polished AI draft without checking it, and the message is technically fine but slightly wrong — wrong tone, wrong claim, wrong voice, wrong format for the channel. None of these failures are dramatic. They erode trust slowly.

### Audience fit: the same message is not the same message for different readers

A piece of writing has a sender, a message, and a reader. AI can produce one strong version of the message. Audience fit is the move that turns it into the right version for *this* reader. The audiences you write for in normal work are typically a manager, a customer, a peer, a student, a parent or guardian, a hiring manager, a client, or a mixed internal audience. Each one needs different things from the same message.

A manager wants the decision and the next step at the top. A customer wants what is happening to *their* problem and what you will do about it. A peer wants the substance without procedural framing. A student wants clarity without condescension. A parent or guardian wants reassurance, the specifics, and what they need to do. A hiring manager wants evidence that you can do the job, in their language, in under a screen of text. A client wants confidence and accountability without overpromising. A mixed audience requires the simplest version that still says everything that needs to be said.

You will rarely write the same paragraph well for all of these readers. Audience fit is what AI is genuinely useful for — not because AI knows your reader, but because AI can produce three or four variants quickly and you can pick or repair the one that matches. The judgment is still yours.

### Tone versus truth

A frequent failure of AI rewriting is that it softens too much. You ask for a "more polite version" of a sharp email, and the AI writes a message that is gentler but no longer says what you needed to say. The deadline disappears. The consequence is removed. The escalation is hidden behind apologies. The recipient now feels good and remains misinformed.

The rule is: tone is how the message lands; truth is what the message contains. You can change tone freely. You cannot change truth without checking. If the original message says "the report is two weeks late and we will pause the project on Friday if it is not delivered," a softer rewrite still has to deliver both facts. A rewrite that becomes "we look forward to receiving the report at your earliest convenience" is not polite — it is misleading.

When you ask AI to soften, harden, or restructure a message, name the facts that must remain. If you do not, the model often smooths them out.

### Voice preservation

Your voice is the way you sound on paper — your sentence length, your word choice, your level of formality, the small habits that make a customer or a manager say "yes, that sounds like Mzalendo." AI defaults to a generic, mid-formal, slightly American voice. If you accept that default, your messages start to sound like everyone else's AI-assisted messages, and the reader notices, even if they cannot name it.

Voice preservation is not a creative-writing concern. It is a trust concern. A message that does not sound like you reads as if it was sent by a different person — or by no person — and the reader's confidence in your reply quietly drops.

You preserve voice by giving the AI a sample, by naming voice constraints in the prompt ("write in short sentences, no exclamation marks, no marketing words like 'thrilled' or 'excited'"), and by editing the AI's output back toward how you actually speak. Most learners need to do all three.

### Format fit

Format is the shape the message takes — email, WhatsApp message, LinkedIn note, internal memo, customer reply form, SMS, formal letter, slide bullet, support ticket. Each has expectations. An email opens with a greeting and ends with a sign-off. A WhatsApp message is shorter, no greeting needed, and can use line breaks instead of paragraphs. A LinkedIn outreach has roughly 300 characters before the reader has to click to expand. A formal letter to KRA, a school, or a bank has a specific layout the reader expects.

AI defaults to a long-email shape unless told otherwise. If you ask for "a message to a customer about the delayed refund" you will often get a four-paragraph email when WhatsApp would have been more appropriate. Format fit is part of the prompt. Name the channel, the length cap, and the formality level.

### Responsibility for the final communication

Whoever sends the message is responsible for the message. This is true of AI-assisted messages as much as any other. The tool does not share blame. The tool does not write the disclosure. The tool does not absorb the consequences if a date is wrong, a tone is harsh, or a claim cannot be backed up. The discipline from Module 6 — verify what matters before sending — applies here. The disclosure note (per the course-level Disclosure Note) is the final step on anything that goes outside personal use.

### A-T-V-F-R: a five-point review for any AI-assisted message

Before you send an AI-assisted message, run it through five quick checks. If you can answer each one cleanly, send. If you cannot, fix.

- **Audience.** Who is this for? What do they need? What do they already know? Does this message read for *that* reader, not for a generic reader?
- **Truth.** Is every fact, number, date, and commitment in this message correct? Have I verified anything I am not sure of? Has the AI smoothed away a hard fact I needed to keep?
- **Voice.** Does this sound like me, the sender? Would the recipient recognise it as mine? Are there words or phrases here that I would never use?
- **Format.** Is this the right shape for the channel? Right length? Right tone of formality? Right opening and closing?
- **Responsibility.** Am I willing to put my name on this exactly as written? Is there anything I would want to disclose to the reader about how this was produced?

A-T-V-F-R is the review you run on every piece of writing you produce in this module and in your real work afterwards. It is short enough to actually use.

### How Modules 3, 4, 5, and 6 support better writing prompts

Module 3 taught you that the prompt is your control surface. For writing, the prompt has to name the audience, the message, the tone, the format, and any constraints (length, what must be kept, what to avoid). Without those, AI fills in defaults you did not choose.

Module 4 gave you a structured prompt frame (T-C-C-F-A and similar). Writing prompts benefit from the same structure. *Task:* rewrite this email. *Context:* recipient is a parent of a Form 2 student in Nairobi who missed three days of school. *Constraints:* keep the consequence (a meeting on Friday), maximum 120 words, do not threaten, do not apologise for the policy itself. *Format:* short email with greeting and sign-off. *Audience:* concerned parent.

Module 5 trained iteration. For writing, that means producing two or three versions and comparing them on audience, truth, voice, and format. Picking the first AI draft is the most common reason AI-assisted messages sound off — there was no compare-and-choose step.

Module 6 trained verification. Anything in your message that is a claim, a number, a date, a name, a policy reference, or a commitment is a Module 6 question. AI is fluent and will not flag where it has guessed. You have to.

### When not to use AI for sensitive communication without review

Some messages are not safe to draft and send straight from AI, even with A-T-V-F-R review. They need a human review beyond yours, or they need to be drafted manually because the privacy and accountability stakes are too high. Examples:

- A disciplinary letter to an employee or a student that may go into a permanent record.
- A medical message that gives advice to a patient or a family member, even informally.
- A legal communication where wording has binding effect (notice of termination, demand letter, contract clause sent to a counterparty).
- A communication about another person's salary, performance issue, immigration status, or health.
- A grief, condolence, or crisis message where a generic-sounding response is harmful.
- A message that contains personal data of someone else (a customer's full account details, a student's record, a colleague's HR file). This is also a Module 10 issue — do not paste sensitive data into AI; summarise generically first.

In these cases, AI may still help with structure or with a first sketch, but the actual sending requires human judgment, often more than one human, and sometimes a manager, a lawyer, a clinician, or HR. Recognising these cases before you draft is part of the skill.

## Fully written worked examples

### Worked example 1: Harsh email rewritten to respectful, with the truth kept

**Harsh original (a manager wrote this in frustration about a late report):**
> Joyce, this report was due two weeks ago. I have asked for it twice. If I do not receive it by Friday, I am pausing the project. I do not know what else to say.

**Weak AI rewrite (after a thin prompt: "make this email more polite"):**
> Hi Joyce, I just wanted to gently follow up on the report when you have a moment. We are so excited to keep the project moving forward, and I would really appreciate hearing from you soon. No pressure at all — let me know if there's anything I can do to support! Thanks so much for everything you do.

**What is wrong with the AI rewrite.** The two-week delay is gone. The Friday deadline is gone. The pause-the-project consequence is gone. The message now reads as a gentle nudge with no urgency. The recipient has no idea this is the third request or that the project will pause if no report arrives. The tone is also off — "so excited," "really appreciate," "thanks so much for everything you do" — none of which sound like a manager handling a real delay. The AI softened the message past usefulness.

**Better prompt.**
> Rewrite this email so the tone is calm and respectful, but keep all of the following: the report is two weeks late, this is the third request, and the project will pause on Friday if the report is not received. Maximum 90 words. Plain professional tone. No exclamation marks. No "excited," "thrilled," or marketing words. Sign-off: short, neutral.

**Improved message:**
> Joyce, following up on the project report, which is now two weeks past its due date. This is my third request. To keep the project on track, I will need to pause the next phase on Friday if the report has not arrived. Please let me know if something is blocking you that I should know about, and we can work out the timeline from there. Thank you, [Name].

**What the learner should notice.** The improved message preserves all three facts (two weeks late, third request, pause on Friday) and also opens space for a real conversation ("if something is blocking you"). Tone has been changed; truth has not. This is the difference Module 7 is teaching.

### Worked example 2: Same notice rewritten for students, parents, and staff

**Original notice (an internal note from a school principal):**
> The school will close from Wednesday at noon due to the suspected water-supply contamination flagged by the public-health office. We are awaiting test results. Classes resume Monday at 8 a.m. unless updated. Bus services are suspended for the remainder of the week. Lunches will not be served on Wednesday. Parents collecting students should arrive by 12:30. Any boarders unable to travel home should report to the deputy principal's office by 11 a.m.

This works internally but lands differently for the three audiences who need to hear it. The same facts have to be reframed.

**Rewrite for parents (WhatsApp, short):**
> Dear parents and guardians, the school will close from Wednesday at noon because of a suspected water-supply issue flagged by the public-health office. Please collect your child by 12:30. Bus services are suspended for the rest of the week. Classes resume Monday at 8 a.m. unless we send another update. Boarders who cannot travel home should report to the deputy principal's office by 11 a.m. We will message again as soon as we have test results. Thank you. — [School name]

**Rewrite for students (assembly announcement, plain):**
> Quick announcement. School is closing today at noon because of a possible problem with the water supply. We are waiting for the test results. There will be no lunch today. If your parents are coming to pick you up, they have been told to arrive by 12:30. Boarders, if you cannot get home, go to the deputy principal's office by 11 a.m. and we will help you. Bus services are suspended for the rest of the week. We expect to reopen on Monday at 8 a.m., and we will message your parents if anything changes.

**Rewrite for staff (internal email, full):**
> Team, the school is closing from Wednesday noon due to a suspected water-supply contamination issue flagged by the public-health office. Please be ready to:
> 1. Wind up classes by 11:45 and supervise students moving to collection points.
> 2. Confirm boarder departures with the deputy principal's office (deadline 11 a.m.).
> 3. Send the parent notification through the official channel before 9:30 so collection by 12:30 is realistic.
> 4. Suspend lunch service and inform catering staff today.
> Bus services are suspended for the rest of the week. Classes are scheduled to resume Monday at 8 a.m. We will update once test results are received. Please raise any concerns with the deputy principal directly.

**What changed across the three.** The parent version foregrounds *what they need to do and when* and reassures them that more information is coming. The student version drops procedural language ("public-health office flagged") for clarity and gives a clear path for boarders. The staff version is procedural — it lists the work and the order. All three keep the same facts: closure time, reason, lunch suspension, collection time, bus suspension, boarder instruction, return date. None invent details the original did not contain.

**What the learner should notice.** A single source of truth (the original notice) becomes three messages by changing audience, format, and emphasis — but never by changing facts. AI is useful here because producing three faithful variants by hand is slow; producing them with AI and then checking each against the original is fast and reviewable.

### Worked example 3: Job-search writing — a rough cover letter adapted for a specific role

**Rough draft a learner wrote in 10 minutes:**
> Dear Hiring Manager, I am writing to apply for the operations role at your company. I have worked for several years in administration and customer support, and I have helped my team improve how we handle requests. I am also learning AI tools and have started using them to draft replies and summarise meetings. I think I would be a good fit for your team. Please find my CV attached. Thank you for considering my application. Sincerely, Mzalendo.

The draft is honest but generic. It does not match the role, does not mention the company, does not give any evidence, and does not stand out from a hundred other letters.

**The role posting (paraphrased):** Operations Associate at a small Nairobi-based logistics startup. Responsibilities: handling customer queries on WhatsApp and email, coordinating with drivers, maintaining records in Google Sheets, escalating issues to the operations manager. Useful: familiarity with AI tools to speed up routine drafting; comfort with structured records.

**Prompt:**
> Rewrite the cover letter below to match this role posting. Constraints: maximum 200 words, plain professional tone, no marketing words ("passionate," "thrilled," "world-class"), keep the writer's voice (calm, direct, not over-promising), include one concrete example showing experience handling customer queries and one showing record-keeping. Mention how the writer uses AI tools as a productivity helper, not as a skill they oversell. Do not invent any experience. If a claim cannot be supported by the draft, leave it out.
>
> Role posting: [paste]
> Draft cover letter: [paste]

**Improved output:**
> Dear Hiring Manager,
>
> I am applying for the Operations Associate role. The work you describe — handling customer queries on WhatsApp and email, coordinating with drivers, and maintaining structured records — matches what I have been doing for the last three years.
>
> In my current role I handle around 40 customer messages a day across WhatsApp and email, including refund queries and delivery follow-ups. I keep a Google Sheet of every issue, the action taken, and the resolution date, which has reduced repeat questions from the same customers.
>
> I have also started using AI tools (Claude and ChatGPT) to draft first versions of replies and to summarise weekly issue patterns for my team lead. I review every AI draft before sending, especially anything involving a date, a refund amount, or a commitment to the customer. I treat the tools as a faster keyboard, not a decision-maker.
>
> I have attached my CV. I would appreciate the chance to talk about how I would handle a typical day in this role.
>
> Sincerely,
> Mzalendo

**Human review notes (the learner should write these on the same artifact):**
> I checked: the "40 messages a day" figure is from my own log, not invented. The "reduced repeat questions" claim is true but small — I would not generalise it to a percentage. The AI-tool description is accurate and matches how I actually use them. I removed a sentence that said "I am passionate about logistics" because it was not specific. I am responsible for every claim made about my experience.

**Disclosure (short, attached when sent):**
> AI assistance: I used Claude to adapt my draft cover letter to this role posting. I rewrote sentences that did not sound like me, removed two lines I could not back up, and verified that the example numbers match my own records. The final wording, the claims about my experience, and the application are mine.

**What the learner should notice.** The improved letter is shorter than the rough draft but says more, because each sentence now does work — it shows evidence, not enthusiasm. The role-specific match was guided by a constraint in the prompt ("match this role posting"), not by hoping the AI would notice. The disclosure is short, accurate, and would not embarrass the writer if a hiring manager asked about it.

### Worked example 4: Voice flattening, with a corrective prompt

**Original message (a learner wrote this to a customer about a delayed M-Pesa refund):**
> Hi James, I saw your message about the refund. The transaction has been flagged on our side and I am chasing the reversal with Safaricom this morning. I will send you a confirmation code as soon as it lands, usually within 24 hours but sometimes a bit longer. Sorry for the wait. — Maria

**Polished AI rewrite (after the learner asked for "a more professional version"):**
> Dear James, Thank you for reaching out and for your patience. We sincerely appreciate you bringing this matter to our attention. Please be assured that your refund request is being handled with the highest priority. Our team is currently liaising with Safaricom to expedite the reversal, and you can expect a confirmation within one business day. We deeply apologise for any inconvenience this delay may have caused. Should you have any further questions, please do not hesitate to reach out. Warm regards, Maria.

**What was lost.** Maria's original was clear and human. She said "I am chasing the reversal" — first person, active, specific. The AI rewrite says "our team is currently liaising," which is generic and corporate. Maria's original gave a real timeline ("usually within 24 hours but sometimes a bit longer") — honest. The AI rewrite says "within one business day" — falsely precise. Maria's original signed off with a short "sorry for the wait." The AI rewrite says "we deeply apologise for any inconvenience this delay may have caused," which is a stock customer-service phrase. The customer reading the polished version cannot tell who is writing or whether anyone is actually working on the refund.

**Corrective prompt:**
> Rewrite the message below in the original sender's voice. Voice constraints: short sentences, first person ("I" not "we" or "our team"), plain words, no "sincerely," "deeply apologise," "rest assured," "do not hesitate," "warm regards," or other customer-service stock phrases. Keep the original's honest timeline phrasing ("usually within 24 hours but sometimes a bit longer"). Maximum 70 words. Sign-off: just "Maria."
>
> Message: [paste]

**Revised version:**
> Hi James, I have your refund request. The transaction is flagged on our side and I am chasing the reversal with Safaricom this morning. I will send you the confirmation code as soon as it lands — usually within 24 hours, sometimes a bit longer. Sorry for the wait. — Maria

**What the learner should notice.** The revised version is almost the same as Maria's original because the original was already in her voice. The lesson is not that AI cannot help here; it is that AI's first instinct is to over-formalise and to flatten voice. A corrective prompt that names the voice constraints — first person, short sentences, no stock phrases — pulls the rewrite back toward the sender. If a message already sounds like you, sometimes the right answer is to use AI to *shorten* or *check*, not to rewrite.

## Supplied practice activity materials

### Practice activity 1: Tone repair lab (15–20 minutes)

You are given three rough messages. Rewrite each one for tone, truth, and audience fit. After each rewrite, name in one line which facts you preserved and which softening moves you allowed yourself to make.

**Rough message A — manager to a colleague:**
> I do not understand why this proposal still has the wrong figures in it after I sent corrections last week. We cannot send this to the client like this. Please fix it before end of day.

Audience: a peer at the same level. Channel: email. Constraint: keep the same-day deadline, keep "wrong figures" as the issue, do not blame the colleague for everything in the proposal.

**Rough message B — small-business owner to a supplier:**
> Your last delivery was three days late and one of the boxes was opened. We are losing customers because of this. If this happens again I will move to another supplier.

Audience: an existing supplier you would prefer to keep. Channel: WhatsApp message followed by email. Constraint: keep the late delivery and the opened box as facts, keep the implied consequence (you will consider another supplier), but make space for them to explain.

**Rough message C — student to a lecturer:**
> I missed the deadline for the assignment because my laptop broke and I could not afford to fix it immediately. I know this is not your problem but I am asking if I can submit late.

Audience: a university lecturer in Nairobi. Channel: email. Constraint: keep the real reason (laptop broke, money), do not over-apologise, ask for a specific extension date, not a vague "as soon as possible."

For each rewrite, run A-T-V-F-R. Save your three rewrites in your working notes — they feed into the portfolio artifact.

### Practice activity 2: Audience adaptation drill (20–25 minutes)

You are given one source message and three audiences. Produce three rewrites — one per audience — and write a one-paragraph rationale explaining what changed for each.

**Source message (a small Nairobi-based clinic informing people that an appointment system is changing):**
> From 1 May, all appointments must be booked through the new online portal at [link]. Walk-ins will only be accepted between 8 a.m. and 10 a.m. on weekdays. The old phone-booking line will be closed from 30 April. Patients with regular ongoing prescriptions should book a 15-minute review with their assigned clinician within the first six weeks. Patients without internet access can call reception during office hours and a staff member will book on their behalf.

Rewrite this for:

1. **A WhatsApp broadcast to existing patients.** Short, friendly but clear, around 80–100 words. The reader is on a phone. Highlight what they need to do.
2. **A printed notice on the clinic wall.** No greeting, no sign-off. Bullet points are acceptable. The reader is in the waiting room with five minutes. Highlight the three or four things that must not be missed.
3. **An email to a corporate client whose employees use the clinic.** Slightly more formal. The reader is an HR manager who will forward it to staff. Highlight what changes for their employees and what assistance the clinic can give.

For the rationale paragraph, name the audience-specific change in each version and explain why you made it (for example: "the printed notice drops the greeting because the reader is already inside the clinic; the email keeps the prescription-review detail because HR may need to communicate it to staff with chronic conditions").

### Practice activity 3: Voice-preservation check (15 minutes)

You are given one original message and one AI rewrite. Identify what voice was lost in the rewrite and write a corrective prompt that restores it.

**Original message (a freelance designer following up a missed payment, sent as an email):**
> Hi Daniel, just checking in on the invoice from 12 March — it is now 18 days overdue. I know things come up, but if there is something I should know on your side, let me know and we can sort it. If everything is fine, can you confirm a payment date? Thanks, Wanjiru.

**AI rewrite:**
> Dear Daniel, I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to follow up on the outstanding invoice dated 12 March, which is now significantly overdue. I would deeply appreciate your prompt attention to this matter. Should there be any concerns or issues from your end, I am happy to discuss them at your earliest convenience. Otherwise, kindly confirm the expected date of payment. Thank you for your continued cooperation. Warm regards, Wanjiru.

Identify three voice elements that were lost. Then write a corrective prompt (constraints, voice rules, sign-off) that would produce a rewrite closer to Wanjiru's original.

### Practice activity 4: Professional or job-search communication task (20–25 minutes)

You are given one rough professional or job-search message. Improve it using A-T-V-F-R, with a clear prompt, a single rewrite (not three), and a short human review note.

**Rough message — LinkedIn outreach to a potential mentor:**
> Hi, I saw your profile and I am very interested in your career. I am also working in the same field and would love to connect and learn from you. I have been reading about AI and I think it is very interesting. If you have time, can we talk? Thank you. — [Your name]

The recipient is a senior product manager at a Nairobi tech company who already gets many cold messages a week. The channel is LinkedIn (around 300 characters before the message is truncated in their inbox). The goal is a 15-minute conversation, not a long-term mentorship arrangement on day one. Constraint: no flattery, no "would love to," no "very interesting," no claims about your own background you cannot support.

Produce: an improved prompt, the improved message, and a short human review note (three lines maximum) describing what you preserved as true, what you removed as unsupported, and how you made the message recognisably yours.

## Knowledge-to-output task

Produce one document: an **Audience-Fit Communication Pack**. It contains your applied work for this module and is the artifact you save into your portfolio. It is self-learner-ready — you do not need a facilitator to produce it.

The pack contains, in order:

1. **One original message.** Choose a real message from your own life that has a clear sender, a clear receiver, and at least one fact that must remain intact. It can be a customer reply, a parent message, a manager email, a school notice, or a business follow-up. Paste it as it actually was, not cleaned up.
2. **Three audience-specific rewrites of that same message.** Each one is for a different audience you genuinely write for. For each, name the audience and the channel.
3. **A short tone rationale (around 150 words).** Explain what changed across the three rewrites and why. Name at least one place where tone changed but truth did not.

## Checkpoint quiz

Eight questions. Pass threshold: at least 6 of 8 questions correct. Mixed formats per the Course Assessment Standards.

1. *(Multiple choice)* The "Truth" check in A-T-V-F-R is mainly designed to catch:
   a. Long sentences that should be shortened.
   b. Facts, numbers, dates, or commitments the AI may have smoothed away or changed during a rewrite.
   c. Spelling and grammar errors.
   d. Overly formal tone.

2. *(Multiple choice)* You ask AI to make a sharp email "more polite" without naming what to keep. The most common failure is that:
   a. The message becomes too long.
   b. The grammar gets worse.
   c. The tone is softened so much that the original facts (deadline, consequence, request) disappear or weaken.
   d. The AI refuses to rewrite the email.

3. *(Scenario)* A school sends out an AI-drafted closure notice that opens with "Dear esteemed parents and guardians, we are thrilled to inform you of an exciting update regarding our operations." Identify two specific A-T-V-F-R failures in this opening line.

4. *(Scenario)* A learner asks AI to "rewrite this customer apology to sound more professional." The AI replaces "I am chasing the reversal with Safaricom this morning" with "Our team is currently liaising with the relevant service provider to expedite resolution." What two things has the rewrite damaged, and how would you correct it in a follow-up prompt?

5. *(Short answer)* In one or two sentences, explain the difference between *tone* and *truth* in an AI-assisted message, and why the difference matters.

6. *(Short answer)* Name three voice constraints you can put into a writing prompt to stop AI from flattening your voice into a generic style.

7. *(Application)* Take the rough sentence below and write (a) a one-line audience-specific rewrite for a parent of a Form 2 student in Nairobi, and (b) a one-line audience-specific rewrite for an HR manager at a corporate client. Keep the same facts in both.
   > Rough sentence: "Due to a suspected water-supply contamination issue, the school will close from Wednesday at noon and reopen on Monday at 8 a.m. unless updated."

8. *(Application)* Read the AI-assisted message below. Run A-T-V-F-R on it and return one short comment per check (Audience, Truth, Voice, Format, Responsibility). Identify whether you would send this message as written or rewrite it, and why.
   > Message: "Dear valued customer, we sincerely apologise for any inconvenience caused by the recent payment delay. Please rest assured that our dedicated team is working tirelessly to resolve this matter, and you will receive your refund shortly. Should you have any further concerns, please do not hesitate to reach out. Warm regards, [Company]."

## Answer key with explanations

1. **b.** Truth in A-T-V-F-R is the check on facts, numbers, dates, and commitments — exactly the things AI is most likely to smooth or invent during a rewrite. (a) and (c) are not what Truth is for; (d) is the Voice or Format check, not Truth.

2. **c.** This is the harsh-email failure mode shown in Worked example 1. Without naming what must be preserved, AI tends to remove the consequence and the deadline because those words sound impolite. (a), (b), and (d) are not the typical failure.

3. *Strong answers* identify two of the following: *Audience* — a closure notice for a real safety issue is not an "exciting update," and parents reading this are concerned, not celebrating, so the framing is wrong for the audience. *Voice* — "thrilled" and "esteemed" are stock-AI words that the school would not normally use; the voice has been flattened into a generic announcement. *Format* — a closure notice should open with the closure information, not with greetings and excitement. *Truth* — calling a contamination closure an "exciting update" misrepresents what is happening. Any two of these named clearly with reference to the line is full credit.

4. *Strong answers* identify both damages: (1) Voice has been lost — "I am chasing" (first person, active, specific) became "our team is currently liaising" (corporate, vague, no person responsible), so the customer no longer knows who is working on the refund. (2) Truth has been weakened — "Safaricom" (named) became "the relevant service provider" (generic), so the customer cannot tell whether the company actually escalated the right party. *The corrective prompt* should specify: keep first-person voice, keep the named party (Safaricom), no stock customer-service phrases, short sentences, sign off with the actual sender's name.

5. *Strong answers* explain that *tone* is how the message lands (warm, sharp, formal, calm) and *truth* is what the message contains (the deadline, the figure, the commitment, the policy). The difference matters because AI rewriting often changes tone *and* truth at the same time when only tone was meant to change, so you must protect truth explicitly in the prompt.

6. *Strong answers* name three concrete constraints, for example: use first person ("I" not "we" or "our team"); short sentences (under 20 words on average); no stock phrases like "rest assured," "deeply apologise," "do not hesitate," "thrilled," or "warm regards"; preserve a specific phrase from the original; specify the sign-off; cap the length. Any three concrete, prompt-ready constraints.

7. *Strong answers* keep all three facts (closure from Wednesday at noon, reopen Monday at 8 a.m., reason is suspected water-supply contamination) and adapt the framing to each audience. Example acceptable answers:
   - *(For a parent):* "Dear parents, the school will close from Wednesday at noon because of a suspected water-supply problem and reopen on Monday at 8 a.m. unless we send another update — please collect your child by 12:30."
   - *(For an HR manager):* "We are writing to inform you that the school will close from Wednesday at noon due to a suspected water-supply contamination issue, with classes scheduled to resume on Monday at 8 a.m. unless updated; staff with children attending should plan accordingly."
   *Acceptable* if both versions keep all three facts and clearly fit their audience. *Partial* if one fact is missing or the tone is identical across both. *Unacceptable* if facts have been changed or invented.

8. *Strong answers* run the message through all five checks. Acceptable comments resemble:
   - *Audience:* generic — "valued customer" tells the reader nothing; the message could be addressed to anyone.
   - *Truth:* weak — "you will receive your refund shortly" makes a commitment without naming when, which is a fact that should be specific or removed.
   - *Voice:* flattened — stock phrases ("sincerely apologise," "rest assured," "working tirelessly," "do not hesitate") with no recognisable sender.
   - *Format:* the email is too long for what it actually says; this could be 50 words.
   - *Responsibility:* signed off with "[Company]," not a person, so no one is named as responsible for the refund.
   *Strong answers* conclude that the message should be rewritten before sending, with a specific timeline, a named sender, and Maria-style first-person voice. *Partial credit* if three or four checks are run cleanly. *Unacceptable* if the answer says the message is fine as written.

## Revision guidance

If your Audience-Fit Communication Pack does not yet meet the criterion, the most likely issues are below. Each one points to a specific section of this module to revisit before you revise.

- **The message is polished but not true.** Return to the *Tone versus truth* section of the core lesson and Worked example 1. Then re-run the Truth check from A-T-V-F-R on every rewrite — list every fact, number, date, and commitment in your original, and confirm each one survived the rewrite untouched.
- **The audience is unclear.** Return to the *Audience fit* section of the core lesson and Worked example 2. Then redo Practice activity 2 with a different source message — name the audience and the channel for each rewrite *before* you write, not after.
- **Your voice has disappeared.** Return to the *Voice preservation* section of the core lesson and Worked example 4. Then redo Practice activity 3, paying attention to the corrective-prompt step. Read your rewrites aloud — if a recipient who knows you would not recognise the message as yours, the voice check failed.
- **The tone is too harsh or too vague.** Re-read the harsh-email rewrite (Worked example 1) and pay attention to how the improved version preserves consequence without being aggressive. If your rewrites are too soft, look for the place a fact has been removed; if they are too sharp, look for the place a constraint or context is missing.
- **Claims are not verified.** Return to Module 6's C-S-V-E model. Any claim, number, date, or commitment in your professional or job-search artifact must be verifiable to a real source — your own records, an employer's confirmation, a public document. If you cannot trace a claim back to something real, remove it from the message.
- **The message is not appropriate for the situation.** Return to *When not to use AI for sensitive communication without review* in the core lesson. If your message touches on someone else's personal data, a disciplinary issue, a medical or legal question, or a crisis, you may need to draft it manually or get a human reviewer beyond yourself before sending.
