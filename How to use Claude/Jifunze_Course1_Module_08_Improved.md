# Module 8: AI for Learning, Study, and Understanding

## Continuity bridge from Module 7

Module 7 was about communication. You learned to use AI to write to different audiences, preserve your voice, keep facts intact, and stay accountable for what went out under your name. The discipline there was simple to state: AI can help you say something more clearly, but it cannot decide what is true and it cannot replace the responsibility you carry for the words on the page.

Module 8 takes the same posture and turns it inward. Where Module 7 protected the reader, Module 8 protects the learner — you. The risk shifts. In communication, the danger is sending out something AI shaped that you did not actually mean. In learning, the danger is reading something AI shaped and believing you understood it when you did not. A clear AI explanation is not the same as your own understanding. A fluent summary you can nod along to is not a recall test you can pass tomorrow. This module is about the gap between those two states and how to close it on purpose.

You will use earlier skills throughout. The prompt control from Module 3 lets you ask for explanations at the right depth instead of taking the first answer the tool offers. The structured prompt design from Module 4 lets you set up retrieval questions, scenario drills, and graded difficulty. The iteration and comparison from Module 5 lets you push past the first explanation when it is too vague or too dense for the way you actually study. The verification work from Module 6 applies the same way to learning claims as to research claims — a confidently delivered fact in a study session is no more reliable than a confidently delivered fact in a brief. And the communication discipline from Module 7 carries directly into the teach-back: explaining a concept in your own voice, to a specific imagined audience, is one of the strongest ways to find out whether you actually understand it.

By the end of this module, you should be able to use AI as a study partner that strengthens your understanding rather than as a shortcut that hides the gaps in it.

## Module purpose

Most learners do not fail because they cannot find good explanations. They fail because they confuse reading a good explanation with knowing the material. AI makes this confusion easier by producing explanations that sound right, look organized, and slide down without effort. A learner who reads three of these in a row often feels confident, then sits down to a problem and discovers they cannot reproduce the reasoning, answer the question, or apply the concept. The information was in the room. It never got into the learner.

This module exists to keep that gap from forming silently. It teaches a practical discipline for using AI in study: ask for explanations at the right level, generate questions you cannot already answer, test recall before declaring understanding, identify and repair weak areas, and use teach-back as the final check. The discipline is small. It is also the difference between a learner who passes assessments because they actually understood the material and a learner who can recognize correct answers in a list but cannot produce them on demand.

The module also takes a clear position on honesty. AI-supported study is not the same as AI-replaced study, and the line between them matters in school, in workplace assessment, and in self-paced upskilling. This module names where the line sits, when disclosure is required, and what to avoid altogether — so a learner who follows the practice can do so with their integrity intact.

## Learner outcomes

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

- Distinguish AI-supported learning, where AI strengthens your own thinking, from AI-replaced learning, where AI does the thinking for you.
- Use AI to explain a concept at a chosen depth, generate examples and analogies, produce practice questions, give feedback on your answers, and surface gaps you did not see.
- Test your own understanding through retrieval practice and teach-back, not through re-reading.
- Recognize false confidence — the feeling of having understood that comes from fluent input alone — and use specific checks to confirm or reject it.
- Use AI honestly across study, assessment, and workplace-learning contexts, with a clear sense of what is acceptable, what should be disclosed, and what should be avoided.
- Produce a weak-area repair plan tied to a real practical learning goal, with a one-week retrieval and spaced-practice schedule.

## Core lesson

### Convenience versus understanding

There is a tempting state when you study with AI. The explanation arrives quickly. It is well organized. It uses the right words. You read it, follow it, and feel that you have understood. Often you have not. What has happened is that the AI has done the cognitive work for you: it has organized the concept, produced an example, drawn the connection, and handed the result back as a finished thought. You have read a finished thought. You have not yet built one.

This is the convenience-versus-understanding distinction. Convenience is fast input. Understanding is the ability to reproduce, vary, apply, and explain a concept on your own. Convenience is what AI is very good at giving you. Understanding is what only your own effort can give you. The two are easy to confuse because they feel similar in the moment — fluency in, fluency out — but they part company the moment the AI is no longer there. A learner who has only used AI for convenience falls silent under a recall test. A learner who has used AI to support real practice does not.

The good news is that AI is also very good at *helping you build* understanding when you point it that way. The same tool that can hand you a finished explanation can quiz you, generate harder examples, point out where your reasoning broke, and suggest the next thing to drill. The choice between convenience and understanding is not about whether you use AI. It is about what you ask AI to do and what you do with the answer.

### Why clear explanations create false confidence

False confidence is the specific failure mode this module is built to prevent. It happens when a learner reads a clear AI explanation, recognizes every sentence as plausible, and concludes that they have learned the concept. Recognition is a low bar — you can recognize a face in a photo without being able to draw it from memory — and AI explanations are designed to be highly recognizable. They use familiar phrasing, common analogies, and the structure your brain expects. The fluency of the explanation gets misread as the fluency of your own grasp.

The honest test is not *can I follow this explanation* but *can I reproduce this explanation, or apply it to a new problem, without looking?* If the answer is no, the only thing you have confirmed is that the AI wrote a readable paragraph. That is information about the AI, not about you. The repair is straightforward: close the explanation and try to recall the core idea, the steps, or the example in your own words. If you can — keep going. If you cannot — that is exactly the place to study, not to skim.

### Using AI for explanation, examples, questions, feedback, and revision

A study-supportive use of AI lives in five practical modes. Each one moves you toward understanding rather than away from it.

**Explanation at the right depth.** Ask the AI to explain a concept at a specific level — *as if to a Form 4 student, as if to a colleague unfamiliar with the topic, as if I already know X but not Y*. Vague prompts get vague explanations. Use Module 3 control: name the audience, the depth, the length, and the pieces you want covered. If the first explanation is too high or too low, iterate (Module 5) — *now do it one level deeper, with a worked example.*

**Examples and analogies.** Ask for two or three examples that illustrate the concept, then ask which example most closely fits the situation you actually care about. Examples are how concepts get rooted; an explanation without a concrete example is harder to retain and easier to misremember.

**Practice questions.** Ask the AI to generate questions that test recall and application of what you just learned. Specify the format (multiple choice, short answer, scenario, application) and the difficulty (a learner who has just read this once, a learner preparing for an exam). The point is not that the AI's questions are perfect. The point is that you are forced to retrieve, not to recognize.

**Feedback on your answers.** Answer the questions in your own words first, *without* looking at the explanation. Then paste your answer to the AI and ask for honest feedback against the original concept — what is correct, what is missing, what is misstated. This is the bridge from input to output: the AI moves from teacher to grader, and you move from reader to producer.

**Revision support.** Ask the AI to surface what you should re-study based on the gaps in your answers. Ask it to generate a short, prioritized list of weak areas, the questions you should be able to answer next, and the order to address them in. Revision should not be a vague *go over it again* — it should be a list of specific items with specific tests.

These five modes turn AI into a study partner rather than a study substitute. They share one quality: each one ends with you producing something — an answer, an example, a teach-back — rather than with you receiving something.

### Retrieval practice

Retrieval practice is the act of pulling information out of your head, with the source closed. It is the strongest study technique known, and it is the technique AI most easily lets you skip. When you read an AI explanation and feel you have understood, the next move that actually builds learning is to close the explanation, set a timer for two or three minutes, and write what you remember. Whatever you cannot write — that is the gap. Whatever you can — that is the concept beginning to land.

Retrieval is uncomfortable. It feels less productive than re-reading, because re-reading feels smooth and retrieval feels effortful and partial. The discomfort is the point: the effort of retrieval is what consolidates the memory. A learner who re-reads three times learns less than a learner who re-reads once and recalls twice.

AI is useful for retrieval in two ways. First, it generates the questions you retrieve against. Second, after your retrieval attempt, it can show you what you missed and suggest the next set of questions, harder ones if you did well or simpler ones if you did not. The AI never replaces the act of retrieval; it scaffolds the act around it.

### Teach-back

Teach-back is retrieval at full strength. You take a concept you believe you understand, close every source, and explain it — out loud, in writing, to a real person, or to an imagined one — as if you were teaching someone who has not seen the material. The audience matters. Explaining to a colleague, a younger sibling, a hypothetical interview panel, or a real friend each forces a different translation, and translation is where understanding shows itself.

A teach-back is good when it covers the core idea, an example, and the limit (where the idea breaks down). A weak teach-back is one that copies the AI's phrasing, uses jargon without explaining it, or skates over the example. If your first teach-back feels weak, that is normal — and it is the most useful diagnostic in this module. A weak teach-back tells you exactly which part of the concept has not yet landed. The repair is to study that part, not the whole concept again.

The link to Module 7 is direct. The audience-fit and voice-preservation work you did for communication carries straight into teach-back: explaining the same concept for two different audiences, in your own voice, is both a learning check and a communication exercise.

### Spaced practice

Spaced practice is retrieval over time. A concept retrieved once, today, lands lightly. A concept retrieved today, tomorrow, four days later, and a week later lands deeply. The intervals matter more than the total time spent. A learner who studies one hour today and forty minutes spread over the following week will outperform a learner who studies four hours today and nothing afterward.

A simple spacing schedule for a single new concept:

- **Day 0:** Learn the concept (with AI explanation, examples, and a first set of questions).
- **Day 1:** Retrieve the concept from memory before looking at any source. Mark gaps.
- **Day 3:** Retrieve again. Repair the gaps from Day 1.
- **Day 7:** Final retrieval. If solid, the concept is consolidated; if not, identify the specific weak spot.

This is the schedule the practice activities below use. It works whether you are preparing for a school exam, a digital-work skill assessment, a workplace certification, or simply trying to retain something you read.

### Weak-area repair

Weak-area repair is what you do when retrieval surfaces a gap. The wrong response is to re-read the entire chapter or to ask the AI to explain it all again. The right response is narrower: identify exactly which sub-concept, step, or example you cannot reproduce, and target that. AI is useful here because it can take a partial, hesitant answer of yours, identify the specific missing piece, and generate a focused mini-lesson and a couple of new questions just for that piece. You then retrieve again, on the narrower piece, the next day.

The cycle is: retrieve → identify weak area → repair → retrieve again. A learner who runs this cycle even five or six times across a week of study will outperform a learner who passively reads the same material for the same number of hours.

### Honest use in school, work, and certification settings

The honesty question in study is not the same as the honesty question in professional output. In study, the question is not only *who is the work for* but also *who is the work building*. If you let AI do the cognitive work that was meant to build *you* — write your essay, answer your assessment, complete your assignment — you have not produced dishonest work primarily; you have produced an unbuilt skill. The grade may pass; the capability does not exist.

The line is therefore practical:

- **Acceptable in most learning contexts:** asking AI to explain a concept, generate practice questions, check your answers, give feedback on a draft you wrote yourself, suggest examples, point out gaps in your reasoning, or help you plan a study schedule.
- **Often acceptable but disclose:** using AI to draft an outline you then heavily revise, to brainstorm angles for an essay you write yourself, to translate an explanation into your stronger language, or to summarize a long source you then check against the original.
- **Avoid in any assessment context where AI is not permitted:** producing the answer the assessment is meant to grade, completing a take-home exam, generating the analysis a course is testing you on, writing the reflection meant to evidence your own thinking. If the rules forbid AI, do not use it. Module 7 made the same point about author voice; here it is about author capability.
- **Always ask first when unsure:** if your school, employer, or certification body has a written policy, follow it. If they do not, ask the assessor in writing before you start. The Tool Stance and Disclosure Note set the standard: when in doubt, disclose; when forbidden, do not use.

A useful working principle: AI should make the *learning process* faster, not the *demonstration of learning* faster. If you cannot tell which side of that line you are on, you are probably on the wrong one.

### Disclosure in study and assessment contexts

Disclosure in learning contexts works the same way it does in writing (Module 7) and will work in responsibility (Module 9): you name the tool, the task, the review, and what is your own. In study and assessment specifically, three additional things are worth being explicit about.

**Disclose to the assessor when AI assistance touched the work the assessor is grading.** A take-home essay that AI helped outline still needs disclosure even if the writing is yours. A coding exercise where AI suggested an approach you implemented yourself still needs disclosure. The reader is forming a judgment about *your* capability; they need an accurate picture of where AI ended.

**Disclose to yourself when AI is doing more cognitive work than you are.** This is private, but useful. If you cannot recall how you arrived at an answer because most of the steps came from AI, treat that answer as not yours yet, and re-do the steps. Learning that bypasses your effort does not stay.

**Do not disclose performatively.** A disclosure that overstates AI involvement to look modest is no better than one that understates it. Be accurate in both directions, the same way Module 7 required.

The Course1_Disclosure_Note has the standard short template. For a study artifact, a one-line disclosure is usually enough: *I used Claude to generate the practice questions in this set and to give feedback on my first three answers. The teach-back and the weak-area diagnosis are mine.*

### Self-directed upskilling for employability pathways

Self-directed upskilling is the use case that makes this module matter past the next exam. A learner preparing for a digital-work skill assessment, a freelancer learning a new tool to take on a contract, a small-business owner picking up basic bookkeeping, a junior tech builder studying a no-code platform — each of them is doing the same thing: turning a defined, employable skill into something they can demonstrate. AI is well suited to support this kind of learning when used with the discipline this module teaches.

The pattern, which Worked Example 2 walks through in full, is:

1. Define the target skill specifically. Not *Excel*; *I can build a budget spreadsheet with formulas for totals, percentage change, and a basic chart, and I can explain each formula.*
2. Use AI to break the target into a small number of sub-skills you can study in sequence.
3. For each sub-skill, run the explain-example-question-feedback-retrieve cycle.
4. Build a portfolio artifact that demonstrates the skill on a realistic task — not a tutorial example.
5. Disclose AI use on the artifact.

The module's portfolio task uses exactly this pattern, sized to one practical learning goal.

### Module 6 verification applied to learning claims

A clear AI explanation can be wrong. This is the hardest case because the explanation often sounds *most* clear when the AI has filled in plausible detail to smooth the prose. Module 6 trained you to verify confidently stated claims in research and writing; in learning, the same discipline applies. When the AI tells you a fact, a date, a definition, a formula, or a procedure with crisp confidence, treat the claim like any other confidently stated AI output: ask whether you can check it.

This matters most for:

- Definitions in technical fields, where the AI may produce a near-correct but subtly off definition.
- Procedural steps (how to do X), where the AI may invent or reorder a step.
- Quoted sources, dates, statistics, or named studies, where the AI may produce something plausible-but-fictional.
- Local-context claims (laws, exam structures, certification requirements in a specific country), where the AI's defaults may not match your reality.

The check does not need to be heavy. A quick cross-reference against an authoritative source — the textbook, the official documentation, the issuing body's website — is usually enough. The cost of skipping the check, when the claim is wrong and you have memorized it, is high: you will retrieve the wrong fact under pressure, with confidence, because you were taught it confidently.

### Module 7 communication skills applied to teach-back

Module 7 taught audience fit, voice preservation, and the A-T-V-F-R review (audience, tone, voice, facts, responsibility). All five elements apply to a teach-back. Audience: who are you teaching to? Tone: encouraging or rigorous? Voice: your own, not the AI's. Facts: only what you can stand behind. Responsibility: your understanding is the thing being demonstrated.

A teach-back done well is, in form, a Module 7 piece of writing — short, audience-aware, voice-preserving, fact-honest. The difference is the purpose: in Module 7 the writing was for the reader; here, the writing is for *you*. A teach-back that reads cleanly enough to be Module 7-acceptable is also a teach-back that almost certainly reflects real understanding.

## Fully written worked examples

### Worked example 1: False-confidence example

**Scenario.** Joyce is studying basic financial literacy in preparation for a Digital Work Starter pathway assessment. She wants to understand the difference between gross and net pay, and how a payslip is constructed. She asks Claude.

**Prompt:**
> Explain the difference between gross pay and net pay in a Kenyan context, with an example of a payslip showing how deductions are calculated. Use plain language for someone new to formal employment.

**AI explanation (clear, well organized):**
> *Gross pay is the total amount you earn before any deductions. Net pay is what you take home after deductions. A typical Kenyan payslip might look like this:*
>
> *- Gross salary: KES 60,000*
> *- PAYE (income tax): KES 7,500*
> *- NSSF (pension): KES 1,080*
> *- SHIF (health insurance, formerly NHIF): KES 1,650*
> *- Housing Levy: KES 900*
> *- Net pay: KES 48,870*
>
> *Each deduction is calculated as a percentage of gross pay or against statutory bands. PAYE is calculated using progressive tax bands set by the Kenya Revenue Authority. NSSF is a fixed-rate contribution under the new Tier I and Tier II structure. SHIF replaced NHIF in 2024 and is calculated as a percentage of gross pay. The Housing Levy is a fixed percentage of gross pay.*

**Why Joyce thinks she understands.** The explanation is clear. It uses real Kenyan terms — PAYE, NSSF, SHIF, KRA, Housing Levy. The numbers add up. She nods through it twice and feels ready.

**The retrieval question (closed-book, the next day):**
> *Without looking, write the formula or rule for each of the four deductions on the payslip above, in your own words. Then construct a payslip for a gross salary of KES 80,000 and explain whether each deduction scales linearly with gross pay or not.*

**What happens.** Joyce can name all four deductions. She remembers PAYE is a band-based progressive tax, but cannot reconstruct the bands or recall whether the KES 7,500 figure was right for KES 60,000 gross. She thinks SHIF is a percentage but cannot say what percentage. She knows the Housing Levy is *a* percentage but cannot recall the figure. She freezes on the KES 80,000 case because she cannot scale the numbers without the rules.

The fluent explanation produced recognition. It did not produce reproducible knowledge.

**Repair plan.** Joyce does not re-read the explanation. Instead, she identifies the four narrow gaps — PAYE bands, NSSF rate structure, SHIF rate, Housing Levy rate — and runs a focused cycle on each:

1. *Verify each rate against an authoritative source (KRA website, NSSF Act, the Social Health Insurance Act, the Affordable Housing Act).* The AI's figures may be close but should not be trusted blindly. (Module 6 verification applies.)
2. *Generate a small set of recall questions for each rate.* Plain examples: *what is the PAYE rate for the KES 32,333–500,000 band; what is the NSSF Tier I cap; what is the SHIF rate as a percentage of gross.*
3. *Construct three new payslips at different gross levels (KES 35,000, KES 80,000, KES 200,000) without looking, then check.*
4. *Teach-back: explain to a friend why net pay does not scale linearly with gross pay.*

**What Joyce should notice.** The first explanation was not wrong (modulo the verification check). It was just not enough on its own. False confidence came from reading; real understanding came from retrieving, scaling, and teaching back. The same pattern applies to almost any concept she will study this year.

### Worked example 2: AI-supported study plan for a digital-work skill assessment

**Scenario.** Brian is preparing for a Digital Work Starter pathway skill assessment that includes a practical Excel/Google Sheets task: build a simple monthly cashflow tracker for a small business, with formulas for totals, monthly comparisons, and a chart. He has used spreadsheets only lightly. The assessment is in two weeks. He has about 45 minutes a day.

**Prompt to plan the study:**
> I am preparing for a practical spreadsheet assessment in two weeks. The task is to build a monthly cashflow tracker for a small business with formulas for monthly totals, percentage change between months, and a basic chart. I have about 45 minutes a day. Help me design a 12-day study plan that breaks this into sub-skills, with retrieval practice at each step. I want to be able to build the tracker from scratch on the day of the assessment, not follow a tutorial.

**AI study plan (Brian's iteration after one revision — see Module 5):**

> *Sub-skills, in order:*
> *1. Cell references and basic arithmetic formulas.*
> *2. SUM and SUMIF for totals.*
> *3. Percentage change formulas, absolute vs relative references.*
> *4. Building a clean monthly layout (rows = expense categories, columns = months).*
> *5. Charting from a range.*
> *6. Putting it together as a cashflow tracker for a real small-business case.*
>
> *12-day schedule:*
> *Days 1–2: sub-skill 1. Day 3: retrieval — build five small formulas from memory, no source open.*
> *Days 4–5: sub-skill 2. Day 6: retrieval — build a totals row from memory; explain SUMIF in your own words.*
> *Days 7–8: sub-skills 3 and 4. Day 9: retrieval — percentage change and a clean monthly layout, both from scratch.*
> *Days 10–11: sub-skill 5 and full integration on a small example.*
> *Day 12: dry run of the full assessment task, timed, with no source open.*

**The retrieval-practice loop Brian runs each day.**
- Watch or read the explanation for the day's sub-skill (AI explanation, official documentation, or a YouTube tutorial — the source is fine to vary).
- Close every source. Open a blank spreadsheet. Reproduce the example from memory.
- Where stuck, write the specific question (*how do I lock a column reference for percentage-change-against-January?*) and ask the AI for a focused answer, not a full re-explanation.
- Apply the answer. Try once more from scratch.
- End the session by writing one or two sentences describing what the sub-skill does and when you would use it. (This is a micro teach-back.)

**Weak-area review on Day 9.** Brian discovers his percentage-change formulas work for adjacent months but break when he tries to compare each month back to January. He sees the issue is absolute references. He does not re-do all of sub-skill 3. He runs a small focused cycle — five practice cells with mixed reference types, then a re-build of the percent-change column from scratch.

**Disclosure note Brian writes for his portfolio.**
> *I used Claude to design my 12-day study plan and to give targeted feedback when I got stuck on absolute references. The formulas, the layout, and the final cashflow tracker are mine. I did not have AI assistance during the assessment.*

**What the learner should notice.** The plan is not magic. It is the explain-example-question-feedback-retrieve cycle from the core lesson, sized to a real two-week window. The AI did not learn Excel for Brian. It scaffolded the practice that *let Brian* learn Excel. The portfolio artifact at the end is a tracker Brian can actually build, not a tutorial he followed.

### Worked example 3: Teach-back example

**Scenario.** Aisha is studying the concept of *cash flow versus profit* for a Small Business and Entrepreneurship pathway module. She has just read an AI explanation that includes the line: *"A business can be profitable on paper while running out of cash if customers pay slowly and suppliers must be paid fast."*

**AI explanation (excerpt):**
> *Profit is what is left after revenue minus expenses are recognized in your books. Cash flow is the timing of money actually moving in and out. A business can be profitable but cash-poor if it has invoiced customers who haven't paid yet, or if it must pay suppliers immediately. This is why even profitable small businesses can fail when working capital runs out.*

**Aisha's first teach-back attempt (weak):**
> *Profit is what's left when you subtract expenses from revenue. Cash flow is when the money actually comes in and out. A business can be profitable but cash-poor if customers haven't paid yet. So you need both to survive.*

**What is wrong.** This is mostly the AI's phrasing reorganized. It does not include an example, it does not explain *why* the timing mismatch matters, and it does not mention working capital — the underlying concept that makes the distinction operational. Aisha could have produced this teach-back without understanding the idea.

**Feedback prompt:**
> Here is my teach-back of the difference between profit and cash flow: [paste]. Compare it against the original concept. What is missing or weak in my explanation? Be specific. Do not rewrite it for me.

**AI feedback (excerpt):**
> *Three things to strengthen. First, your teach-back states the difference but does not give a concrete example — without an example, the concept is recognized but not pictured. Second, you do not mention working capital, which is the underlying idea that explains why the timing mismatch matters. Third, your teach-back ends with "you need both to survive," which is true but generic. The original explanation included the operational consequence — that profitable small businesses can fail when cash runs out. That is the point of the concept and it is missing.*

**Aisha's improved teach-back:**
> *Profit and cash flow are different because they answer different questions. Profit asks: did the business earn more than it spent over a period — measured in the books, regardless of when money moved? Cash flow asks: when did money actually move in and out, and is there enough on hand right now to keep operating?*
>
> *Here is an example. A printing shop sells KES 200,000 of branded notebooks in March, on 60-day payment terms. The cost of the paper and printing is KES 120,000, which the shop pays its supplier in March. On the books, March looks profitable — KES 80,000 profit. In the bank, March looks empty: KES 120,000 went out, nothing came in. The customer pays in May.*
>
> *The shop is profitable but cash-poor. If it cannot pay April rent and salaries with the cash it has, it will close in April even though March was a profitable month. This is the working-capital problem: the gap between when money is owed to you and when you have to pay others.*
>
> *The practical lesson for a small business owner is that you cannot run the business off the profit number alone. You need to watch cash on hand, payment terms, and the timing of supplier obligations. A business that ignores cash flow can be killed by its own success — selling more than it can afford to wait to be paid for.*

**What changed.** Aisha now has a concrete example with KES figures, names the underlying concept (working capital), and ends with the operational consequence. Her teach-back is in her own voice (*"this is the working-capital problem"*) rather than the AI's. A reader who did not see the original explanation could learn the idea from this teach-back. That is the bar.

**What the learner should notice.** The first teach-back was honest effort. It was also weak. The fix did not come from re-reading the AI explanation; it came from generating a real example and forcing the operational consequence to the surface. This is the cycle: weak teach-back → focused feedback → improved teach-back. The improvement is the learning.

### Worked example 4: Honesty and disclosure across study, assessment, and workplace learning

**Scenario.** Same learner — Brian, from Worked Example 2 — is now in three slightly different situations. The honest move differs in each.

**Situation A — Self-directed study task (acceptable, light disclosure if shared).**
Brian is using AI to understand percentage-change formulas. He asks Claude to explain absolute versus relative references and to generate practice cells. He answers the practice cells himself, asks the AI to give feedback, repairs the gaps. He keeps a note in his portfolio that AI was used to scaffold the study.
- *Acceptable:* AI is supporting his learning, not replacing it.
- *Disclose:* in the portfolio context note, since the artifact will be reviewed.
- *Avoid:* nothing in this scenario crosses the line.

**Situation B — Take-home assessment with explicit AI-allowed-with-disclosure rules.**
Brian's pathway assessor allows AI use during the take-home cashflow-tracker task, provided AI use is disclosed. Brian uses Claude to suggest an approach for the percentage-change formulas, implements the formulas himself, asks AI to spot-check his chart range. He writes a one-paragraph disclosure naming what AI did and what he did.
- *Acceptable:* he followed the rule.
- *Disclose:* required by the rule. Disclosure is plain — what AI suggested, what he implemented, what he checked.
- *Avoid:* having the AI build the entire tracker for him and submitting it. Even with disclosure, that would not evidence his capability — and the assessor is grading capability.

**Situation C — Closed assessment with AI explicitly forbidden.**
Brian's certification body conducts a separate closed-book practical assessment where AI is not allowed during the test itself. Brian studies with AI in the weeks leading up. During the assessment, he does not use AI at all. He does not use a hidden phone window, does not use a translation tool with AI features, does not paste the question into anything.
- *Acceptable:* studying with AI before the test.
- *Disclose:* not required for the test itself, since AI was not used during it. He may mention his study process privately if asked.
- *Avoid:* any use of AI during the closed assessment. The rule is the rule.

**Situation D — Workplace learning where the manager is unsure.**
Brian's employer asks him to learn a new internal reporting tool. There is no written policy on AI use for self-paced internal training. Brian uses AI to draft a study plan. Before submitting his learning artifact, he asks his manager: *can I document that I used AI to scaffold my learning, or would you prefer I do not?* The manager says disclosure is fine.
- *Acceptable:* asking when unsure.
- *Disclose:* once permission is given.
- *Avoid:* assuming silence equals permission. The Tool Stance is explicit: when in doubt, ask.

**What the learner should notice.** "Honest AI use" is not a single rule. It is the same posture (Module 7 communication, Module 9 responsibility) applied to four different settings. The three things that travel across all four are: follow the rule that exists, disclose where disclosure is expected, and do not let AI replace the capability the assessment is meant to measure.

### Practice activity 2: Retrieval practice builder

Below is a short topic explanation. Read it once. Then *close the page* and complete the steps.

> **Topic: M-Pesa float and why a small business needs it.**
> A mobile-money agent in Kenya holds two pools: cash in the till and electronic float in their M-Pesa account. When a customer deposits cash, the agent's till goes up and their float goes down by the same amount, because M-Pesa is debiting the agent's float to credit the customer's wallet. When a customer withdraws, the opposite happens: the agent's till goes down (cash out to the customer) and float goes up. An agent who runs out of float cannot accept deposits; an agent who runs out of cash cannot pay out withdrawals. The agent's central operational task is keeping both pools balanced — usually by visiting an aggregator or a bank to convert between cash and float during the day. This is why a busy agent in a market area near payday needs more working capital than a quiet agent in a residential area on a Tuesday afternoon: their volume of conversions is higher.

**Steps.**

3. Re-open the topic. Compare your answers to the source. Mark each as *correct*, *partial*, or *missed*.
4. Pick the one item you missed or partially answered, and write one sentence explaining the specific gap (not "I forgot it" — *what* about it did you forget).

## Answer key with explanations

**Question 1.** *Correct: b.* Reproduction with an example from memory is the only option that demonstrates the learner has built the concept in their own head. (a) tests recognition, which is a low bar and does not show ownership. (c) tests in-the-moment fluency, which is exactly what creates false confidence. (d) is information about the explanation, not about the learner.

**Question 2.** *Correct: c.* Retrieval practice is closed-source recall followed by a check against the original. (a) is re-reading, which feels productive but produces less learning per minute. (b) is a useful explanation step but is still input, not output. (d) shifts modality but remains input.

**Question 3.** Strong answers should mention both: (1) Daniel mistook recognition for understanding — reading the explanation produced familiarity but not the ability to reproduce or apply the rules. (2) The repair: he should close the source and try to retrieve the deductions and rates from memory, then identify the specific sub-rules he could not reproduce; he should also verify the AI-stated rates against authoritative sources (KRA, NSSF, the relevant Acts) before memorizing them, since AI-stated rates can be near-correct but slightly wrong (Module 6 verification). Naming retrieval and verification both earns full credit. Naming only one of the two earns partial credit. A generic *"he should study more"* earns none.

**Question 4.** *Correct option: c.* Strong answers explain that closed-source recall (retrieval practice) builds memory and surfaces gaps, while re-reading or hearing a different explanation reinforces recognition without forcing the learner to produce the concept. The underlying technique is retrieval practice. Full credit for naming retrieval and explaining why output is stronger than input. Partial credit for picking c without explaining the mechanism. No credit for picking a or b.

**Question 5.** Model answer: *AI-supported learning is when the AI helps you produce understanding — by generating examples, questions, or feedback against work you produce yourself. AI-replaced learning is when the AI produces the work the learning was supposed to build, leaving you with the output but not the capability.* Strong answers name both directions of the distinction (what AI does, and what the learner does). Answers that only define one side earn partial credit.

**Question 6.** Model answer: *No, the learner does not need to disclose AI use during the closed assessment, because AI was not used during it. The learner may, separately, mention their study process if asked, but disclosure attaches to the work being graded.* Strong answers distinguish between *during the work* and *during the studying*. Disclosure attaches to the work the assessor is grading, not to the entirety of the learner's preparation. Answers that say "always disclose any AI use ever" miss the distinction; answers that say "never disclose anything study-related" miss the point of disclosure as well. Both earn partial credit.

**Question 7.** Strong answers identify three of the following weaknesses and propose a concrete improvement for each:

- *No example.* Improvement: add a concrete decision (e.g., spending KES 5,000 on a new tool versus buying inventory).
- *Generic ending.* "It's important to think about it when making decisions" is filler. Improvement: replace with a specific operational consequence (e.g., a small business owner choosing between two suppliers must include the foregone opportunity, not just the price).
- *Surface-level definition.* The teach-back defines opportunity cost as "what you give up" but does not capture that it is the *value of the next-best alternative* you forgo, which is the technical heart of the concept. Improvement: name *next-best alternative*.
- *No application context.* The teach-back is abstract. Improvement: tie to a real decision the imagined audience would face.
- *AI-flavored phrasing.* "It's important to think about it" is a generic AI-style closer. Improvement: replace with the learner's own voice.

Three weaknesses and three improvements, each specific, earns full credit. Two with specifics earns partial. Generic comments ("the teach-back is weak") earn none.

**Question 8.** A strong answer outlines a step-by-step plan that uses AI for explanation, example generation, question generation, and feedback, while keeping retrieval, teach-back, and assessment dry-runs as the learner's own work. A reasonable model answer:

> *Step 1 (Days 1–2). AI explains the difference between cash flow and profit at two levels (introductory, then operational). I read once, then close and write what I remember. Retrieval check: a one-paragraph difference statement from memory.*
>
> *Step 2 (Days 3–4). I ask AI to generate five practice questions including one numeric example (a small printing business with timing differences). I answer each without looking. Retrieval check: my answers compared against the explanation.*
>
> *Step 3 (Day 6). Teach-back. I write a 250-word explanation, in my own voice, including a KES-denominated example, that a small-business owner could understand. No AI input on the writing itself.*
>
> *Step 4 (Day 9). AI gives feedback on my teach-back — what is missing, what is weak, what is overstated. I revise once. I do not paste the AI's wording back in; I rewrite based on the feedback.*
>
> *Step 5 (Day 13, dry run). Closed-source. I produce a fresh teach-back on a new business example. I check it against the original concept. If a gap appears, I run one more focused retrieval cycle on it before the assessment.*

Strong answers name AI's role and the learner's role at each step, include retrieval and teach-back, and end with a dry run. Plans missing retrieval, missing teach-back, or that have AI doing the producing earn partial credit. Plans that are vague ("study more, ask AI when stuck") earn no credit.
