# Module 9: Responsible AI Use, Human Accountability, and Practical Guardrails

## Continuity bridge from Module 8

Module 8 taught you to use AI for learning without letting it do the learning for you. You read AI explanations critically, you used teach-back to check whether you actually understood a concept, you ran practice questions before you trusted yourself on a topic, and you wrote a weak-area repair plan that was honest about what you still needed to work on. The discipline behind that module is straightforward: AI is allowed to assist your understanding, but the understanding has to belong to you. If the AI's explanation is the only reason you can answer a question, you have not yet learned the topic — you have borrowed an answer.

Module 9 takes that posture and widens it. The same logic applies to every other thing AI helps you produce. You can use AI to draft an email, summarise a report, plan a project, prepare a customer reply, structure a study session, or sketch a workflow — and in each case, the work, the judgment, and the consequences remain yours. Responsibility does not shift to the tool because the tool wrote the first version. The tool does not know your customer, your manager, your patient, your student, your reader, or your context. You do. So you decide what is appropriate, you check what is true, you adjust what is wrong, and you stand behind what is shipped.

This module turns that responsible learning posture into practical guardrails for all AI use: personal, school, work, and business. It teaches you to classify a task by stake, to put guardrails in place *before* you start rather than apologise *after* something fails, to identify who is affected by an AI-assisted decision, to handle bias deliberately rather than by accident, and to decide when disclosure is required, wise, or simply unnecessary. It does not synthesise the whole course. The full synthesis belongs in Module 16. Module 9 is the bridge between foundational AI use — which you have now built — and applied AI work, which begins in Module 10 and continues through the rest of the course.

## Module purpose

Most early failures with AI use are not failures of the AI. They are failures of human accountability. Someone shipped an AI draft that contained an invented fact. Someone forwarded an AI summary as their own analysis without checking it. Someone used AI for a hiring decision that should have been made by a person. Someone disclosed AI involvement late or not at all and damaged a relationship that had been working. In every case, the person at the keyboard was the responsible party — and the responsibility did not disappear because AI contributed to the work.

This module teaches the operational habit that prevents that pattern. You learn to classify the stakes of a task before you start, to put a small set of guardrails in place that match those stakes, to identify the people affected by what you produce, to decide whether disclosure is required, wise, or unnecessary, and to recognise the situations where the right move is to pause or escalate rather than to keep refining a prompt. These habits carry directly into Module 10 (privacy and operational risk) and into every applied module after it. They are the difference between AI use that is productive and AI use that quietly creates problems for other people.

## Learner outcomes

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

- Identify a given AI-use task as low-, medium-, or high-stakes and explain why.
- Explain why human accountability for AI-assisted outputs does not transfer to the tool, and apply that explanation in your own writing about the work.
- Build a practical guardrail checklist that fits a specific personal, school, work, or business context.
- Decide when disclosure of AI use is required, wise, or unnecessary, and explain the reasoning.
- Identify likely bias and accountability risks in a given AI output, name who is affected, and rewrite the output with explicit human review and accountability.
- Produce a one-page Responsible AI Use statement covering one real or realistic context of your own.
- Recognise the situations where the right move is to pause or escalate a task rather than to refine the prompt.

## Core lesson

### AI assists, humans remain accountable

Start with the rule that does not move. Whatever AI helped produce, the human who chose the task, wrote the prompt, reviewed the output, and shipped the result is the accountable party. Accountability is not a moral abstraction; it is a practical position. If the customer reply you sent contains a wrong delivery date because the AI invented one, the customer is not going to call the AI. They are going to call you. If the report you submitted contains a fabricated citation, the marker is not going to fail the AI — they will fail you. If the candidate evaluation you wrote was shaped by an AI's biased phrasing and the candidate is harmed, the consequences land on the people, the team, and the organisation that signed off on the document. The tool does not have a name, a job, a licence, or a relationship with anyone affected. You do.

This is why every later section of this module is concrete rather than abstract. *Who is affected* is a question with names and faces. *What review did you do* is a question with a checkable answer. *What disclosure did you make* is a sentence either present or absent in the artifact. Accountability is built from those concrete pieces, not from the general statement that "the human is responsible." The general statement is true and almost useless on its own. The pieces that follow are how the truth gets operationalised.

### Stakes: low, medium, and high

A useful first move on any AI-assisted task is to ask, *what kind of stake does this carry?* The right guardrails depend on the answer. Three tiers are enough.

**Low-stakes use.** The output is for you, or for a small audience that already knows the context, and getting it slightly wrong is easy to fix. Examples: drafting a personal to-do list, brainstorming gift ideas, planning your own study schedule, generating practice questions for yourself, sketching a personal blog post you will edit later, summarising your own notes from a class you attended, drafting a casual message to a friend. The cost of an error is small and lands mostly on you. The right guardrails are light: a quick read for accuracy and tone, a sense check that nothing in the output should not be there, and the basic prompt-engineering discipline from Modules 3 and 4.

**Medium-stakes use.** The output goes to someone else — a colleague, a manager, a client, a customer, a small team — and getting it wrong creates rework, embarrassment, or a damaged relationship, but not lasting harm. Examples: a customer email about a delivery issue, an internal report summary, a proposal section, a meeting note shared with the team, a marketing post for a small business, a social media reply on behalf of an organisation, a research summary for a non-specialist colleague. The cost of an error includes someone else's time and trust. The right guardrails are firmer: explicit verification of any claim that could be wrong (the Module 6 discipline), a tone check (the Module 7 discipline), at least one read in the voice of the recipient before sending, and disclosure of AI involvement where the context expects it.

**High-stakes use.** The output influences a decision about a person, money, health, safety, employment, education, legal status, or anything that affects someone's life in a way that is hard to reverse. Examples: a hiring or firing decision, a clinical assessment or patient communication, a credit or loan recommendation, a disciplinary letter, an academic assessment that affects a student's record, a legal document, a contract clause, a regulatory filing, a safety procedure, a public statement on behalf of an organisation. The cost of an error can be a person's job, health, finances, education, reputation, or freedom. The right move is usually *not* to keep refining the prompt. The right move is to slow down, follow the organisation's policy, involve the person whose role it is to make the decision, and treat AI as at most a structuring or drafting aid that a qualified human reviews before any action is taken — if AI is allowed in the workflow at all. Many organisations restrict AI involvement in these contexts entirely, and for good reason. Module 10 covers the privacy side of these restrictions in detail.

The borders between tiers are not sharp. A summary of a meeting is medium stakes if it is shared with the team and high stakes if the meeting was a disciplinary hearing. A draft email is low stakes if it is to a friend and medium stakes if it is to a customer. The skill is to make the classification deliberately rather than by default, and to err upward when uncertain.

### Guardrails before use, not apology after failure

The most common failure pattern with AI use is reactive: someone shipped something, it went wrong, they apologised, they put a rule in place. That order is backwards. Apology after failure is necessary when failure has happened, but it does not prevent the next failure. Guardrails before use do.

A guardrail is a small, named rule you apply before you start a task. Guardrails are short. They are not a corporate policy document. The point is that they fit in your head and you actually use them. A typical personal guardrail set might be: *for medium-stakes work I will verify any factual claim before sending; for high-stakes work I will pause and check whether AI is appropriate at all; I will name the AI tool in any work I share that was substantially shaped by it; I will not paste personal data of others into a general AI tool*.

Guardrails work because they remove the decision from the moment of pressure. If you decide in advance that high-stakes work pauses for verification, you do not have to decide that under deadline. If you decide in advance that you will not paste raw client data, you do not have to think about it the next time you are in a hurry. The discipline is in the *advance*. A guardrail you write only after you have failed once is still useful, but it is more expensive than the same rule written before the work began.

The practice activities in this module include a guardrail checklist builder for several contexts. The activity is not theoretical. The output is meant to be a checklist you can paste into your own notes and reuse for the next year of AI work.

### Verification responsibility

Module 6 taught you to verify AI claims against trusted sources. That responsibility does not relax just because the work is now reaching another person. If anything, it tightens. The reader of your medium-stakes report assumes you checked the figures. The recipient of your customer email assumes the dates are correct. The colleague reading your summary assumes the conclusions are supported. They are not assuming that "the AI is usually right." They are assuming that *you* checked.

Verification responsibility means three things in this module. First, you are the verifier, not the AI; the AI cannot reliably check its own output. Second, the more the work matters, the more verification is required — a casual personal note may need almost none, a customer-facing email may need a quick check of dates and names, a report needs source-checking on every load-bearing claim. Third, when you cannot verify a claim, you say so honestly. The Module 6 vocabulary — *known*, *needs verification*, *assumption*, *open question* — applies here too. Shipping unverified claims as if they were verified is a category of failure that is hard to recover from.

### Communication responsibility

Module 7 taught you that audience fit is a responsibility, not a finishing touch. The same applies to AI-assisted communication. A drafted-by-AI customer email is not a finished customer email. A drafted-by-AI manager update is not a finished manager update. The output you send carries your name and your relationship with the reader. The AI's draft is a *first version* that has not yet been read in the voice of the person who will receive it.

Communication responsibility in Module 9 means three habits. First, read the AI's draft in the recipient's voice before sending — if it does not sound like something you would send to *this* person, edit until it does. Second, watch for tone drift; AI drafts tend toward generic-warm or generic-formal, and the recipient may need something specific to your relationship. Third, take responsibility for what the message commits you to. AI drafts sometimes promise things you cannot deliver — a refund timeframe you do not control, a follow-up date you have not blocked, a level of help you have not authorised. Those promises become yours the moment you press send.

### Learning responsibility

Module 8 taught you not to outsource your understanding to the AI. In Module 9, that responsibility extends to the learning your work shows on its face. If you ship AI-assisted analysis, the analysis represents what you understand. If you cannot explain a claim in your own report when asked, the claim should not have been in the report. *Defensible understanding* is a Module 8 idea that becomes a Module 9 rule: ship work you can defend without the AI in the room.

This is also the test that catches the worst silent failures. You can usually feel the difference between a sentence you understand and a sentence you only copied. The AI is fluent; fluent text is easy to ship without reading carefully. The simple discipline — *read every sentence as if you will be asked to defend it* — is enough to prevent most of the embarrassing cases.

### Bias and fairness responsibility

AI outputs can carry biases from their training data, from the prompts that produced them, and from the gaps in what they were optimised to do well. This shows up in small and large ways: gendered assumptions about roles, defaults that favour some languages or regions over others, examples that flatten cultural specifics, performance writing that praises some communication styles and downgrades others, candidate evaluations that subtly track non-job-relevant attributes, summaries that drop dissent and amplify majority views. None of this is a feature of the *task* you asked for. It is a feature of how the AI completes the task by default.

The accountability rule applies cleanly here. If the AI's draft contains a biased framing, you are the person who decides whether to ship it, edit it, or replace it. You cannot write *the AI was biased* on a performance review and walk away from it. The review is yours.

The practical habit is a short bias check before you accept an AI output for any work that touches a person. Three questions are enough most of the time. *Does this output assume something about the person — gender, age, language, region, role, ability — that is not actually given in the prompt?* *Does this output flatten or skip a perspective that should have been kept?* *Would this output read the same way if the person at the centre of it were someone else?* If any of the answers is concerning, edit before you ship. If you cannot tell, that is the moment to involve another person, especially someone closer to the person affected.

Bias is not always dramatic. The smaller cases — a tone that is slightly more formal for one demographic than another, a recommendation that shades toward a default the AI sees more often, a summary that omits the contribution of one team member — accumulate over many outputs. The bias check is a one-minute habit; the cumulative effect of skipping it is what causes the harm.

### Disclosure: required, wise, or unnecessary

Disclosure is how you tell a reader that AI was part of how a piece of work was made, what part it played, and what review you performed. The Course 1 disclosure standard (see Course1_Disclosure_Note.md) treats this as a normal professional habit. Module 9 needs you to make the call cleanly in three categories.

**Required.** Disclosure is not optional in five settings: course submissions for any Jifunze course, portfolio artifacts you publish, employer or client work (unless you have written confirmation that disclosure is not required), academic and assessment contexts (unless the institution explicitly permits unattributed AI use), and regulated or sensitive contexts including healthcare, legal, financial, HR, and child-welfare work. In these settings, silent AI use creates real risk to the reader, to you, or to a third party.

**Wise.** Disclosure is a judgment call but usually wise outside the required settings when (a) the work might be reused, forwarded, or republished later, (b) AI assistance changed something meaningful about the work — surfaced an idea you would not have reached, restructured an argument significantly, or generated a substantial portion of the language — or (c) the work is going to someone whose long-term trust matters more than the brief friction of a disclosure note. The test is simple: if disclosure now is cheaper than disclosure later, disclose.

**Unnecessary.** Disclosure is genuinely not required for personal-only work that no other person will rely on — your own to-do lists, your own draft notes, brainstorming you do for your own thinking. The audience for the work is you. There is no trust decision being made by another person. Adding a disclosure to your own grocery list is performative; skip it.

The disclosure note itself should be short and accurate. Course 1's standard template (Tool used / Task performed / Human review performed / What was changed / What you remain responsible for) is enough for almost every case. Avoid the four weak forms covered in Course1_Disclosure_Note.md: vague ("AI was used in the production of this work"), evasive ("AI may have been consulted"), minimising or overstating the role, and off-loading responsibility ("any errors are the AI's"). Each of those weakens trust rather than building it.

### Jurisdiction and organisational policy — without legal advice

Disclosure rules and AI use rules vary by jurisdiction and organisation. Kenya's Data Protection Act, the EU/UK GDPR area, and US sector-specific frameworks (HIPAA, FERPA, GLBA, COPPA, state-level privacy rules) all impose different obligations on different kinds of personal data. Schools, employers, professional bodies, and clients also have their own policies — sometimes written, sometimes unwritten. The course does not give legal advice. It does give one practical posture: when you do not know whether disclosure or restricted use applies, ask before you proceed, not after. *The absence of a clear yes is not a yes.* If your organisation has a position on AI use and you cannot find it, the question goes to your manager, your compliance contact, your IT lead, your school administration, or your professional body. Module 10 covers the privacy and operational side of this in depth.

### When to pause or escalate

Some tasks should not be solved by refining the prompt. They should be paused and raised. Pause and escalate when any of these is true.

The task is high stakes by the definition above and you do not know whether AI is permitted in your organisation for this content. The task touches an active legal matter, disciplinary process, regulatory investigation, or contractual dispute. The task involves a child, a patient, a victim of harm, or another person whose privacy or vulnerability carries extra weight. You are being asked to put AI in a decision-making role that should belong to a human — hiring, firing, medical diagnosis, credit decisions, disciplinary outcomes. You feel a small unease about the request and cannot articulate why. Unease is data; do not paste over it.

Escalation does not mean *give up*. It means raise the question to whoever in your organisation has the authority to answer it. Where no such function exists — common in small businesses and freelance work — the safer default is to do the task without AI involvement, or to do the AI-assist part on a tightly redacted version of the work and to keep the high-stakes decision in your own hands.

### Solo learner versus team or user context

Most of this module describes responsibility for your own work. The same logic applies in team and user-facing contexts, with one extra dimension: you are also responsible for the conditions you create for the people working with or under you. If a team uses AI without a shared guardrail set, the weakest practitioner sets the floor for the team's risk. If a customer uses an AI feature you built without seeing how to verify the output, you have shipped a tool that quietly bypasses verification.

For solo learners, the accountability statement (the portfolio artifact for this module) is written for one person — you. For learners working in a team, organisation, or user-facing context, write a second short version: a *team or user* paragraph that names the shared rules, the disclosure expectation, and the review responsibilities across the people involved. Module 14 will return to team-level rules in detail. The version you write here is the seed.

### How Module 9 prepares Module 10

Module 9 establishes accountability and practical guardrails. The next move is operational: applying those guardrails to privacy, risk, boundaries, and safe operational use. Module 10 takes the discipline you have just built and turns it into specific habits at the moment of pasting — what information classifies as safe, caution, restricted, or never-enter; how to redact and abstract sensitive content; what to do when an organisation does not yet have an AI policy; when to pause entirely. The accountability statement you write here becomes the front matter of the safer workflow you build there. You will not need to repeat the responsibility framing — you will *use* it.

## Fully written worked examples

### Worked example 1: Low-stakes AI use — a personal study plan

**Scenario.** Brenda is preparing for an internal qualification at her workplace. She wants to plan the next four weekends of study. The plan is for her own use; nobody else will see it.

**Prompt:**
> Help me draft a four-weekend study plan for an internal compliance certification. I have ten chapters to cover, two practice exams to take, and four weekends of two hours each on Saturday morning and one hour on Sunday evening. Spread the chapters and practice exams realistically and leave the last weekend lighter for revision.

**AI output (lightly trimmed for length):**
> Weekend 1: Chapters 1–3 (Saturday), Chapter 4 + practice questions (Sunday). Weekend 2: Chapters 5–7 (Saturday), Practice exam 1 + review (Sunday). Weekend 3: Chapters 8–10 (Saturday), Re-do weak topics from Practice exam 1 (Sunday). Weekend 4: Practice exam 2 (Saturday), Light revision and rest (Sunday).

**Why this is low stakes.** The plan is for Brenda. The cost of the plan being slightly off is that she rearranges her own time, which she controls. No third party is affected. No claim about a person or an organisation is being made. No money, health, or legal consequence depends on the schedule.

**Appropriate guardrails.**

A short read-through for sense — does the volume per weekend match Brenda's reading speed; does the practice-exam timing leave space to fix weak topics. A small adjustment if a weekend is over-scheduled. No verification of external claims is required because the plan does not contain any. No disclosure is required because the plan is personal. No bias check is required because no person is being described or evaluated.

**What the learner should notice.** The guardrails for a low-stakes task are short, but they are not absent. Even here, Brenda reads the output rather than blindly accepting it, and she keeps the right to adjust the plan. The Module 6 verification habit and the Module 7 voice-fit habit are not triggered, because the task does not call for them. That is appropriate. Stake-matched guardrails are the point.

### Worked example 2: Medium-stakes AI use — a workplace customer email

**Scenario.** Joseph runs a customer-service desk for a logistics company. He needs to write to a corporate customer, ABC Foods, whose pallet was delayed by two days and arrived with one of three crates damaged. The customer has complained politely but firmly. Joseph wants AI help drafting the reply.

**Prompt (Joseph has redacted identifying detail per Module 10's discipline, although that module formally arrives next):**
> Draft a short, warm, professional reply to a corporate logistics customer whose pallet arrived two days late, with one of three crates damaged. Acknowledge both issues, offer to refund the damaged-crate delivery fee, commit to redelivering replacement product within five business days, and ask the customer to confirm the redelivery address. Tone: accountable, plain English, not corporate. Length: under 150 words. Format: a single email body, no subject line.

**AI output:**
> Thank you for letting us know about the delay and the damaged crate on the recent delivery. We are sorry the consignment did not reach you in the condition or the timeframe we committed to. We will refund the delivery fee for the damaged crate and arrange replacement product to reach you within five business days. Please confirm the address you would like the replacement sent to and the best contact for the receiving team. We appreciate your patience and the chance to put this right. If anything else needs attention from our side, please tell us before the redelivery is dispatched.

**Verification, tone, and disclosure work.** Joseph reads the draft against three checks. He confirms the *five business days* commitment is one his operations team can actually meet — he asks them before he sends, because the AI does not know his team's capacity. He checks the *refund the delivery fee for the damaged crate* commitment against his refund authority — he can authorise this level on his own. He reads the message in the voice of his customer's procurement officer, who he knows prefers short messages without filler; he trims one sentence accordingly. He decides on disclosure: the email is direct customer correspondence, the customer has not asked about AI assistance, the wording is short and standard, and Joseph has reviewed and adjusted it. By the conventions of his industry and his employer's current AI policy (which he has checked), disclosure is not required for this kind of routine reply. He sends the email without a disclosure note in the message itself, but he records in his own log that AI helped draft this one — so that if anyone asks later, he can answer accurately.

**What the learner should notice.** Medium stakes pulled in the verification habit (Module 6), the audience-fit habit (Module 7), and a deliberate disclosure decision. None of these took long. The total time from prompt to send was probably ten minutes, most of it in the verification step. The accountability is explicit: Joseph is responsible for the five-day commitment, the refund, and the customer relationship. The AI helped phrase the message; it did not commit to anything. Joseph did.

### Worked example 3: High-stakes AI use — an HR disciplinary letter

**Scenario.** Faith works in HR. Her manager has asked her to "use AI to draft a disciplinary letter" for an employee who has missed three deadlines this quarter and been late to two scheduled meetings with a client. The employee has previously raised personal-health concerns informally.

**Why this is high stakes.** The letter will go on the employee's personnel record. It can affect the employee's role, reputation, and possibly continued employment. The previously raised health concerns mean any disciplinary framing has to be careful not to penalise an issue that might be a protected matter under workplace policy or law. Errors in a letter of this kind can be revisited in a grievance process, by a regulator, or in a tribunal. The cost of getting this wrong is borne by the employee first, by Faith and her manager second, and by the organisation third.

**Why "refining the prompt" is the wrong move.** The conventional Module 4 prompt-engineering approach — clearer role, tighter constraints, better output format — would produce a more fluent letter. It would not solve the real problem, which is whether AI should be involved in this letter at all. A fluent letter that subtly mishandles the employee's health context, or that uses standard disciplinary phrasing in a case where mitigating factors apply, is a worse outcome than no letter. The fluency hides the problem.

**What Faith should do instead.**

She pauses and escalates. She tells her manager that the case has features — past health disclosure, performance issues that may or may not be linked, formal-record consequences — that need (1) a check of the organisation's policy on AI use in HR documents (some organisations restrict it; many require human-only authorship for disciplinary content); (2) input from the employee or their representative if the policy requires it; (3) human authorship of the substantive judgments in the letter, even if AI is later allowed for tone or formatting; (4) review by a second HR person, and where appropriate by a manager outside the line of reporting. She does not paste the employee's name, the health context, or the dated incidents into a general AI tool while she waits for the policy answer (Module 10 will return to this exact discipline). If, after the policy check, AI assistance is permitted only for limited drafting of generic procedural language — for example, the section that describes the formal process — she uses it only for that, with the substantive content written by her and reviewed by a colleague.

**Accountability framing.** If the letter is later challenged, Faith and her manager are the accountable parties. *The AI drafted it* is not a defence and is not even a relevant fact about who is responsible. The accountable parties are the people who chose the words, signed the document, and authorised the action. That is true whether AI helped or not. The point of the pause-and-escalate move is to make sure the people in those roles do their part of the work directly, rather than via a fluent draft that obscures whose judgment shaped what.

**What the learner should notice.** High-stakes work is the situation where stronger prompt engineering is exactly the wrong tool. Better prompts do not fix the question of whether AI belongs in the workflow. The right move is to pause, check the policy, and move the substantive judgment back to the human who is accountable for it. Module 10 will give you the privacy companion to this rule. The accountability rule is the one Module 9 leaves you with.

### Worked example 4: Bias and accountability — a candidate evaluation summary

**Scenario.** Susan is a hiring manager. She has interviewed three candidates for a junior coordinator role. She has interview notes and a scoring rubric. She wants AI help producing a one-paragraph summary of each candidate to share with her colleague.

**Prompt:**
> I will paste my interview notes and rubric scores for three junior-coordinator candidates. Write one short paragraph per candidate summarising strengths, areas of concern, and a recommendation. Tone: factual, neutral, suitable for an internal hiring panel.

**AI output (excerpt, candidate two only):**
> *Candidate B (David)* came across as confident and well-spoken, with strong eye contact and a polished delivery. He answered the technical questions correctly and gave a clear example of leading a small team. There was a slight hesitation when discussing why he left his previous role, but he recovered quickly. He may need support adjusting to a more multicultural team environment but is otherwise a strong recommendation.

**What sounds correct.** The paragraph is fluent. It uses neutral-sounding professional language. It claims to summarise strengths and a concern. It ends with a recommendation. On a fast read, it looks like exactly what Susan asked for.

**What is wrong.**

*Unjustified attribute.* "Strong eye contact and a polished delivery" was not in the rubric and probably not in Susan's notes. The AI inferred it from the implication that the candidate was confident. Inference dressed as observation is a small but real form of fabrication.

*Coded language.* "May need support adjusting to a more multicultural team environment" is the kind of phrase that can carry a serious bias. Nothing in a typical interview note would justify this claim, and the language echoes a known pattern of subtly disadvantaging candidates from minority backgrounds in evaluations. If David is from any group that the AI's training has associated with this pattern, the sentence is doing harm independent of David's actual interview.

*Praise framing for "confident."* Confidence is a culturally specific signal. AI outputs frequently treat "well-spoken" and "confident" as virtues without examining whether the rubric measured them. If Susan's rubric did not include presentation style, the AI has rewarded an attribute the role does not require.

*Hidden recommendation.* "Otherwise a strong recommendation" is presented as a conclusion, but the paragraph has not actually traced any rubric score to support it. The hiring decision should rest on the rubric, not on the paragraph's own framing.

**Who is affected.** The candidate, primarily — David, in this case, and equivalent candidates whose evaluations get shaped this way. Susan's colleague, who will rely on the summary. Susan's organisation, which is exposed to fairness and equal-opportunity risk if hiring decisions track a biased framing. Other candidates in the pool, since David's elevated framing changes the comparative picture.

**Rewrite with accountability and review.**

Susan does three things. First, she discards the AI's paragraph as the basis of the summary; she keeps it only as a starting point for her own writing. Second, she rewrites, using only the rubric scores and her actual interview notes. Third, she adds explicit review notes to make her judgment auditable.

> *Candidate B (David).* Rubric score 4/5 on technical knowledge, 4/5 on communication of past examples, 3/5 on team-fit example, 3/5 on stated reason for leaving previous role. Strengths: correct answers on the two technical scenarios; specific example of coordinating a four-person project. Concern: the example given for handling a disagreement was thin; I will follow up with a reference if shortlisted. Recommendation: shortlist for second interview, alongside Candidate A. — *Notes: I drafted a first version using AI assistance and discarded it because it included claims (eye contact, polish, multicultural-environment adjustment) that were not in my rubric or my notes. The summary above is written from my notes against the rubric.*

**What the learner should notice.** The bias was not loud. It was a single sentence in an otherwise fluent paragraph, and the paragraph's overall recommendation sounded supportive. That is exactly why the bias check has to be a habit, not a special event. The accountability move is also visible: Susan does not write *the AI was biased* on the candidate's record. She writes the version *she* will defend, and she records what she did with the AI's output. *Recording what you did with the AI's output is itself part of the accountability.*

### Worked example 5: Jurisdiction-aware disclosure — three short scenarios

**Scenario A — academic submission.** Aisha is finishing her postgraduate dissertation at a Kenyan university. She has used AI to suggest restructuring of her introduction and to generate practice questions she used to test her own understanding before writing. The university's AI policy (which she has read) requires disclosure of AI use in academic submissions and requires that any text suggested by AI be rewritten in the student's own voice before inclusion. Aisha's institution does not provide a fixed template; it asks for a clear statement.

*Disclosure decision.* **Required.** Aisha includes a short note in her front matter naming the tool, the tasks AI assisted with (introduction restructuring suggestions; practice questions for self-testing), the review she performed (rewrote the suggested structure in her own words; verified all sources cited in the introduction against the originals), and the responsibility she retains (every claim, every citation, every conclusion in the dissertation is hers). She does not need legal advice for this; she needs accuracy. The university's policy is the rule.

**Scenario B — employer policy.** Brian works at a bank that has an explicit AI-use policy: AI is permitted on internal documents but only via the bank's approved internal tool, and any external-facing communication must disclose AI assistance to internal reviewers (not to the customer) before the document is finalised. Brian has used the approved tool to draft a standard internal credit-policy summary for his team.

*Disclosure decision.* **Required, internally.** Brian discloses AI use to his internal reviewer per the policy. He does not add an external disclosure to the customer-facing version, because the document is internal only. *He does not assume that the absence of an external-disclosure requirement means he can use a non-approved external tool* — the policy specifies the approved tool, and using a different tool would be a separate violation regardless of disclosure.

**Scenario C — client/workplace freelance work.** Cynthia is a freelance writer drafting a website-copy update for a small-business client. The client did not ask about AI use during scoping. Cynthia used AI to suggest three headline options and to draft a first version of the *About* page. She rewrote the *About* page in the client's voice based on prior conversations and chose one of the three headlines after some adjustment.

*Disclosure decision.* **Wise, even if not formally required.** The default for client work is to disclose unless the client has stated in writing that disclosure is not required. The client has not made such a statement, so Cynthia adds a one-line note in her delivery email: *"AI was used to suggest headline options and draft a first version of the About page. I rewrote the About page in your voice from our previous conversations and chose the final headline after testing two options. I am responsible for the accuracy and tone of every line as delivered."* If the client asks more or less detail later, Cynthia can adjust. The brief note in advance protects the relationship; the alternative — silent use, discovered later — would not.

**What ties the three scenarios together.** None of these decisions required legal advice. Each one applied the same logic: identify the rule that governs the context (institutional policy, employer policy, client convention), apply it cleanly, and write a short, accurate disclosure when one is needed. The case where Cynthia disclosed without being required to is the one most learners get wrong; *the absence of a clear yes is not a yes*. When in doubt, disclose, briefly and accurately, in advance.

**What the learner should notice.** Disclosure is rarely difficult once you have decided to do it. The hard work is in recognising when it is required, when it is wise, and when the absence of a rule should default toward disclosure rather than away from it. The Course1_Disclosure_Note.md template is the practical tool; this module is the practice that makes its use automatic.

## Answer key with explanations

**Question 1.** **Correct answer: C.** Human accountability for an AI-assisted output stays with the human who chose the task, wrote the prompt, reviewed the output, and shipped the result. The AI does not have a role, a relationship with the affected parties, or any way to be held to account. Options A and B treat AI as a co-author it cannot be; option D confuses *origin of wording* with *responsibility for consequence*. The two are different.

**Question 2.** **Correct answer: C.** A disciplinary letter added to a personnel record can affect the employee's role, reputation, and possibly their continued employment, and it is reviewable in formal processes. That makes it high stakes by the module's definition. The other options describe low- or medium-stakes uses where the cost of error is small or limited to internal logistics.

**Question 3.** **Strong-answer criteria.** A strong response notes that Sandra's post is medium stakes (it represents her business to customers), that the AI involvement was light and probably acceptable to leave undisclosed in a casual social-media post, but that two checks are still appropriate before publishing: a verification of the new opening hours against her actual schedule, and a brief read in the voice of her customers to make sure the tone fits. A response that says only "she should disclose AI use" misses that disclosure on a casual marketing post is not usually required and may be unnecessary; a response that says "everything is fine" misses the verification step that medium-stakes work requires.

**Question 4.** **Strong-answer criteria.** A strong response identifies this as a high-stakes task that should not be solved by refining the prompt. James should pause and escalate: he should not paste identifying employee detail into a general AI tool, he should attempt to reach a second senior contact (acting HR, his manager, compliance) to confirm the policy, and if the deadline cannot move, he should write the letter without AI assistance for the substantive content rather than risk a policy violation. A response that recommends "asking the AI to be careful" or that uses prompt-engineering as the answer does not meet the strong-answer bar.

**Question 5.** **Strong-answer criteria.** A strong response says, in its own words, that a guardrail is a small named rule applied before a task begins (not after). The reasoning for *before*, not *after*: under deadline or pressure, decisions made in advance survive; decisions delayed to the moment of action often do not. *Apology after failure* may be necessary but does not prevent the next failure; guardrails do.

**Question 6.** **Strong-answer criteria.** A strong response distinguishes the three cleanly. *Required* — a setting where silent AI use creates real risk and disclosure is not optional (course submissions, employer or client work without an explicit waiver, academic and assessment contexts, regulated or sensitive contexts). *Wise* — a setting where disclosure is a judgment call but the work might be reused, AI changed something meaningful, or long-term trust is at stake. *Unnecessary* — work that no other person will rely on; the audience is the writer themselves. Each example should be plausible (e.g., required: a Jifunze portfolio artifact; wise: a piece of freelance writing where the client did not specify; unnecessary: a personal grocery list). A response that conflates required with wise, or that gives no concrete examples, scores partial.

**Question 7.** **Strong-answer criteria.** A strong response identifies at least two of the following four problems: *energetic and articulate / friendly demeanour* are unjustified attributes drawn from a presentation style rather than the rubric; *paused briefly … recovered well* is inferred theatre, not rubric data; *cultural background may require some onboarding support* is the kind of coded language that can disadvantage candidates from minority backgrounds and is almost certainly unsupported by the actual rubric; the recommendation *otherwise a strong fit* presents itself as a conclusion without tracing it to rubric scores. The affected parties named should include the candidate (primarily), the reviewer relying on the summary, the organisation's hiring fairness, and the comparative position of other candidates. A defensible rewrite is along the lines of *Recommendation: shortlist for second interview against rubric scores X / Y / Z; review the example given on handling difficult colleagues with a reference if shortlisted.* A response that does not name affected parties, or that rewrites the paragraph without removing the unjustified attributes, scores partial.

**Question 8.** **Strong-answer criteria.** Specificity is the test. The task should be a real or realistic one (drafting a customer email about a refund; drafting a section of a CV; preparing study questions for an exam; summarising a meeting). The stake classification should match the task and be defended in one phrase. The three-line checklist should be concrete enough to be followed (e.g., *verify any date or figure against the source; read once in the recipient's voice before sending; do not paste names of clients into a general AI tool*) — not generic statements. The disclosure decision should be one of *required, wise, or unnecessary*, with a one-sentence reason that draws on the worked examples or the disclosure section. A response that is generic across all three parts does not meet the bar.
