# Module 15: Building Reusable AI Systems — Prompt Packs and Playbooks

## Continuity bridge from Module 14

Module 14 helped you move from individual AI use into shared AI practice. You learned to see shared AI use as a coordination problem rather than only a skill problem; to name the six coordination risks that turn skilled individual users into uneven team output; to design disclosure, review ownership, data boundaries, prompt-library rules, escalation, and approval at a *governance-lite* level a small team can actually maintain; to write a one-page team AI use agreement; to design shared prompts and shared templates that another person could pick up and use responsibly; and to do the same as a solo learner using a hypothetical four-person team. Module 15 takes the next step. Until now, every prompt you have written, every workflow you have designed, every review checklist you have built has been built for one task at a time. Worked example 4 in that module gave you a prompt-library *rule* (only tested prompts enter the library), but stopped short of showing you what a prompt-library *entry* looks like at full quality. Module 15 closes that gap. It teaches you to take the best prompts, workflows, standards, and review habits you have built across Course 1 and turn them into reusable assets — prompt packs and playbooks — that can support consistent, safe work over time, by you, by your team, by a future colleague, or by a client who has not seen the rest of your course work.

Carry forward seven things in particular. From Module 3, the discipline of prompt control: you, not the AI, decide what the prompt is for. From Module 4, structured prompt design: context, audience, format, and constraints supplied up front. From Module 5, iteration and comparison: a prompt that works once is a candidate; a prompt that works across several variations is the one you keep. From Module 10, privacy boundaries and minimum-necessary information: the rules you wrote about what does not enter an AI tool now belong inside every reusable asset, not in a separate document. From Module 12, workflow maps and SOPs: a playbook is an SOP with prompts inside it. From Module 13, decision ownership: the assets you build do not decide; they support a person who decides. From Module 14, team standards and shared prompt artifacts: every reusable asset you build is, in effect, a small piece of a team standard, even when the team is yourself working at different times.

The principle that holds this module together is short: *good AI use should become easier and more reliable over time, not because the tool got better, but because you wrote down what works.* Reusable assets are how the third Tuesday of doing a task is faster, safer, and more consistent than the first Tuesday — not because you remembered, but because you designed.

## Module purpose

Most early problems with AI use over time are not about a single prompt failing. The single prompt was fine. The problem is structural: a prompt that worked beautifully on Tuesday was forgotten by Friday and rewritten badly the next week; a workflow that depended on a careful sanitisation step was abbreviated under time pressure; a review checklist that was applied diligently the first three times was skipped the fourth; a colleague who would have been useful was given a prompt with no usage notes and produced something unrecognisable; a client asked the team how it handles AI use and the answer was *we are careful*, which is not an answer.

This module teaches the discipline that prevents those failures. You learn to see *reusability* as the design property that turns scattered AI skill into a working system; to identify which of your recurring tasks deserve a reusable asset and which do not; to write a prompt pack with the nine elements that distinguish a pack from a list; to write a playbook that turns a workflow into something a teammate or a future-you can run safely; to keep version notes that survive the tool changing and the team changing; to test a reusable asset against a fresh scenario before relying on it; and to know when to retire an asset that no longer fits. The result is AI work that compounds — work where the tenth time is faster, safer, and better than the first, because the design held — and a portfolio you can show a team, a client, or a senior reviewer with confidence.

## Core lesson

### One-time prompt versus reusable system

A prompt is a one-time instruction to an AI tool for a specific task in a specific context at a specific moment. *"Help me draft a thank-you message to a former lecturer who wrote me a recommendation letter — warm but brief, mentions the specific role I just got, no exaggeration"* is a one-time prompt. The context is unique, the task is unique, the audience is unique, and the prompt is shaped to that uniqueness. After the AI has produced a draft and you have edited it into a final message, the prompt has done its job. Saving it does not pay back; another former-lecturer message is unlikely enough that recreating the prompt from scratch when needed is faster than maintaining the saved version.

A reusable system is a prompt — or a sequence of prompts — designed to be picked up by you (later), by a teammate, or by a colleague who has not seen the rest of your work, and used safely on a class of tasks rather than a single instance. *"Draft a thank-you note for a Kenyan secondary-school student who has just completed an academic milestone"* is closer to a reusable system if it is built with explicit inputs (the student's name, the milestone, the relationship), explicit boundaries (no specific institutions named without verification, no claims about future success), explicit review criteria (under 100 words, warm tone, no marketing language, addresses the student directly), and explicit version notes (drafted by Wanjiru on 2026-03-12, revised on 2026-04-09 after the inconsistent-tone issue). A reusable system is a prompt that has been *designed for repeated use*, not just *used repeatedly*.

The difference is not the saving. A saved one-time prompt is still a one-time prompt. The difference is the design: a reusable system has been built with the structural elements (purpose, inputs, boundaries, review, version notes) that let it be used safely by someone who was not in the room when it was first written, on a task that is similar but not identical to the one it was first written for.

A useful test for whether a prompt is a candidate for becoming a reusable system: count how many times you have done the same shape of task in the last quarter. If the answer is *one*, the prompt is one-time. If the answer is *several, and I expect to do it several more times*, the prompt is a candidate. If the answer is *several, by several different people*, the prompt is more than a candidate — it is overdue.

### Why good AI use should become easier and more reliable over time

There is a quiet feature of skilled AI use that is worth naming: it should compound. The first time you write a recap of a meeting with AI's help, the work takes forty minutes (you write the prompt, edit the output, decide what to leave in and out, and check that no sensitive content slipped through). The fifth time, if you have learned nothing from the first four, the work still takes forty minutes. The fifth time, if you have written down what worked the first four times — the prompt that produced the right voice, the inputs that mattered, the things to check before sending — the work takes fifteen minutes, and the recap is more consistent across the five than the unaided versions would have been.

The forty-to-fifteen difference is not about the AI tool getting better. It is about you writing down what worked. Every reusable asset is a small bet that the next time you (or someone else) face this task, having the design ready will save effort and reduce risk. Most of those bets pay back. A few do not — the task changed, the tool changed, the team changed — and the asset is retired or rewritten. The discipline is in noticing which is which.

This is also why a learner finishing Course 1 with no reusable assets has done less than a learner finishing the same course with five thoughtful packs and one playbook. Both learners may be equally skilled in the moment; the second has built a base they can carry into work, into a team, into a client engagement, or into the next thing they study, where the first will be re-deriving the same patterns each time. The portfolio at the end of Course 1 is partly evidence of skill and partly evidence of accumulation.

### What belongs in a prompt pack

A working prompt pack is not a list of prompts. It is a small specification, with nine elements, that together let another person use the prompt safely.

**1. Purpose.** What this prompt is for, in one or two sentences, in the language a user would recognise. *"Drafts a short customer reply for a delivery-window change request, in our team's standard tone, when the order has not yet been dispatched."* Purpose is what tells a user whether the prompt is the right one for their task before they paste their inputs.

**2. Audience or user.** Who is meant to use this prompt. Sometimes it is everyone on a team; sometimes it is a specific role; sometimes it is the version of you working under time pressure six months from now. *"Customer-care associates handling routine queries"*, *"the part-time student-liaison drafting alumni outreach messages"*, *"me, when I am in a hurry"*. Naming the audience lets the prompt's tone, complexity, and review cues be calibrated to the right reader.

**3. Input requirements.** What the user must supply for the prompt to work. List them. *"Customer first name; original delivery window; requested new delivery window; whether the new window is feasible (yes/no/unclear); associate's first name; optional customer note."* If a required input is missing or unclear, the user should know to fill it in before running the prompt rather than letting the AI guess.

**4. Prompt text.** The actual prompt, written so it can be pasted into an AI tool with the user's inputs filled in. The prompt itself follows Module 4's structured-prompt-design discipline: context, audience, format, constraints, examples where useful, the task, and any explicit *do not* clauses. The prompt text is the central element of the pack but is only one of nine; a pack with a great prompt and weak surrounding structure is still a weak pack.

**5. Example use.** One worked example showing inputs and the expected output shape (not necessarily a single canonical answer, since AI output varies, but a representative example). The example does for the prompt what worked example 1 in Module 4 did for prompt design: it makes the abstract concrete. A user who is uncertain whether they have understood the prompt can compare their inputs and outputs against the example.

**6. Boundaries.** What the prompt is *not* for, and what to do instead in those cases. *"Do not use for refund requests (use the refund pack); do not use for already-dispatched orders (escalate to dispatch lead); do not use when the customer is angry, distressed, or threatening (the empathetic-reply path is human-only)."* Boundaries are not a polite footer; they are the protection that prevents a reusable asset from being used in a context it was not designed for.

**7. Review criteria.** What the user checks before treating the AI output as work the team can stand behind. The review criteria are the user's version of Module 6's verification discipline applied to *this* prompt's output. They are usually short — three to seven items — and concrete enough to check without ambiguity. *"Customer first name present and spelled correctly? Reply under 120 words? No promises beyond the window change? Tone matches our standard? Disclosure note present where required?"*

**8. Ownership.** The named role responsible for keeping the prompt pack current, for adding or retiring entries, and for being asked when a user is unsure whether the pack still applies. *"Owned by the customer-care lead; questions or proposed changes go to the lead first."* Ownership matters because a prompt pack with no owner drifts: members assume someone else is checking, until the day someone notices the pack is six months out of date.

**9. Version notes.** A small log at the top or bottom of the pack: name, date, version, what changed, why. *"v4, 2026-04-15. Replaced v3 after the inconsistent-tone issue raised in the team retro. Added the four-working-hour commit clause for the unclear-window case."* Version notes are what make a pack auditable. They also make retirement a recorded act rather than a quiet deletion.

A pack that has all nine of these elements is a working asset. A pack that has only the prompt text and a usage note is a starting draft. A pack that has only the prompt text is a list.

### What belongs in a playbook

A playbook is a workflow with prompts inside it — Module 12's SOP discipline applied to AI-supported work specifically. Where a prompt pack is the unit for a single task, a playbook is the unit for a sequence of tasks that run together. A playbook has eight elements.

**1. When to use it.** The conditions under which the playbook applies. *"Use this playbook for any weekly internal team recap. Do not use for board-facing recaps (a separate playbook applies); do not use for meetings involving safeguarding, HR, or active complaint matters (those are human-only and have their own process)."* The when-to-use is the playbook's outer boundary.

**2. Steps.** The named stages of the workflow, in order, with named owners per stage. This is Module 12's stages-and-owners discipline, unchanged. The steps are short — usually four to eight — and each one is a unit of work, not a single keystroke.

**3. Prompts.** The prompts used at the steps that involve AI. Each prompt is itself a prompt-pack entry (see the nine elements above) — usually pulled from the team's prompt library rather than written fresh inside the playbook. The playbook references the prompt by name and version (*"Use the Internal Recap Prompt v4"*) so the playbook does not have to be revised every time a prompt is updated.

**4. Review checkpoints.** Where in the workflow a defined review happens, who does it, and what they check. Module 12's review-point discipline applied to AI-assisted output specifically. Each checkpoint names what is being checked, by whom, and what happens if the check fails (return to drafter? escalate? hold the artifact?).

**5. Safety boundaries.** What does not enter an AI tool inside this workflow, what sanitisation is expected before any input is pasted, and which content categories trigger an immediate stop. Module 10's discipline written at the workflow level so it is consulted at the right moment, not at the end.

**6. Escalation rules.** What stops the playbook and routes the work to a higher review or a different process. Module 14's escalation triggers applied to this specific workflow. Escalation rules are short, specific, and not negotiable in the moment.

**7. Expected outputs.** What the playbook produces when it works correctly. *"A weekly internal recap, under 300 words, with priorities, decisions, actions, blockers, and customer-impacting issues, stored in the team recap folder, disclosed as AI-assisted, reviewed by the team lead, approved by the team lead."* The expected outputs let a user know whether the playbook ran end-to-end successfully.

**8. Failure signs.** What it looks like when the playbook is producing weak or unsafe output, so the user knows to stop, escalate, or fall back. *"Recap contains items not in the original notes (the AI invented an action); recap mentions a customer by name (sanitisation step was skipped); recap contains a recommendation (the prompt's no-recommendations rule was overridden); the disclosure line is missing; the review checklist was not run."* Failure signs are the playbook's early-warning system — the points at which the workflow is not yet wrong but is about to be.

A playbook that has all eight elements is a workflow another person can run. A playbook that has steps and prompts but no checkpoints, boundaries, escalation, expected outputs, or failure signs is a list of instructions, not a playbook.

### How earlier modules become the prompt foundation

Modules 3, 4, and 5 are the foundation of every prompt pack. Module 3 taught you that prompts are control: the instruction shapes the output. Module 4 taught you the structure of a strong prompt: context, audience, format, constraints, examples, the task, the *do not* clauses. Module 5 taught you to iterate and compare: a prompt that produces good output once is a candidate, a prompt that produces good output across three or four variations of the same task is the one that earns a place in a pack.

A prompt that has not been through the Module 5 discipline does not belong in a pack. It might still be a useful one-time prompt — those are valuable too — but the pack is for prompts that have been tested. The promotion criterion from Module 14's worked example 4 (a prompt enters the library only after it has been tested in real work three times) is the team-level expression of the Module 5 discipline. The solo-learner version is the same: before a prompt enters your personal pack, you have used it on at least three real instances of the task and confirmed it produces consistent, reviewable output.

### How Module 10 sets boundaries inside reusable assets

Every reusable asset has Module 10's privacy discipline written into it, in two places. First, in the *boundaries* element of the prompt pack: *what this prompt is not for* names the categories of input that should not enter the tool and what to do instead. Second, in the *safety boundaries* element of the playbook: a workflow-level boundary list that applies across all the steps. The two work together. The pack-level boundary protects the user from misusing the prompt; the playbook-level boundary protects the workflow from being run against the wrong inputs.

A reusable asset that does not encode Module 10's boundaries is more dangerous than the one-time prompt it replaced, because reusability multiplies the boundary failures. A one-time prompt that pastes a sensitive input is one mistake. A reusable prompt with no boundary that pastes a sensitive input is one mistake repeated by every user who picks up the prompt, until the boundary is added.

### How Module 12 supplies workflow and SOP structure

A playbook is, structurally, an SOP from Module 12 with prompts inside it. The eight playbook elements map directly onto Module 12's eight workflow elements:

| Module 12 workflow element | Module 15 playbook element |
|---|---|
| Stages | Steps |
| Sequence | (Implicit in steps) |
| Dependencies | (Implicit in steps and inputs) |
| Owners | (Named per step) |
| Review points | Review checkpoints |
| Constraints | Safety boundaries |
| Failure points | Failure signs |
| AI-assist decision | (Resolved per step; AI is in the steps where prompts appear) |

The mapping is intentional. A learner who has built a Module 12 SOP for a recurring task already has the structural skeleton of a playbook; the work of building the playbook is to identify where AI assists, write or pull the prompts, and add the failure signs. That is much less work than building a playbook from scratch, which is the design intent.

### How Module 13 supplies decision criteria

Reusable assets do not decide. Module 13 was clear about this: AI may support decisions; the decision remains human-owned. A prompt pack that drifts into deciding (an *auto-recommend* prompt, a prompt whose output is treated as the decision rather than as input to a person who decides) has misused the reusable form. A playbook that drifts into deciding (a workflow whose final step is *the AI's recommendation is acted on without explicit approval*) has misused the playbook form.

The protection is in the design. Every reusable asset that supports a decision should: (a) name the decision question explicitly inside the asset; (b) name the decision owner — the role responsible for making the call; (c) keep AI's role limited to the support functions Module 13 named (clarifying options, surfacing tradeoffs, listing risks, generating counterarguments, naming missing information); and (d) require a human approval step before the decision is acted on. The decision-support packs you may have built in your own work — comparison frames, criteria-builder prompts, counterargument prompts — are useful precisely because they are designed to support, not to decide.

### How Module 14 supplies shared standards

Module 14's team agreement is the wider envelope inside which the team's reusable assets live. The agreement names what the team uses AI for, what it does not, the disclosure rule, the data boundaries, the review and approval levels, the escalation triggers, and the prompt-library rules. A prompt pack added to the team's library is consistent with the agreement or it does not enter the library. A playbook designed by a team member references the agreement's data boundaries and escalation triggers rather than restating them. The team agreement is the source of truth; the assets implement it.

For a solo learner, the same logic applies with you as the team: your personal AI use standard (disclosure habits, data boundaries, review habits) is the envelope inside which your personal reusable assets live. The discipline transfers.

### Version control habits

A reusable asset that is not versioned drifts. Six months after a prompt was added to a pack, the team's voice has shifted, the underlying task has shifted, the AI tool has been updated, or the boundaries have been refined — and the prompt that worked in March produces output in October that nobody on the team would write themselves. Version notes are how this is managed.

A small version-control discipline that scales from solo learner to small team:

- **Name.** Each asset has a name that identifies its purpose at a glance. *"Customer Reply — Delivery-Window Change"*, *"Internal Recap Prompt"*, *"Donor Report Playbook"*. Names are short, descriptive, and unique within the asset library.
- **Date.** The date the current version was finalised, in `YYYY-MM-DD` format so it sorts cleanly. *"2026-04-15"*.
- **Version.** A simple version number (`v1`, `v2`, `v3`). For most small teams and solo learners, semantic versioning (`v1.2.3`) is overkill; a single number is enough.
- **What changed.** One or two sentences describing what was different from the previous version. *"Added the four-working-hour commit clause for the unclear-window case. Added an escalation trigger for distressed customers."*
- **Why it changed.** One sentence saying what prompted the change. *"The previous version produced inconsistent replies in unclear cases, which the team retro flagged in April."*
- **When to retire.** Each asset should have a retirement test — the conditions under which it should be removed from the library or rewritten. Common retirement tests: *the underlying task has changed* (the team no longer handles delivery-window changes that way), *the tool has changed in a way that breaks the prompt's behaviour*, *the boundaries are no longer adequate* (a new sensitive input category has emerged), *no one has used it in six months* (the task may not have recurred; consider whether it should be in the library at all), *a better version exists* (version notes capture the replacement).

The retirement test matters because libraries that only grow eventually become unreadable. A prompt library of fifty entries, of which only fifteen are current, is harder to use than a library of fifteen entries, all of which are current. Retirement is not failure; it is maintenance.

### Quality tests for reusable assets

Four tests, applied before an asset enters the library, separate working assets from drafts. None of the four can be skipped without weakening the asset.

**Test 1: Could another person use this without explanation?** Hand the asset (the pack or the playbook) to a colleague — real or imagined — who has not seen the rest of your work. Could they read it, pick it up, and use it on a real task without asking you a clarifying question? If yes, the asset's structure is adequate. If no, identify the part that triggered the question and revise it. The most common failure: the prompt assumes context only the original author has, and the user has no way to supply that context from the inputs the pack lists.

**Test 2: Does it work on a fresh scenario?** Build the asset against one task. Test it against a different instance of the same task — different inputs, different specific context, same shape. Does the output meet the review criteria? If yes, the asset is generalising. If no, the asset is tied too tightly to its first scenario, and the prompt or the boundaries need to be loosened or rewritten. Activity 4 in this module is designed around this test specifically.

**Test 3: Does it preserve safety and review?** Read the asset against Module 10's privacy discipline (does it block sensitive inputs?), Module 12's review-points discipline (does it require a defined human check?), Module 13's decision-ownership discipline (does it support, not decide?), and Module 14's team-standards discipline (is it consistent with the agreement?). If all four are intact, the asset is safe to share. If any of the four is missing or weak, the asset is not yet ready, regardless of how good the prompt itself is.

**Test 4: Does it produce reviewable output?** When the asset is run, can the output be reviewed against the review criteria? Reviewable output is concrete, structured, and bounded. Output that is fluent, vague, and hard to fact-check fails this test. The fix is usually in the prompt: add structure (numbered sections, named fields, length limits), constrain the format (a table, a checklist, a short list), and prohibit the patterns that make AI output hard to review (open-ended recommendations, claims with no source, generic praise).

Asset that passes all four tests enters the library. Asset that fails any test stays as a personal draft until it passes.

### When not to make something reusable

Not every recurring task deserves a reusable asset. Three patterns suggest staying with one-time prompts.

**The task is recurring but the context is unique each time.** A condolence message is recurring in the sense that you may write several over a year, but each one has a unique relationship, a unique person, and a unique context that makes a structured prompt pack feel hollow. The prompt is shaped to the moment. A pack would either be too generic to be useful or too specific to fit the next case. Stay with one-time prompts.

**The task is recurring but rare enough that a pack would go stale.** A task that comes up twice a year, with significant variation between instances, may not earn the maintenance overhead of a reusable asset. By the time the second instance arrives, the asset is six months out of date and you would write a fresh prompt anyway. The maintenance cost outweighs the reuse benefit.

**The task is recurring but the right answer is human, not AI-assisted.** Some tasks recur often and could in principle be packaged, but the right shape is *the human writes it from scratch each time, with care*: a personal note to a long-standing customer, a sensitive conversation with a colleague, a public statement during a crisis. A pack would either be misused (the user runs it on the wrong context) or unused (the user does the right thing each time and the pack sits idle). Module 14's *human-only* discipline applies: name the task as not-for-pack and stop.

The discipline of *not packaging* is the same kind of discipline as *not automating* in Module 12. The point is not to package everything; the point is to package the things that pay back.

### Worked example 2: Study-support toolkit

This toolkit is a small reusable asset for personal learning. It pulls together prompts a learner can use across study tasks — explaining a concept, doing retrieval practice, repairing a weak area — with an evidence-of-learning check at the end.

> **Toolkit name.** Study Support — Four-Prompt Toolkit (v1, 2026-04-02).
>
> **Owner.** Me (the learner).
>
> **Purpose.** Supports my self-directed learning across any topic by providing four reusable prompts: explanation, retrieval practice, weak-area repair, and evidence of learning.
>
> **Audience.** Me, while studying. Adaptable to a study group if the *audience* line in each prompt is broadened.
>
> ---
>
> **Prompt 1 — Explanation.**
>
> *Input requirements.* Topic; my current understanding (one or two sentences in my own words); my background level (beginner, intermediate, advanced); preferred analogy domain (everyday life, sport, cooking, business — pick one).
>
> *Prompt text.*
>
> *Explain the concept below to me at a [level] level. Start with what the concept is (one paragraph), then explain why it matters (one paragraph), then give one analogy from [domain] that illuminates the concept without simplifying it past usefulness. End with one sentence I can quote back to a friend who has not heard of the concept. Use plain English. Do not flatter my current understanding; if my one-sentence summary is wrong or incomplete, say so directly and then proceed.*
>
> *Topic: [topic]. My current understanding: [summary]. Level: [level]. Domain: [domain].*
>
> *Review criteria.* The explanation matches my level (not too simple, not too dense); the analogy is illuminating, not just decorative; the closing sentence is genuinely quotable; if my summary was wrong, the AI said so explicitly.
>
> *Boundaries.* Not for medical, legal, or financial topics where the explanation might be relied on for action — those need a qualified human source.
>
> ---
>
> **Prompt 2 — Retrieval practice.**
>
> *Input requirements.* Topic; the concepts I want to be tested on (a list of three to seven); difficulty level (recall, application, synthesis).
>
> *Prompt text.*
>
> *Generate [number] retrieval-practice questions on the topic and concepts below, at the [difficulty] level. The questions should test my understanding without being trick questions. Provide the questions only — do not include answers in the same response. After I send my answers, you will mark them and explain any errors.*
>
> *Topic: [topic]. Concepts: [list]. Difficulty: [level]. Number of questions: [number].*
>
> *Review criteria.* Questions match the concepts I named; questions match the difficulty level (recall is recall; application asks me to use the concept; synthesis asks me to combine concepts); answer-marking, when received, explains why an answer is wrong rather than just marking it.
>
> *Boundaries.* Not for high-stakes assessments (the AI's marking is not authoritative). Not for content where the AI is likely to hallucinate (very recent events, niche regional content) — verify before relying.
>
> ---
>
> **Prompt 3 — Weak-area repair.**
>
> *Input requirements.* The weak area (concept I keep getting wrong); two or three of my recent wrong answers (with my reasoning); the source of the correct answer (a textbook chapter, lecture note, article).
>
> *Prompt text.*
>
> *I have been getting questions on [weak area] wrong. Below are two or three of my recent wrong answers, my reasoning at the time, and the correct answer. Diagnose the pattern in my wrong reasoning, name the misconception in plain language, and design two short retrieval-practice questions that target the misconception. Do not flatter my reasoning; if I had a fundamental misunderstanding, say so directly. Refer to the correct-answer source rather than re-deriving from scratch.*
>
> *Weak area: [area]. Wrong answers and reasoning: [pasted]. Correct answer source: [source].*
>
> *Review criteria.* The diagnosed pattern feels recognisable when I read it; the misconception is named in language I can repeat to myself; the two new questions target the misconception, not the surface form of the wrong answers.
>
> *Boundaries.* Same as Prompt 2.
>
> ---
>
> **Prompt 4 — Evidence-of-learning check.**
>
> *Input requirements.* The topic; the concepts I have been studying; the period (e.g., this week's study).
>
> *Prompt text.*
>
> *Help me check whether I have actually learned [topic] this [period], not just exposed myself to it. Ask me three to five short open-ended questions whose answers would only be available to someone who has integrated the concepts. Do not give me multiple-choice questions. After I answer, score me against this rubric: 'integrated' (I can use the concept to address a new situation), 'recognised' (I know what it means but cannot use it), 'unfamiliar' (I cannot answer or I confuse it with another concept).*
>
> *Topic: [topic]. Concepts: [list]. Period: [period].*
>
> *Review criteria.* The questions are open-ended; the rubric distinguishes integrated from recognised in a way I can self-check; the score, when given, includes one sentence explaining what would move me from one rubric level to the next.
>
> *Boundaries.* The score is the AI's reading of my answers; treat it as feedback, not a grade.
>
> ---
>
> **Toolkit-level boundaries.** None of the four prompts replaces a qualified human teacher, mentor, or assessor for high-stakes learning (academic credit, professional certification, regulated topics).
>
> **Toolkit-level review criteria.** A study session that ran the toolkit end-to-end produces: an explanation I understood, retrieval-practice questions I attempted, a weak-area diagnosis I recognised, and an evidence-of-learning score I can act on. If any of the four feels hollow, that prompt's boundaries or inputs need to be revised.
>
> **Version notes.** v1, 2026-04-02. Initial version, used across one week of study on workflow design.
>
> **Retirement test.** Retire any prompt that consistently produces output that does not pass its review criteria across three uses; rewrite rather than retain.

**What the learner should notice.** This toolkit is for personal use, not for a team, but the structural discipline is the same. Each prompt has inputs, prompt text, review criteria, and boundaries. The toolkit-level elements protect the user from over-relying on the toolkit in contexts it was not designed for. The evidence-of-learning prompt explicitly distinguishes *recognition* from *integration* — a discipline more useful to a self-learner than a generic *do you understand?* check.

### Worked example 3: Team recap playbook

This playbook turns Module 14's worked example 1 — the team meeting recap workflow — into a reusable asset with the full eight-element playbook structure.

> **Playbook name.** Weekly Internal Team Recap Playbook (v3, 2026-04-22).
>
> **Owner.** The team lead. Proposed changes go to the team lead first.
>
> **When to use it.** Use for any weekly internal team coordination meeting recap. Do not use for board-facing recaps (a separate playbook applies). Do not use for meetings involving safeguarding, HR, or active-complaint matters (those are human-only and follow a separate process).
>
> **Steps.**
>
> 1. *Take meeting notes during the call.* Owner: associate on rota. Output: raw notes.
> 2. *Sanitise notes.* Owner: associate on rota. Replace customer names attached to issues with placeholders (Customer-A, Customer-B). Remove HR, salary, or board-confidential references. Output: sanitised notes.
> 3. *Draft AI-assisted recap.* Owner: associate on rota. Use the *Internal Recap Prompt v4* from the team prompt library, with the sanitised notes as input. Output: AI-drafted recap.
> 4. *Run the review checklist.* Owner: team lead (or, in their absence, the senior associate). Run the seven-item review checklist (see *Review checkpoints* below). Output: reviewed recap or returned-to-drafter recap.
> 5. *Approve for circulation.* Owner: team lead. Output: approved recap.
> 6. *Store and disclose.* Owner: associate on rota. Save the final recap in the team recap folder with the filename `Recap_YYYY-MM-DD.md`. Append the disclosure line: *AI-assisted draft from sanitised notes; reviewed by [name]; approved by [name].* Output: stored, disclosed recap.
>
> **Prompts.** Internal Recap Prompt v4 (from team prompt library; pack entry at [shared folder]/prompt-library/internal-recap-prompt-v4.md).
>
> **Review checkpoints.**
>
> *Step 4 review (run by team lead before approval):*
>
> 1. Are the priorities, decisions, and actions all present in the original notes? (No invented items.)
> 2. Are role names used correctly, and are no individual employee names present that should not be?
> 3. Are customer placeholders preserved (Customer-A, Customer-B), with no real customer names visible?
> 4. Are commitments to customers, partners, or external parties absent? (The recap should not contain promises; those go through a different workflow.)
> 5. Are HR, salary, performance, or disciplinary references absent?
> 6. Is the disclosure line present at the bottom?
> 7. Is the recap under 300 words and aligned to the team's standard structure?
>
> Failure of any check returns the recap to the drafter (associate on rota) for correction. Two consecutive failures of the same check trigger a prompt-pack review (the prompt may need to be tightened).
>
> **Safety boundaries.** No real customer names attached to specific issues; no supplier rates or commercial terms; no HR content (performance, salary, disciplinary); no commitments to external parties; no content the AI added that was not in the sanitised notes. Sanitisation step (step 2) is the boundary enforcement; if it cannot be completed without losing the substance of the recap, the recap is written by hand instead.
>
> **Escalation rules.** Stop and escalate to the team lead when: the AI output contains a claim about a real person or customer not in the sanitised notes; the prompt would require pasting Tier 3 content; the AI proposes an action that would commit the team beyond our published terms; the recap is being prepared during an active complaint or regulator interaction.
>
> **Expected outputs.** A weekly internal recap, under 300 words, with priorities, decisions, actions (with role-based owners), blockers, and customer-impacting issues at a high level, stored in the team recap folder, disclosed as AI-assisted, reviewed by the team lead, approved by the team lead, circulated to the small leadership group on Wednesday afternoon.
>
> **Failure signs.** Recap contains items not in the original notes (the AI invented an action or a decision); recap mentions a customer by name (the sanitisation step was skipped); recap contains a recommendation (the prompt's no-recommendations rule was overridden by a follow-up prompt); the disclosure line is missing; the review checklist was not run (visible because no reviewer name appears on the disclosure line); a recap arrives Friday or later (the workflow is being run late, which often correlates with shortcuts).
>
> **Version notes.** v3, 2026-04-22. Added the *no real customer names not in the sanitised notes* check to step 4 after a near-miss in April. v2, 2026-02-15. Added the failure sign about late-arriving recaps after the team retro. v1, 2026-01-08. Initial version.
>
> **Retirement test.** Retire if: the team's weekly meeting structure changes substantially (e.g., the meeting becomes monthly); a different workflow takes over the recap function; the prompt library's Internal Recap Prompt is retired without replacement; or no recap has been generated by the playbook in eight weeks (which would suggest the meeting is not happening or the playbook is being bypassed — investigate).

**What the learner should notice.** The playbook references the prompt by name and version (*Internal Recap Prompt v4*) rather than embedding the prompt inline; this means a prompt update does not require a playbook update, and the prompt and the playbook can be versioned independently. The review checkpoints are the same seven checks from Module 14's worked example 1, now formalised into the playbook structure. The escalation rules and safety boundaries pull directly from the team agreement (Module 14) without restating it. The failure signs are an early-warning system, not an after-the-fact post-mortem. The retirement test names specific conditions, not vague *when no longer useful*.

### Worked example 4: Weak reusable prompt that is too vague to scale

A learner builds the prompt below after writing a successful customer reply for one situation, and adds it to their personal pack as a reusable prompt.

> **The weak prompt (v1).**
>
> *Help me write a polite customer reply.*

The first time the learner uses this prompt, with a routine delivery-window query in their head, the reply they get is fine. The customer's name is obvious from the conversation context, the situation is simple, the AI's default polite reply matches the case adequately, and the learner ships it after light editing.

The second time, two weeks later, the learner uses the same prompt for a refund-request reply. The output is also polite, but generic, with no acknowledgement of the refund, no commitment to a timeline, no escalation hint, and a closing line that sounds like a customer-service template from somewhere else. The learner edits heavily, ships the reply, and notices that the prompt did almost no work.

The third time, the learner uses the prompt for a reply to a customer complaint that is starting to escalate. The output is *very* polite, including phrases the team would not use (*we sincerely apologise for the inconvenience caused*), promises the learner cannot keep (*we will resolve this immediately*), and a tone that, in this context, sounds dismissive rather than caring. The learner does not ship the reply, rewrites it from scratch, and concludes the prompt is not reusable.

**Diagnosis.** The prompt is too vague to scale. *Help me write a polite customer reply* gives the AI almost nothing structural: no audience, no input requirements, no length constraint, no tone definition beyond *polite*, no scope (which kinds of customer replies?), no boundaries (what should the reply not promise?), no review criteria, no version notes. It worked the first time because the situation it was used in did not require the prompt to do much work — the AI's defaults happened to fit. It failed the next two times because the situations differed and the prompt had no structure to absorb the difference.

The shape of the failure matches a pattern worth naming: a prompt that works once is *consistent with* the situation it worked in; a prompt that scales is *responsive to* a class of situations. The vague prompt was the first; it could not become the second without redesign.

**The rewritten prompt (v2).** The learner rewrites the prompt with the full nine-element pack discipline. The pack now has three reply prompts (delivery-window change, refund request, complaint escalation), each with the structure shown in worked example 1.

> **Pack name.** Customer Reply Pack (v2, 2026-04-12).
>
> **Owner.** The learner (personal pack).
>
> **Purpose.** Drafts customer replies for three recurring shapes — delivery-window change, refund request, complaint escalation — at consistent quality and tone.
>
> *(Each of the three prompts then includes its own input requirements, prompt text, example use, review criteria, and boundaries — following the structure of worked example 1.)*
>
> **Pack-level boundaries.** No customer name pasted with an account number, transaction ID, or financial detail. Sanitise the case to its shape before pasting, or do not use AI for the task.
>
> **Version notes.** v2, 2026-04-12. Replaced v1 (a single vague *help me write a polite reply* prompt that did not scale across reply shapes). Split into three prompts, each shaped to its reply class. v1, 2026-03-29. Initial single-prompt version, retired after failing on two of three test scenarios.

**What the learner should notice.** The first prompt was not a reusable system at all; it was a one-time prompt that was saved and re-used. The rewrite is what reusability looks like: structure, scope, boundaries, review criteria, and version notes that capture *why* the rewrite was needed. The retirement of v1 is a recorded act, not a quiet deletion. A learner reading the version notes six months from now knows what changed and why — a colleague reading them knows the pack is on its second iteration and what the first iteration's failure mode was.

## Checkpoint quiz

Answer all eight questions. Mark your own answers using the answer key that follows. Pass threshold: at least 6 of 8 questions correct (substantively correct per strong-answer criteria where rubric-graded).

**1. (Multiple choice.)** Which of the following best describes the difference between a one-time prompt and a reusable system?

a) A reusable system is a prompt that has been saved; a one-time prompt is a prompt that was not saved.
b) A reusable system has been designed for repeated use across a class of similar tasks, with explicit inputs, boundaries, review criteria, and version notes; a one-time prompt is shaped to a single task in a single context and is not designed for reuse.
c) A reusable system uses a more advanced AI tool than a one-time prompt.
d) A one-time prompt is shorter than a reusable system.

**2. (Multiple choice.)** A team adds a prompt to its prompt library that has been used successfully once. After two months, the prompt is producing inconsistent output for several team members. Which of the following is the most likely root cause?

a) The AI tool was updated, breaking the prompt.
b) The prompt was added to the library before being tested across multiple variations of the task — it had not earned its place under the Module 5 iteration discipline or the Module 14 promotion criterion.
c) The team members who used the prompt are not skilled enough.
d) The prompt is too long.

**3. (Scenario-based.)** A learner has built a personal prompt pack with three prompts. Each prompt has clear text and a usage note. There are no boundaries, no review criteria, no input requirements, and no version notes. The learner believes the pack is complete. What is missing, and why does each missing element matter?

a) Nothing is missing; usage notes are enough.
b) Boundaries (so the prompt is not used on tasks it was not designed for), review criteria (so the output can be checked against a defined standard), input requirements (so the user knows what to supply), and version notes (so the pack is auditable when used later by the same person or by a colleague).
c) Only version notes are missing.
d) Only boundaries are missing.

**4. (Scenario-based.)** A team has built a playbook with steps, prompts, and review checkpoints. The playbook does not include safety boundaries, escalation rules, expected outputs, or failure signs. The team plans to use the playbook for AI-assisted donor reports. What is the most likely failure mode?

a) The playbook will produce reports that are too long.
b) The playbook will run against inputs it should not (no safety boundaries), the team will not know when to stop or escalate (no escalation rules), there will be no shared definition of what success looks like (no expected outputs), and the team will not catch problems early (no failure signs) — together these gaps make the playbook a list of instructions, not a workflow another person can run safely.
c) The playbook will not produce reports at all.
d) The reports will be too short.

**5. (Short answer.)** In your own words, why does it matter to include version notes (name, date, version, what changed, why) in every reusable asset? Give one concrete example of how version notes prevent a problem.

**6. (Short answer.)** Name two of the four quality tests for a reusable asset, and explain in one sentence each why a reusable asset that fails that test should not enter a prompt library.

**7. (Application.)** Take any recurring task you (or a hypothetical four-person team) do regularly. Write the *boundaries* and *review criteria* sections of a prompt pack for that task: at least three boundaries and at least four review criteria. Both should be specific to the task, not generic.

**8. (Application — when not to make something reusable.)** Name one task — from your own life, your work, or a hypothetical team — that recurs but should *not* be made into a reusable asset. Explain in two or three sentences which of the three patterns from the core lesson applies (recurring-but-unique-each-time, recurring-but-rare, recurring-but-human-only) and why.

## Answer key with explanations

**1. Correct answer: (b).** The substance of the distinction is the design, not the saving (a), the tool (c), or the length (d). A reusable system has been designed with explicit inputs, boundaries, review criteria, and version notes that let it be used safely by someone who was not in the room when it was first written. Saving a one-time prompt does not turn it into a reusable system.

**2. Correct answer: (b).** Tool updates (a) and length (d) are possible secondary causes, but the most likely root cause when a prompt produces inconsistent output across team members shortly after being added to the library is that it was added before being tested across variations — exactly the failure mode the Module 5 iteration discipline and the Module 14 promotion criterion (test in real work three times before adding) are designed to prevent.

**3. Correct answer: (b).** All four elements are missing. Boundaries protect the prompt from misuse on tasks it was not designed for. Review criteria make the output checkable against a defined standard. Input requirements tell the user what to supply. Version notes make the pack auditable. A pack with prompt text and usage notes only is a starting draft, not a working pack.

**4. Correct answer: (b).** Each of the four missing elements names a real failure mode. Without safety boundaries, sensitive inputs may enter the workflow. Without escalation rules, members will not know when to stop or route up. Without expected outputs, *success* has no definition. Without failure signs, problems will not be caught until after they have caused harm. The playbook is structurally a list of instructions, not a workflow another person can run safely.

**5. Strong-answer criteria.** A strong answer makes three points. First, version notes record *what changed and why*, which makes the asset auditable when used later. Second, version notes are how retirement and replacement become recorded acts rather than quiet deletions. Third, version notes are what let a colleague reading the pack know the pack is on its second or third iteration and what the previous failure modes were. The example should be concrete: e.g., *"v3, 2026-04-22 added the no-real-customer-names check after a near-miss in April; without that note, a colleague reading the pack would not know why the check is there and might remove it as redundant."* Generic answers (*it helps with organization*) do not meet the criterion.

**6. Strong-answer criteria.** A strong answer names two of the four tests (could another person use this without explanation? does it work on a fresh scenario? does it preserve safety and review? does it produce reviewable output?) and explains in one sentence each why failing the test means the asset is not yet ready. *Could another person use this without explanation?* — failing means the asset assumes context the user does not have, so it will be misused. *Fresh scenario?* — failing means the asset is tied to its first scenario and will not generalise. *Safety and review?* — failing means the asset multiplies privacy or accountability failures across every reuse. *Reviewable output?* — failing means users cannot tell whether the output is good, which collapses the review discipline.

**7. Strong-answer criteria.** A strong answer names a specific recurring task (not a generic *write emails*), then writes:

*Boundaries (at least three, specific to the task).* Each boundary is a *do not use this for X — do Y instead* sentence. Strong examples for, say, a customer-reply pack: *Do not use for already-dispatched orders (escalate to dispatch lead). Do not use for replies to angry, distressed, or threatening customers (the empathetic-reply path is human-only). Do not use for refund requests above the policy threshold (use the senior-approval workflow).* Weak boundaries are vague (*do not misuse the prompt*).

*Review criteria (at least four, specific to the task).* Each criterion is a checkable item. Strong examples: *Recipient first name present and spelled correctly? Reply under 120 words? No promises beyond the window change? Tone matches our standard? Disclosure note present where required?* Weak review criteria are vague (*is the reply good?*).

**8. Strong-answer criteria.** A strong answer names a specific task, identifies which of the three patterns applies, and explains why in language a colleague would recognise. Strong examples: *(recurring-but-unique-each-time)* a condolence message — the relationship and the specifics make a structured pack feel hollow; *(recurring-but-rare)* a public statement during a crisis that happens once or twice a year — by the time the next one arrives, any pack will be stale; *(recurring-but-human-only)* a personal performance-feedback message to a long-standing direct report — the right answer is human-written care, not a templated assist. Weak answers pick a task that obviously *should* be packaged and try to argue against it.

## Revision guidance

If your prompt pack is just a list of prompts — prompt text, perhaps a short purpose line, nothing else — return to the core-lesson section *What belongs in a prompt pack* and to worked example 1. Add the missing elements (input requirements, example use, boundaries, review criteria, ownership, version notes) one by one. The pack is not a list with a header; it is a small specification.

If the purpose is unclear — *helps with writing* or *for customer replies* without a tighter description — return to the *purpose* element and to worked example 1. The purpose should answer *what is this prompt for?* in one or two sentences a user could recognise, with enough specificity that the user knows whether the prompt is the right one for their task before they paste their inputs.

If input requirements are missing — the user is expected to know what to supply without being told — return to the *input requirements* element and to worked examples 1 and 2. List the inputs explicitly. If an input is optional, mark it. If an input is required, say so. The user should not have to guess what the prompt needs to work.

If boundaries are missing — the pack does not say *what is this prompt not for?* — return to the *boundaries* element and to worked example 1. Add at least three boundaries per prompt, each one a *do not use this for X — do Y instead* sentence. Apply Module 10's privacy discipline; the pack should refuse the inputs that should not enter an AI tool.

If review criteria are vague — *is the reply good?* — return to the *review criteria* element. Replace the vague items with concrete, checkable items. *Recipient first name present and spelled correctly? Reply under 120 words? No commitments beyond the window change?* Concrete review criteria turn into a checklist a user can run; vague ones do not.

If version notes are absent — there is no name, date, version, what changed, why — return to the *version control habits* section. Add the version notes block to every prompt and to the pack as a whole. Even v1 of a brand-new pack should have a version note (*v1, 2026-04-29. Initial version.*). The discipline is in the recording, not in the version number.

If the pack failed on a fresh scenario in your test log and no revision was made — return to activity 4's discipline and to worked example 4. Decide whether the change belongs in the pack going forward or whether the failure is a one-time adaptation. Record the decision in version notes. A pack that fails a fresh-scenario test and is not revised is not yet ready for the library.

If another person could not use the pack without explanation — return to quality test 1. Hand the pack (literally or in your head) to a colleague who has not seen the rest of your work. Identify the part that triggered the question they would ask. Revise that part. Repeat until the pack reads as standalone.

If the artifact ignores Module 10 safety boundaries or Module 14 shared-use standards — the pack pastes inputs that should not enter the tool, or the playbook does not align with the team's agreement — return to Module 10 and Module 14. The pack and the playbook must encode Module 10's privacy discipline at the boundary level and align with Module 14's team agreement at the standards level. A reusable asset that violates either of those modules' disciplines is more dangerous than the one-time prompt it replaced, because reusability multiplies the failure.
