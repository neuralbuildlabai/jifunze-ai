# Module 16: Capstone — End-to-End AI-Supported Workflow

## Continuity bridge from Module 15

Module 15 helped you package what works. You took the strongest prompts, workflows, boundaries, review criteria, and version notes you had built across Course 1 and turned them into reusable assets — a mini prompt pack with all nine elements (purpose, audience, input requirements, prompt text, example use, boundaries, review criteria, ownership, version notes), a playbook with all eight (when to use, steps, prompts, review checkpoints, safety boundaries, escalation rules, expected outputs, failure signs), and a test log that proved the pack held up on a fresh scenario. By the end of the module, you had a working toolkit: not a list of prompts saved in a Notion page, but a small specification another person could pick up and use safely on a class of tasks similar to but not identical to the one it was first built against.

Module 16 now asks you to use that toolkit. Until now, the course has trained the parts: a prompt rewrite in Module 3, a structured prompt template in Module 4, an iteration log in Module 5, a verification table in Module 6, an audience-fit rewrite in Module 7, a teach-back in Module 8, an accountability statement in Module 9, a privacy and safety checklist in Module 10, a synthesis brief in Module 11, a workflow SOP in Module 12, a decision memo in Module 13, a team agreement (or its solo equivalent) in Module 14, and the prompt pack and playbook in Module 15. Each artifact tested one discipline. The capstone tests whether the disciplines hold together when run end-to-end on one realistic task you actually do — or could plausibly do — with the same toolkit you just packaged.

The capstone should show that you can do six things in one continuous piece of work: *plan* — frame a real problem in terms of audience, task, constraints, success criteria, and risk; *prompt* — write or reuse the prompts that workflow depends on, with the structure Modules 3, 4, and 15 trained; *verify* — apply Module 6's verification habit to the claims and outputs the workflow produces; *revise* — treat the first output as a draft, identify what is weak, and improve it deliberately; *protect information* — apply Module 10's privacy boundaries inside the workflow itself, not as a footnote; and *reflect on your own judgment* — name what you decided, what you delegated to AI, and what you would change on a second pass. The capstone is the place where those six things stop being separate.

The principle that holds Module 16 together is short: *integration is the hard part, not the volume.* A capstone that runs a small, well-scoped task end-to-end with the parts visible — the framing, the prompts, the verification, the revisions, the boundaries, the reflection — is stronger than a capstone that runs a large task with the parts blurred together. The goal is not to demonstrate everything you know. It is to demonstrate that the parts hold up when you put them next to each other on a real piece of work.

## What was preserved

The original capstone structure is preserved. The capstone still asks the learner to take a realistic task and run it end-to-end with AI support. It still requires the parts the original named: *problem framing* (the user, the task, the constraints, the success criteria, the risks); *workflow design* (the stages of the work, with AI's role and the human role separated); *prompt set* (the prompts the workflow depends on, written or reused with the structure Modules 3, 4, and 15 trained); *review and verification* (the claim-checking and output-review work the capstone shows in evidence, not in description); *safety boundaries* (the privacy and risk discipline from Module 10 applied at the workflow level); *presentation* (a document or deck a person who was not in the room could follow); *revision* (evidence that the first version was not the final version); and *reflection* (a one-page note on what the capstone showed about the learner's own AI use that they did not know at the start of Course 1). Each of these is still here. The capstone is still the integration test of Course 1, not a fresh subject of its own.

The intent of the original capstone is preserved: a working demonstration of an AI-supported workflow, not an exam, not a creative project, and not a take-home assessment graded against a hidden standard. The capstone is something the learner would be willing to show to a manager, a client, an instructor, or a future collaborator without having to explain away weak parts. That posture — *would I show this to a reviewer who matters to me?* — is the test the rubric formalises. It was the test before the rewrite and it is still the test now.

The pathway connection is preserved. Each Jifunze pathway weights different artifacts, and the capstone is the place where the learner's pathway becomes visible in finished work. A Digital Work Starter capstone produces something workplace-credible. A Remote Work and Freelancing capstone produces something a first client could see. A Small Business and Entrepreneurship capstone produces an operations or customer-communication workflow. An AI Productivity Professional capstone produces a prompt-pack-driven, reusable, agent-aware workflow. A Junior Tech Builder capstone produces something that demonstrates disciplined AI integration. The pathway connection was implicit in the original capstone; it is still here, named explicitly in the *Choosing your capstone task* section.

## What was improved

The original capstone was outline-level in the same way Modules 11 through 15 were before they were rewritten. The structure was named — *problem framing*, *workflow design*, *prompt set*, *review and verification*, *safety boundaries*, *presentation*, *revision*, *reflection* — and the learner was expected to fill it in. A self-learner with no facilitator, no fresh scenario to test against, and no model artifact to compare against would produce a vague outline of an idea and call it a capstone. The most common Course 1 failure mode — *I have a real but vague task, no scaffolding, and nothing to grade myself against* — was the most likely outcome at the most important moment of the course.

This version closes that gap. It gives the learner a *clearer capstone path*: a sequence the learner can actually follow from chosen task to finished bundle, with the order of operations named (plan first, verify the scope before designing, design the workflow before writing prompts, run the prompts before reviewing, review before revising, revise before reflecting, reflect before packaging). Each step is short enough to complete inside a focused two-to-four-day window, and the dependencies between the steps are visible so a learner cannot skip forward and discover the missing piece three days later.

It adds a *self-critique fallback* that does the work a peer or facilitator would do for a learner who has neither. The fallback is not vague advice to *be honest about your work*. It is a method: a 24-hour pause rule that creates fresh-eyes distance, a named-reader test that imports the standards of one specific person whose judgment the learner respects, a claim-trace test that checks whether every claim in the final output can be traced to a verified source, a privacy review that runs Module 10's discipline against the finished workflow, a usefulness test that asks whether the output does the job, a prompt review against Module 15's quality tests, a revision log that records what changed and why, and a final confidence note. A learner who runs all eight surfaces what a peer reviewer would surface, against the same rubric, and either revises before submitting or submits with the gaps named honestly.

It adds *stronger rubric guidance*. The original capstone gestured at criteria — *problem framing*, *prompt and workflow design*, *verification and review*, *safety and privacy*, *reusability*, *reflection* — without naming what *strong evidence* and *weak evidence* looked like at each level. This version provides a four-level rubric (*Not ready*, *Developing*, *Ready*, *Strong*) for each of seven criteria, with what each level looks like described in the language of the capstone task — not generic adjectives. A learner can read the rubric, look at their own work, and see at which level each criterion sits without inventing the standard themselves.

It adds the *certificate-readiness connection* explicitly. The original capstone left the learner to discover the relationship between Module 16, the certificate, and the rest of the course's checkpoint and artifact requirements. This version names the readiness conditions — all module quizzes passed with at least 6 of 8 correct each, all required portfolio artifacts completed, the capstone completed, the disclosure statement included, the self-critique and revision completed, no high-risk privacy failure, no unsupported claims presented as evidence — and places them in plain sight, so a learner who reaches Module 16 with one or two missing pieces sees what is missing before they spend time on capstone work that will be returned for revision.

It adds *portfolio packaging guidance* aligned with Course1_Portfolio_Guide.md. The capstone bundle is not a single document; it is an organised set — the workflow map, the extended prompt pack and playbook from Module 15, the AI-assisted outputs, the verification and review notes, the safety and privacy boundaries, the revision notes, the final output, the self-critique notes, the one-page reflection, and the disclosure statement — saved with consistent filenames and a context note. A learner finishing Module 16 should walk away with something they could open the next day, share with one specific reviewer, and explain in two minutes.

It adds *explicit reuse of the Module 15 toolkit*. The original capstone described itself as the place where earlier work pays back, but did not say which earlier work or how. This version names the artifacts to bring forward (the Module 15 prompt pack and playbook are required; the Module 12 workflow map, the Module 10 classification table, and the Module 6 verification habit are required; the Module 9 accountability statement is required for the disclosure) and tells the learner how each one feeds the capstone (the pack and playbook *run* the workflow; the workflow map *structures* it; the classification table *bounds* it; the verification habit *checks* it; the accountability statement *adapts* into the disclosure). A learner who arrives at Module 16 with the Module 15 toolkit in hand spends Module 16 demonstrating, not rebuilding.

Module 1 (mental model), Module 2 (responsible judgment), Module 3 (prompt control), Module 4 (structured prompt design), Module 5 (iteration), Module 6 (verification), Module 7 (audience fit), Module 8 (learning support), Module 9 (accountability), Module 10 (privacy and safety), Module 11 (research and synthesis), Module 12 (workflows), Module 13 (decision support), Module 14 (team standards), and Module 15 (prompt packs and playbooks) are woven into the capstone path — each at the step where it does the most work — rather than gestured toward at the end.

## Estimated time

Roughly twelve to eighteen hours of focused work end-to-end, ideally spread across two to four sittings over a week. The capstone is not a single sitting; the 24-hour pause inside the self-critique step alone forces at least two distinct working days, and the verification step usually takes longer than learners predict on the first read.

A breakdown by stage:

- **Capstone planning** — 90 to 120 minutes. Includes choosing the task against the six selection criteria, walking it through the seven-question scope checklist, completing the planning canvas, and confirming the task does not require restricted-tier inputs. A learner who arrives with a candidate task already in mind can finish planning closer to 90 minutes; a learner choosing under time pressure will spend longer and should.
- **Workflow design** — 60 to 90 minutes. Stages, owners, AI-assist decisions, review checkpoints, safety boundaries, and the connection to the Module 15 playbook. If the Module 15 playbook covers the same task family, this step is mostly extension; if not, the workflow is built fresh against the Module 12 SOP discipline.
- **Prompt and workflow execution** — 180 to 300 minutes. The actual running of the workflow on the chosen task. This is where the prompts produce outputs, the inputs are fed in (sanitised against Module 10), the AI is iterated against (Module 5), and the working artifacts of the workflow accumulate. The variation here is large — a study-workflow capstone may run faster than a research-or-reporting capstone — so the high end of the range is realistic, not pessimistic.
- **Review and verification** — 90 to 150 minutes. Module 6's verification habit applied to every claim in the final output, Module 11's source-quality discipline applied where the workflow consumed sources, and the review criteria from the Module 15 prompt pack applied to every AI-assisted output. Learners consistently underestimate this stage on first pass; the rubric will catch a capstone that under-verified.
- **Revision** — 60 to 90 minutes. Acting on what the review surfaced. A first-version output that survives review with no changes either had a strong process behind it or had a weak review behind it; the rubric distinguishes the two.
- **Self-critique** — 60 to 90 minutes spread across two days. The 24-hour pause is part of the time, not extra to it. The active self-critique itself — running the eight steps of the fallback against the work — takes 60 to 90 minutes once the pause has done its work.
- **Final reflection** — 30 to 45 minutes. One page. The reflection is short on purpose; the discipline is in deciding what is worth saying, not in volume.
- **Portfolio packaging** — 45 to 75 minutes. Filenames, folder structure, context notes, disclosure statement, the bundle assembled in the format the learner has chosen (PDF, Notion, Google Drive, GitHub, personal site, or a printed packet for in-person review).
- **Total estimated time** — approximately twelve to eighteen hours, with the median learner finishing at roughly fourteen.

A learner who already runs operational processes at work, has built informal prompt notes for themselves, and arrives with a strong Module 15 artifact may move through the planning and design stages faster but should spend the full time on review, verification, self-critique, and packaging. The certificate-readiness rules require *strong evidence* on at least four of the seven rubric criteria; rubrics are not graded leniently because the learner moved quickly.

A learner who arrives at Module 16 without a strong Module 15 artifact should expect the capstone to take longer — possibly twenty hours or more — because they will be designing the prompt pack, the playbook, and the workflow inside the capstone window. This is not impossible, but it is harder than the design intent. The Capstone Prep guide named this: a learner who reaches Module 16 with the Module 15 toolkit complete spends Module 16 demonstrating the workflow, not rebuilding the toolkit. If you are reading this paragraph and your Module 15 artifact is not yet at full quality, return to Module 15 before continuing.

## Module purpose

Most failures in real AI use are not failures of any single skill. They are failures of integration. A strong prompt with no verification ships an unreliable output. A clean workflow with sensitive data leaking through it fails on the safety axis even when every other axis is strong. A confident decision memo with no record of what was actually checked is indistinguishable, on first reading, from a memo that checked nothing. A research synthesis that overstates what the sources support is more dangerous than a research synthesis that underclaims, because the reader has no way to tell. The capstone is where the parts you have practiced separately are tested together on one piece of work that is small enough to finish and serious enough to mean something.

This module teaches no new skill. Every skill in the capstone has been trained earlier — prompt control in Module 3, structured prompt design in Module 4, iteration in Module 5, verification in Module 6, audience fit in Module 7, learning support in Module 8, accountability in Module 9, privacy in Module 10, research in Module 11, workflows in Module 12, decisions in Module 13, team standards in Module 14, reusable systems in Module 15. What this module teaches is *integration*: the discipline of running the skills together on a real task, in a sequence that holds up, with the parts visible to a reviewer who was not in the room.

The integration matters because integration is what real AI-supported work actually looks like. A workplace task does not arrive labelled *now apply Module 6*. It arrives as a request, with a deadline, with constraints the learner has to identify, with sources that may or may not be reliable, with stakeholders whose tolerance for uncertainty varies, with privacy constraints the learner has to spot, and with a final reader who will judge the output by whether it does the job. The capstone is the closest the course can come to that shape inside a controlled environment. A learner who finishes the capstone has not finished learning AI use; they have demonstrated that the foundation holds when integrated, and that the next time they face a similar task in real work, they have a working method to fall back on rather than a set of disconnected techniques.

The capstone also matters because it is the proof point for everything else. The certificate, the portfolio, the pathway connection, the disclosure habit, the version notes — all of them gain weight when there is one piece of finished, integrated, reviewable work behind them. Without the capstone, the portfolio is a set of exercises. With the capstone, the portfolio is evidence that the exercises connect.

## Capstone overview

You are building one thing: an *End-to-End AI-Supported Workflow Portfolio*. It is the final artifact of Course 1 and the centrepiece of your Jifunze portfolio. It is not a free-form project; it is a defined bundle with eleven required components, each of which has a function in the rubric and in the certificate-readiness check.

The eleven required capstone components:

1. **Problem and task framing.** A short document — usually one page — that names the user, the audience, the task, the constraints, the success criteria, and the risks. The framing answers the question a reviewer will ask first: *what is this workflow for?* If the framing is vague, every other component inherits the vagueness. The framing carries forward elements from the Module 1 plain-language definition, the Module 13 decision criteria table, and (where relevant) the Module 11 research scope brief.
2. **Workflow map.** A diagram or numbered list of the stages of the work, with named owners per stage (you, AI, or both), explicit AI-assist decisions, and the review checkpoints at which a human steps in. The map is built on Module 12's workflow-map discipline; if you completed Module 12 well, the map is a small extension of the artifact you already have.
3. **Prompt pack or playbook used.** The actual prompts the workflow runs, with the Module 15 nine-element structure (purpose, audience, input requirements, prompt text, example use, boundaries, review criteria, ownership, version notes). Where the workflow has multiple AI-assisted stages, the playbook's eight elements (when to use, steps, prompts, review checkpoints, safety boundaries, escalation rules, expected outputs, failure signs) hold them together. Most learners extend their Module 15 artifact rather than write a new one.
4. **AI-assisted outputs.** The actual outputs the workflow produced — the drafts, summaries, comparisons, classifications, or rewrites the AI generated when the prompts were run. Save these as evidence, not just the final output. The rubric grades the workflow, not only the final document.
5. **Verification and review notes.** A claim-verification table (Module 6) for every checkable claim in the final output, the source-quality notes (Module 11) for any sources the workflow consumed, and the review criteria from your prompt pack applied to every AI-assisted output. Verification is shown, not described. *I checked the figures* is not verification; *I checked the 14% figure against the KNBS 2024 release; the figure is in the report; the page reference is on page 27* is.
6. **Safety and privacy boundaries.** The Module 10 classification of the inputs the workflow uses, the sanitisation steps applied to anything in the *caution* tier, the categories excluded from the workflow entirely, and the operational safety checklist (your Module 10 artifact) applied to the workflow stage by stage. If the workflow handles real customer, client, student, patient, or employee data, this section is graded against the strictest interpretation of Module 10 — abstracted or synthetic stand-ins, not the real data itself.
7. **Revision notes.** A short log — usually one page — recording what changed between the first version of the final output and the version submitted, why each change was made, and which review or critique surfaced it. A capstone with no revision is a capstone that under-reviewed.
8. **Final output.** The thing the workflow is for. The customer reply pack, the weekly study cycle, the research synthesis brief, the operations SOP, the decision memo — whatever your task produces. The final output is what a reader of the workflow will most directly act on; it carries the weight of every other component. *Final* here means the version after revision, not the first version the AI produced.
9. **Self-critique or peer/facilitator critique.** Either a peer or facilitator review (when one is available in your deployment) or the self-critique fallback (described in *Self-critique fallback* below). Both produce the same kind of output: an honest reading of the work against the rubric, naming where each criterion sits and what would need to change to lift a *Developing* criterion to *Ready*.
10. **Final reflection.** One page, maximum. What the capstone showed about your AI use that you did not know at the start of Course 1; what you would do differently next time; which of the seven rubric criteria you found hardest, and why. The reflection is not a self-congratulation note; it is a working observation about your own judgment.
11. **Disclosure statement.** Adapted from your Module 9 accountability statement, written to match Course1_Disclosure_Note.md's standard. The disclosure names the AI tool, what it was used for, what review you performed, what changed after review, and what you remain responsible for. A capstone without a disclosure statement is incomplete regardless of how strong the workflow is; the disclosure is part of the standard, not a polite addition.

The bundle is saved with the filename pattern `Module16_Capstone_Bundle_[YourName].(pdf|docx|md)` for the assembled document, and the underlying components are saved in a `16_Capstone/` folder following the structure in Course1_Portfolio_Guide.md. If your bundle is hosted (Notion, Google Drive, GitHub, personal site), keep the same component structure — the eleven items are the rubric's surface area, not the file format's preference.

## Choosing your capstone task

The capstone task is the single most important decision in Module 16. A weak choice cannot be rescued by strong execution. The task has to satisfy seven criteria. If your candidate task fails any of the seven, the most reliable fix is to choose a different task or to make the current task smaller, not to push through and hope the rubric forgives the scope.

**Useful.** The output of the workflow should produce something a person would actually use. A weekly study summary you would re-read and revise from. A customer-response template you would actually send. A research brief that informs a decision you are facing. An onboarding checklist a colleague would follow. A capstone whose output exists only to demonstrate that you used AI — a *demo report on a topic* no one will read — fails this criterion regardless of how cleanly it runs.

**Realistic.** The task must be one you actually do, plausibly will do, or could clearly explain to someone who does. A capstone built on a hypothetical job you do not have, or a project you have never touched, drifts into vague generality the rubric catches. If you can describe the audience, the input, and the desired output in concrete terms — *the office assistant in a small accountancy firm in Nairobi who runs the Friday weekly recap for the team lead* — the task is realistic enough.

**Bounded.** The task must be small enough to complete end-to-end inside the time budget. The capstone is a two-to-four-day effort across a week, not a three-week project. A good capstone has one clear input source, one or two AI-assisted stages, one human review step, and one finished output. If you need a week of background research, ten interviews, or a rebuild of an existing system, the scope is wrong. The most common Module 16 failure mode is choosing a task that is interesting but not bounded; the second most common is finishing a too-large task and producing something thin in every component.

**Reviewable.** You — or another reasonable person — must be able to tell whether the output is good. If you cannot describe what *strong evidence* and *weak evidence* look like for this task in concrete terms, you cannot grade the output, and neither can the rubric. A task whose quality depends on subjective preference alone (*write something nice*) is not reviewable. A task whose quality depends on accuracy, audience fit, completeness, fitness-for-purpose, or fit to a written standard is reviewable.

**Not too sensitive.** The task should not require you to put real customer data, real medical records, real legal records, real student records, real HR records, real financial records, or any other restricted-tier information into an AI tool. Module 10 trained you to recognise this; the capstone is where you act on that training. If your strongest candidate task is sensitive, you have two real options: redesign the workflow to use abstracted, summarised, or synthetic inputs, or pick a different task. There is no third option that involves *being careful with the real data*. The rubric grades the workflow as designed; a workflow that pastes restricted data into a tool fails the safety criterion regardless of intent.

**Connected to your learner pathway.** Course 1 maps to five Jifunze pathways. Your capstone should produce evidence that matters for the pathway you intend to enter next. A Digital Work Starter learner builds workplace-credible artifacts. A Remote Work and Freelancing learner builds something a first client could see. A Small Business and Entrepreneurship learner builds an operations or customer-communication workflow. An AI Productivity Professional learner builds a prompt-pack-driven, reusable, agent-aware workflow. A Junior Tech Builder learner builds something that demonstrates disciplined AI integration. If your task does not produce evidence relevant to your pathway, the artifact is weaker for the audience it is meant for; ask which task would.

**Possible to complete in the available time.** The capstone time budget is twelve to eighteen hours of focused work. If your task requires more — because the verification stage alone would take a week, because the source material is not yet accessible, because the input is gated by another person's schedule — the capstone will not finish well. Cut the scope to fit the time, or pick a task whose scope already fits.

A task that satisfies all seven criteria is rarely the most exciting candidate on your list. That is normal. Pick the disciplined option. The exciting candidate may become a strong capstone for Course 2 or for your own work after the certificate; for Module 16, the disciplined option produces the stronger artifact.

The examples below are illustrative shapes, not prescriptions. If your candidate task resembles one of these in scale and structure — bounded, reviewable, useful, pathway-connected — you are in the right zone.

**Student or study workflow.** A learner preparing for a specific certification, end-of-term exam, or licensing assessment in two to three weeks. *Capstone:* an AI-supported study workflow that takes a syllabus or set of notes as input, produces a weekly study plan, generates retrieval-practice question sets at the end of each week, and includes a weak-area repair loop (Module 8). *Output:* the workflow itself, the prompt pack that runs it, one full weekly cycle of evidence (plan, questions, repair notes), and a self-critique on whether the workflow actually helped retention versus just felt productive. *Pathway fit:* most pathways, but especially Digital Work Starter (for upskilling toward a workplace certification) and AI Productivity Professional (for using AI as a learning tool with discipline).

**Office or admin workflow.** An office assistant, operations coordinator, or administrative officer who runs a recurring task — a weekly internal recap, monthly reporting, meeting-notes-to-actions, onboarding checklists, supplier-update emails. *Capstone:* an AI-supported workflow that takes the standard inputs (raw meeting notes, raw weekly events, a draft report) and produces the standard output the role already requires, with explicit review and verification steps and a documented privacy boundary on what data may be pasted in. *Output:* the workflow, the prompt pack, one fully run cycle, and a one-page *how a colleague would use this* handoff note. *Pathway fit:* Digital Work Starter, with strong reuse value for AI Productivity Professional.

**Small business customer communication workflow.** A small-business owner, manager, or customer-service operator who handles repetitive customer messages — order confirmations, delay updates, refund explanations, follow-up nudges, common questions. *Capstone:* an AI-supported customer-message workflow with a standard prompt set, a tone and voice guideline, a privacy boundary on what customer data may be included in prompts, and a human-review step before any message is sent. *Output:* the workflow, the prompt pack, three sample message types fully written and reviewed (using abstracted or synthetic customer details, not real ones), and a short note on which message types the workflow handles well and which still need a human draft. *Pathway fit:* Small Business and Entrepreneurship, with strong reuse value for Remote Work and Freelancing.

**Freelance or client workflow.** A freelance writer, designer, analyst, consultant, or service provider who takes a typical client brief and produces a typical client deliverable. *Capstone:* an AI-supported intake-to-draft workflow with stages for understanding the brief, drafting, internal review, client-ready presentation, and revision. *Output:* the workflow, the prompt pack, one fully run cycle on a real or realistic brief (with the client's identifying details abstracted), a disclosure statement the freelancer would include with the deliverable, and a self-critique on what the workflow improved versus what it did not. *Pathway fit:* Remote Work and Freelancing, with strong overlap to AI Productivity Professional.

**Research or reporting workflow.** A learner researching a defined question for a report, briefing, or recommendation — a market scan, a competitor comparison, a policy summary, a literature note, an internal scoping note. *Capstone:* an AI-supported research workflow that takes a scoped question and a small set of sources as input, produces a synthesis brief with explicit uncertainty labels and source attribution (Module 11), and ends with a human-owned conclusion paragraph that does not over-claim. *Output:* the workflow, the prompt pack, one full brief, the verification log behind it, and a short note on what the brief does and does not establish. *Pathway fit:* AI Productivity Professional, Small Business and Entrepreneurship, and Junior Tech Builder.

**Team or workplace workflow.** A learner in a small team that has agreed on shared AI use (Module 14) and wants to package one shared workflow as a reusable asset the team can run consistently. *Capstone:* an AI-supported team workflow — a shared recap, a shared customer-handover process, a shared report-drafting routine — built on the team's agreement, with the responsibility map applied at the stage level, the disclosure rule applied at the output level, and the prompt-library rules applied to the prompts inside the playbook. *Output:* the workflow, the playbook, one fully run cycle (with team members' real contributions abstracted to roles where needed), and a one-page *how a new team member runs this* handoff note. *Pathway fit:* AI Productivity Professional and Digital Work Starter, with strong overlap to Small Business and Entrepreneurship for owner-operated teams. *Solo-learner alternative:* run the workflow as if for a hypothetical four-person team — the same discipline transfers.

If your candidate task does not fit any of these shapes, that is acceptable. Run it through the seven criteria carefully. The most reliable check: write the task in one sentence that names the audience, the input, and the output — *I am building an AI-supported workflow for [who] to take [what input] and produce [what output] for [what reader], to be used [how often], with the constraint that [what limit].* If you can write that sentence honestly, the task is probably bounded enough.

## Capstone planning canvas

The planning canvas is a one-document fill-in that turns your candidate task into a capstone you can run. Complete it before you write any prompts. Most weak capstones are the ones where the planning canvas was skipped and the workflow design started before the framing was settled; the result is a workflow that fits the prompts the learner already wrote, rather than prompts that fit the task the learner needed to do.

Fill in each field below in your own words. Keep each answer short — one to three sentences. The discipline is in the specificity, not the length.

**1. Task title.** A short name for your capstone, descriptive enough to identify the task at a glance. *Weekly Internal Recap Workflow for a Three-Person Operations Team.* *Customer-Reply Pack for Delivery-Window Change Requests.* *Two-Week Study Workflow for the [Certification Name] Exam.* Avoid generic titles (*Capstone Project*, *AI Workflow*). The title is the first thing a reviewer reads.

**2. Learner context.** Who you are in this capstone. Your role, your relevant background, your pathway, and the situation in which this task arises. *I am a small-business owner running a print-and-design shop in Eastlands, Nairobi, with one part-time assistant. I handle most customer messages on WhatsApp myself. My pathway is Small Business and Entrepreneurship.* The context lets a reviewer place the work; it also disciplines you to write for your real situation rather than a generic workplace.

**3. Intended audience.** Who the final output is for. Be specific. Not *customers* — *recurring small-business customers ordering branded notebooks for corporate gifts, with whom we have an existing relationship.* Not *students* — *Form Three students at a county-level day school in Western Kenya preparing for the end-of-term biology test.* The audience description is what disciplines the tone, length, vocabulary, and assumptions inside the prompts.

**4. Pathway connection.** Which Jifunze pathway this capstone supports, and what evidence it provides for that pathway. *This capstone supports the Small Business and Entrepreneurship pathway. The customer-reply pack is workplace-credible evidence that I can run AI-assisted customer communication with privacy and disclosure discipline.* If your pathway connection is weak, return to *Choosing your capstone task* and reconsider the task.

**5. Problem or need.** Why this workflow exists at all. What is hard, slow, inconsistent, or risky about how the work currently runs without an AI-supported workflow? *Customer messages about delivery delays are written from scratch each time, vary in tone, and occasionally over-promise. A reusable workflow would produce consistent, accurate, on-tone replies with a documented review step.* The problem statement is what makes the capstone matter; if you cannot name a real problem, the workflow is a demo.

**6. Success criteria.** What *good* looks like for this workflow. Three to five named criteria, each concrete enough to check. *The reply addresses the customer by their first name. The reply names the original delivery window and the new committed window. The reply contains no commitment beyond the window change. The reply is under 120 words. The reply is reviewable by me in under thirty seconds before sending.* If you cannot write success criteria, the rubric cannot grade the output.

**7. Inputs.** The information the workflow needs to run. List each input, its source, and any sensitivity classification (Module 10). *Customer first name (sensitive — abstracted to Customer A in capstone). Original delivery window (operational, safe). Requested new window (operational, safe). Whether the new window is feasible (operational, safe). Note from the customer (varies — sanitised before pasting).* Inputs that fall in restricted or never-enter tiers are either abstracted or excluded; the input list is also the boundary list.

**8. Sensitivity classification.** A one-sentence summary of where the inputs and outputs sit on Module 10's four-tier classification. *Inputs are mostly operational with one personal field (customer name) that is abstracted in capstone evidence. Outputs are operational customer-facing messages with no protected information.* If your sensitivity summary contains anything in the restricted or never-enter tier, redesign the workflow before continuing.

**9. AI role.** What AI does in this workflow, named in plain language. *Drafts the first version of the customer reply from the inputs, suggests one alternative phrasing for the apology line, and produces a structured draft a human can review.* The AI role is bounded; *AI does the customer reply* is too broad. The discipline of Module 13 — AI supports, does not decide — applies here.

**10. Human role.** What you do in this workflow. *Sanitise inputs, run the prompt, review the output against the review criteria, edit for tone and accuracy, verify the delivery commitment against the operational tracker, and approve before sending.* The human role is named at the level of work, not at the level of *I am responsible*. Responsibility is a posture; the human role is a list of actions.

**11. Workflow stages.** The named stages of the workflow, in order, with named owners. Usually four to seven stages. *Stage 1: Sanitise inputs (human). Stage 2: Run the customer-reply prompt (AI). Stage 3: Review the output against review criteria (human). Stage 4: Verify the delivery commitment (human). Stage 5: Approve and send (human).* The stages are the workflow map's spine; the prompts and review checkpoints attach to them.

**12. Review checkpoints.** The named points in the workflow where a human review happens, what is reviewed, and what happens if the review fails. *After Stage 2, the human reviews the AI draft against the seven-item review criteria. If any criterion fails, the human edits the draft directly or re-runs the prompt with a clarifying constraint. After Stage 4, the human verifies the delivery commitment against the operational tracker. If the commitment cannot be verified, the workflow halts and the human escalates to dispatch.* Review checkpoints are not generic; they are specific to this workflow's failure modes.

**13. Risks.** What could go wrong with this workflow. Three to five risks, each with a short note on what would catch the failure. *Risk: AI invents a delivery window not supported by the operational tracker. Catch: human verification at Stage 4. Risk: AI uses a phrase the team voice would not use. Catch: review criteria item on tone match. Risk: a customer's note contains sensitive information the human pastes without sanitising. Catch: Stage 1 sanitisation step, with the safety checklist applied.* Naming the risks is what makes the workflow defendable.

**14. Boundaries.** What this workflow is *not* for, and what to do instead. *Not for refund requests (use the refund process). Not for already-dispatched orders (escalate to dispatch). Not for distressed or angry customers (the empathetic-reply path is human-only). Not for customers whose details have not been verified against the customer record.* Boundaries are the workflow's outer protection; they prevent the asset from being applied to a context it was not designed for.

**15. Final deliverable.** What the workflow produces when it runs end-to-end. *One customer reply, under 120 words, addressed to the customer by name, naming the original and new delivery window, with no commitment beyond the window change, in the team's standard voice, ready for me to send.* The final deliverable description doubles as the rubric's surface for the *useful output* criterion.

A completed canvas is one to two pages. It is the document a reviewer reads first when they open your capstone bundle, and it is the document you read again before each working session to keep the capstone honest. If a workflow design choice or a prompt would contradict the canvas, the canvas is what holds; the workflow design or the prompt revises to match.

## Self-critique fallback

A peer or facilitator review is the gold standard for the capstone's review step. A reviewer who knows the rubric and does not know your work is the most reliable way to surface the gaps you cannot see. In Jifunze deployments where peer or facilitator review is available, take it; the time it costs is the time it saves on revision later.

For learners without peer or facilitator review — the standard self-paced path — the self-critique fallback below is the substitute. It is not a polite substitute; it is designed to do the same work the reviewer would do, against the same rubric, with the same honesty. A learner who runs the eight steps below produces what a peer reviewer would produce. A learner who skips the fallback or does it casually produces a capstone that the certificate-readiness check will return for revision.

**1. The 24-hour pause rule.** When you finish the capstone, walk away for a full day. Do not look at the work. Do not edit a typo. Do not re-run a prompt. Most weaknesses become visible inside the first ten minutes of fresh-eyes review and stay invisible in the hours immediately after writing. The pause is not optional. A capstone reviewed inside the same writing session is reviewed by a tired version of you who is already attached to the work.

If a 24-hour pause is impossible because of a deadline you have committed to externally, use a six-to-eight-hour pause and a deliberate context shift — go for a walk, attend an unrelated meeting, sleep — between writing and reviewing. The point of the pause is mental distance; the duration matters less than the distance, but distance shorter than a few hours rarely produces it.

**2. The named-reader test.** Pick one specific real person — a former colleague, a peer from this course, a mentor, a relative who works in a relevant field, a senior person whose judgment you respect — and write down what they would say if they reviewed your capstone. Imagine the comment they would actually make, not the comment you wish they would make. *My former lead would say the workflow is fine but the verification log is thin and she would not approve a customer message based on it.* *My mentor would say the disclosure statement reads like boilerplate and does not match how I actually worked.* *My business-partner peer would say the prompt pack is good but the safety boundaries miss the case where a customer pastes an M-Pesa transaction code into the original message.*

The named-reader test works because most learners can be honest about a named reader's standards even when they cannot be honest about their own. The standard is not *would I be embarrassed?* — that standard is too easy. The standard is *what would my named reader actually say if they read this with the rubric in front of them?* Write down the comments. They are your revision list.

**3. The claim-trace test.** Read the final output of the capstone slowly. For every claim that a reader might act on — a figure, a date, a name, a commitment, a recommendation, a citation, a procedural step, a piece of advice — ask: *can I trace this claim to a verified source the reader could check?* If yes, mark it traced. If no, mark it untraced. The capstone's verification log should already have the traced claims documented; if a claim in the final output is not in the verification log, the verification log is incomplete.

Untraced claims are not always wrong; they are sometimes assumptions, opinions, or framings that do not need a source. The discipline is in distinguishing the two. *The team meets on Friday afternoons* is a fact (traceable to the team's calendar). *Friday afternoons are the best time for a recap* is a judgment (traceable to the learner's reasoning, which should be visible). *Studies have consistently shown that weekly recaps improve team cohesion* is a claim that requires a source, and the source had better exist; this is the failure mode Module 6 specifically corrects.

A capstone with even one unsupported claim presented as evidence fails the certificate-readiness rule. The claim-trace test catches this before the rubric does.

**4. The privacy review.** Run the workflow against Module 10's classification one more time. For each input the workflow consumes, confirm the classification you assigned in the planning canvas is correct. For each output, confirm that nothing in the *restricted* or *never-enter* tier appears in any prompt, any AI output, any review note, or any final document. If the capstone uses real customer, client, student, patient, or employee data — even abstracted — confirm the abstraction is real, not a name change with the rest of the identifying detail intact.

The privacy review surfaces the most common late-stage failure: a learner who sanitised the customer's name in the prompt but left the order number, the phone-call timestamp, and the WhatsApp screenshot in the supporting evidence. The rubric grades the bundle as a whole, not the sanitised parts only.

**5. The usefulness test.** Read the final output of the workflow as if you were the intended audience. Does it do the job? *Would I, as the customer in this scenario, accept this reply as a satisfactory response to my question?* *Would I, as the team lead, take action on this recap without needing to ask for clarification?* *Would I, as the student studying for this exam, use this study plan and the questions it produced?* If the answer is *not really* or *I would need to rewrite the third paragraph*, the workflow has produced a partial output, which is graded *Developing* on the rubric's *useful output* criterion.

The usefulness test is also the catch for the most insidious failure mode in AI-supported work: the output that looks correct on first reading and falls apart when you imagine actually using it. AI output is often fluent enough to pass a quick read; the usefulness test asks for slow reading from the recipient's perspective.

**6. The prompt review.** Run the prompts in your prompt pack against Module 15's four quality tests. *Could another person use this prompt without explanation?* If a reader of the pack would have to ask you a clarifying question, the prompt is not standalone yet. *Does it work on a fresh scenario?* If you have not tested the prompt on a scenario other than the one it was first built against, the test log is incomplete. *Does it preserve safety and review?* If the prompt does not encode Module 10 boundaries and Module 12 review points, the prompt is not pack-ready. *Does it produce reviewable output?* If the prompt's output is fluent but vague, the prompt needs more structure (numbered sections, named fields, length limits, format constraints).

The prompt review is the difference between a capstone whose prompts will survive being read by the rubric and a capstone whose prompts will not. It is also the closest the self-critique gets to the technical core of the work; learners who skip it usually do so because *the prompts are mine and they work* — which is exactly the assumption Module 15 trained you out of.

**7. The revision log.** As you run steps 1–6, you will surface things to change. Record each change as you make it. *Changed the customer-reply prompt's opening line from "Hi {name}" to "Dear {name}" after the named-reader test surfaced that "Hi" reads casual for our customer base.* *Added a verification step at Stage 4 after the claim-trace test surfaced one untraced delivery-window claim.* *Removed the M-Pesa transaction code from the example use after the privacy review.* *Tightened the failure signs section of the playbook after the prompt review.* The revision log is one of the eleven required components; it is also the evidence that the self-critique was real.

A capstone with no revision log entries — a capstone where everything was already correct — is almost always a capstone where the self-critique did not happen.

**8. The final confidence note.** After you have run steps 1–7, write a short note (three to five sentences) that names where you are confident in the capstone, where you are less confident, and what would need to change to lift the less-confident parts to *Ready* on the rubric. *I am confident in the workflow design and the prompt pack. I am less confident in the verification log — I traced six of the seven checkable claims; one would have required a source I could not access in the time available, and I labelled it as uncertain rather than removing it. To lift the verification to Ready, I would need to either find the source or rewrite the section that depends on it.*

The confidence note matters because it is honest about the capstone's own limits. A reviewer (peer, facilitator, or future-you) reading the bundle uses the confidence note to calibrate their own reading. A capstone with a confidence note that says *everything is strong* is harder to trust than a capstone with a confidence note that names two real weaknesses and what they would take to fix; the second sounds like the work of someone who can grade their own output, which is exactly what the certificate certifies.

If, after running the eight steps, you still believe the capstone is strong, it probably is. If any of the eight surfaces a real problem, fix the problem before submitting. Self-critique that produces no revisions is usually self-critique that did not happen; self-critique that produces five small revisions and one larger one is usually self-critique that did.

## Capstone rubric

The capstone is graded against seven criteria. Each criterion sits at one of four levels: *Not ready*, *Developing*, *Ready*, or *Strong*. A passing capstone scores at *Ready* or higher on every criterion, with at least four of the seven at *Strong*. A capstone with one *Developing* criterion is returned for revision; a capstone with two or more *Developing* criteria, or any *Not ready* criterion, requires substantive revision before the certificate is issued.

The rubric is descriptive, not numerical. The level descriptions below are written in the language of the capstone task — what the actual evidence looks like at each level — so a learner can read the rubric, look at their own work, and see at which level each criterion sits without inventing the standard themselves.

### 1. Problem framing

What is graded: whether the capstone names the user, the audience, the task, the constraints, the success criteria, the risks, and the boundaries with enough specificity that a reviewer could grade the output against the framing without inventing what *good* means.

- **Not ready.** The framing is missing or generic. The audience is *users* or *customers* with no further description. The success criteria are absent or written as adjectives (*good*, *useful*, *clear*). The risks are not named. The boundaries are not named. A reviewer cannot tell what this workflow is for.
- **Developing.** The framing is present but underspecified. The audience is named but not described. Success criteria are listed but mix concrete items (*under 120 words*) with adjectives (*professional tone*). Risks are gestured at but not named with their catches. The framing is recognisable as framing but not strong enough to grade against.
- **Ready.** The framing is specific. The audience is described in concrete terms. Success criteria are concrete and checkable. Risks are named with their catches. Boundaries are named. A reviewer could grade the output against the framing without inventing the standard.
- **Strong.** The framing is specific and the planning canvas reads as a working specification a colleague could pick up and use to extend the workflow. The success criteria and risks are calibrated to the real situation, not to a generic version of it. The framing also names what the workflow is *not* for at the same level of specificity as what it is for.

### 2. Prompt and workflow design

What is graded: whether the prompts use the Module 4 / Module 15 structure, whether the workflow uses the Module 12 stage structure, whether the AI-assist decisions are explicit, and whether the design produces a workflow another person could run without explanation.

- **Not ready.** Prompts are short and unstructured. The workflow is described in a paragraph rather than mapped. AI's role and the human role are blurred. The design would not survive being handed to another person.
- **Developing.** Prompts are present and partly structured (a Task and an Audience are named, but Constraints, Format, or Examples are missing). The workflow has stages but the owners or review checkpoints are unclear. The design is recognisable but a reviewer would have questions before running it.
- **Ready.** Prompts have the Module 4 structure visibly applied (Task, Context, Constraints, Format, Audience, Examples where useful, do-not clauses where useful). The workflow has stages, owners, AI-assist decisions, review checkpoints, safety boundaries, and failure signs. The design would survive being handed to another person.
- **Strong.** The prompt pack uses the Module 15 nine-element structure at full quality. The playbook uses the Module 15 eight-element structure. The workflow is mapped against the Module 12 SOP discipline visibly. The prompt set has been version-noted and tested on a fresh scenario, with the test log included.

### 3. Verification and review

What is graded: whether claims in the final output are traceable to verified sources (Module 6), whether AI-assisted outputs were reviewed against the prompt pack's review criteria (Module 15), whether sources used were quality-checked (Module 11 where relevant), and whether the review evidence is shown rather than described.

- **Not ready.** Claims in the final output are presented without verification. AI-assisted outputs were used as-is without a documented review step. There is no verification log, or the log is empty.
- **Developing.** Some claims are verified and some are not. The review is described (*I checked the figures*) but not shown (no claim-by-claim trace). The verification log exists but is sparse or generic.
- **Ready.** Every checkable claim in the final output is traced in the verification log. AI-assisted outputs were reviewed against the prompt pack's review criteria, with the review evidenced in the bundle. Source-quality notes are present where the workflow consumed sources. The review is shown, not described.
- **Strong.** The verification log is detailed enough that a reviewer could re-run the verification themselves. Every untraced item in the final output is explicitly flagged as uncertain, with the reason for the uncertainty named. The review process surfaced and corrected at least one error in the AI output, and the correction is recorded in the revision log.

### 4. Safety and privacy

What is graded: whether the workflow respects Module 10's classification at every stage, whether sensitive inputs are abstracted or excluded, whether the operational safety checklist is applied stage by stage, and whether the bundle as a whole — not just the prompts — is safe to share.

- **Not ready.** Real customer, client, student, patient, employee, or financial data appears in any prompt, output, or supporting evidence in identifiable form. *Or:* the classification of the inputs is not documented and there is no sanitisation step.
- **Developing.** Inputs are mostly classified but the classification is incomplete or inconsistent. Some sanitisation is applied but the bundle contains identifying details elsewhere (in supporting evidence, in screenshots, in a saved chat transcript). The workflow does not have an explicit safety boundary section in the playbook.
- **Ready.** Every input is classified. Caution-tier inputs are sanitised before use. Restricted-tier and never-enter-tier inputs are excluded from the workflow entirely or replaced with abstracted or synthetic stand-ins. The bundle as a whole — including supporting evidence — is free of identifying information about real people without their consent. The operational safety checklist is applied at the workflow level.
- **Strong.** The workflow's safety boundaries are encoded inside the prompts and the playbook themselves, not added as a final check. The boundary list is specific to this workflow's failure modes, not a generic privacy reminder. The bundle could be published openly without harm to anyone the workflow touched.

### 5. Usefulness of final output

What is graded: whether the output does the job it was framed to do, whether the intended audience would accept it as a satisfactory response or product, and whether the output's strengths and limits are honestly described.

- **Not ready.** The output does not do the job. The intended audience would not accept it. The output reads as a demonstration of AI use rather than as something a real reader would act on.
- **Developing.** The output partly does the job. The intended audience would accept it after a moderate revision. The output reads as a working draft rather than a finished artifact, but the working draft is recognisable as fit for purpose.
- **Ready.** The output does the job. The intended audience would accept it as a satisfactory response or product. The output is finished, not draft, and the bundle includes a short note on what the output does and does not establish.
- **Strong.** The output does the job and the output's limits are named honestly inside the bundle. A reader of the bundle understands not only what the output produces but the situations in which it would not be the right output, and the workflow's failure signs are visible.

### 6. Reusability and transfer

What is graded: whether the workflow could be adapted to a similar but not identical task, whether the prompt pack and playbook are reusable assets in the Module 15 sense, and whether a colleague could pick up the bundle and run it without you in the room.

- **Not ready.** The workflow is built only for the one task it was first run against. The prompt pack is a list of prompts. The playbook is a paragraph. A colleague could not run the workflow without you.
- **Developing.** The workflow could be adapted to a similar task with effort. The prompt pack has some of the Module 15 nine elements but not all. The playbook has some of the Module 15 eight elements but not all. A colleague could run the workflow with one or two clarifying questions.
- **Ready.** The workflow is generalisable to the class of tasks it was designed for, not only to the single instance. The prompt pack has all nine elements. The playbook has all eight. A colleague could run the workflow without you.
- **Strong.** The prompt pack and the playbook have been version-noted and tested on a fresh scenario, with the test log included. A colleague who has not seen the rest of your work could pick up the bundle, run the workflow on a fresh instance of the task, and produce an acceptable output without consulting you. The bundle reads as a working asset, not a course exercise.

### 7. Reflection and improvement

What is graded: whether the one-page reflection names what the capstone showed about the learner's AI use that they did not know at the start, whether the revision log is honest about what changed and why, and whether the final confidence note calibrates the bundle's strengths and limits accurately.

- **Not ready.** No reflection, or a reflection that is generic (*I learned a lot about AI*). No revision log, or a revision log with no entries. No confidence note, or a confidence note that says everything is strong.
- **Developing.** The reflection is present but is descriptive rather than reflective (*I built a workflow and ran it*). The revision log has entries but the entries do not name what surfaced the change. The confidence note names confidence levels but not what would lift the lower levels.
- **Ready.** The reflection names at least one thing the learner now knows about their own AI use that they did not know at the start of Course 1, with a concrete example. The revision log records what changed, why, and what surfaced the change. The confidence note names where the bundle is strong, where it is less confident, and what would lift the less-confident parts to *Ready*.
- **Strong.** The reflection is calibrated and specific. It names something the learner got wrong and corrected, not only something they got right. The revision log shows the self-critique fallback's effect on the bundle visibly. The confidence note is the kind of note a reviewer would write if they were reviewing the bundle for someone else — honest, specific, and free of self-congratulation.

### Reading the rubric on your own work

The rubric is meant to be read against your bundle as a whole, not against your favourite parts. The most useful self-grading method is to open the bundle, read the rubric criterion-by-criterion, and write down which level each criterion sits at *before* you defend any of it. The defence comes later; the first read is for the level placement.

A passing capstone has every criterion at *Ready* or *Strong*, with at least four at *Strong*. A capstone with one criterion at *Developing* is one revision away from passing. A capstone with two or more at *Developing*, or any criterion at *Not ready*, needs substantive revision — usually a return to the planning canvas, the workflow design, or the prompt pack, depending on which criterion is weakest.

Most learners' first self-grading is one or two levels too generous. The named-reader test from the self-critique fallback usually corrects this: a reader you respect would not give the level you gave, and the gap between the two ratings is your real revision work.

## Certificate-readiness connection

Module 16 is the last gate on Course 1's certificate. The certificate is issued automatically when all of the following are true at once. Until they are, the certificate is not issued.

**All sixteen module quizzes passed with at least 6 of 8 correct each.** Each module's checkpoint is six correct out of eight, scored against the rubric in the answer key. A learner who reaches Module 16 with one or more checkpoints not yet passed cannot earn the certificate until those checkpoints pass. The platform shows *not yet passed* checkpoints on the dashboard's *return to* list; clearing the list is part of certificate readiness, not a separate task.

**All required portfolio artifacts completed.** Sixteen module artifacts plus the capstone bundle. Each artifact accepted against its named acceptance criteria. Returned artifacts that have not yet been revised do not count. The Module 15 prompt pack and the Module 16 capstone bundle are graded against the strongest acceptance bar — *would this artifact survive being extended in a real working context?* — because both feed the capstone and the portfolio respectively.

**Capstone completed.** The capstone bundle has all eleven required components. The rubric scores at *Ready* or higher on every criterion, with at least four at *Strong*. A capstone returned for revision is not yet complete; revision is part of completion, not a separate stage.

**Disclosure included.** The capstone bundle includes a disclosure statement adapted from your Module 9 accountability statement and aligned with Course1_Disclosure_Note.md. The disclosure names the AI tool, what it was used for, what review you performed, what changed after review, and what you remain responsible for. A disclosure that reads as boilerplate, that overstates AI involvement, that minimises AI involvement, or that off-loads responsibility to the AI is not a valid disclosure for the certificate's purposes; the disclosure standard exists in Course1_Disclosure_Note.md for exactly this reason.

**Self-critique or peer/facilitator critique completed.** Either the self-critique fallback (the eight steps from the section above) or a peer/facilitator review (where available). The review surfaced what it surfaced, the revisions were made, and the revision log records both. A capstone submitted without the critique step is incomplete.

**Revision completed where the rubric or critique surfaced a gap.** Any rubric criterion at *Developing* in the self-grading is either lifted to *Ready* through revision or honestly flagged in the confidence note, with a clear plan for the lift. A capstone with two or more *Developing* criteria submitted without revision does not pass.

**No high-risk privacy failure.** The bundle contains no real customer, client, student, patient, employee, or financial data in identifiable form. The Module 10 classification is documented. Caution-tier inputs are sanitised. Restricted-tier and never-enter-tier inputs are excluded or abstracted. A privacy failure is a hard fail of the safety criterion regardless of other strengths; the certificate-readiness check enforces this.

**No unsupported claim presented as evidence.** Every claim in the final output that a reader might act on is either traced in the verification log or honestly flagged as uncertain. A capstone with one or more unsupported claims presented as if they were verified facts does not pass; this is the failure mode Module 6 corrected and the rubric catches at the *verification and review* criterion.

The certificate is granted when all eight conditions are true at the same time. There is no waiver path. The Course1_Certificate_Readiness.md document is the source of truth on what the certificate certifies and what it does not; a learner reading it will see that the certificate is evidence of completed practice, not promise of outcome, and that the eight conditions above are what *completed practice* means in this course.

A learner who reaches Module 16 with one or two of the eight conditions not yet met is not late; they are at the standard point on the path. The fix is to clear the missing pieces before submitting the capstone, not after. Going back to fix one missing piece is faster than submitting a capstone that will be returned for revision and discovering the same gap then.

A learner who reaches Module 16 with all eight conditions clearly in reach, an arrived Module 15 toolkit, and a chosen task that fits the seven selection criteria should expect the capstone to be demanding but doable — the work of a focused week, not a frantic one — and should expect the certificate to be earned through finished work that they would be willing to show to a reviewer who matters to them.

## Pause and check

Before you start the capstone work, answer the three questions below honestly. They are not graded; they are the last calibration before the work begins. A learner who answers all three confidently is ready to start. A learner who hesitates on one or more should resolve the hesitation before continuing — usually by returning to the named module or the planning canvas — rather than push forward and discover the same gap during the capstone.

**1. Do I have a chosen capstone task that satisfies all seven selection criteria — useful, realistic, bounded, reviewable, not too sensitive, connected to my pathway, and possible to complete in twelve to eighteen hours of focused work — and can I describe it in one sentence that names the audience, the input, and the output?** If yes, the task is ready. If no — if the task is interesting but not bounded, or realistic but not pathway-connected, or useful but contains restricted-tier inputs you have not yet worked out how to abstract — return to *Choosing your capstone task* before continuing. The planning canvas built on a weak task choice produces a capstone the rubric will catch.

**2. Do I have the five required earlier artifacts — Module 6 verification habit, Module 9 accountability statement, Module 10 safety checklist and classification table, Module 12 workflow map, and Module 15 prompt pack and playbook — at full quality, accessible in one folder, and would I be willing to lift their structure directly into the capstone bundle?** If yes, the toolkit is ready. If no — if the Module 15 artifact is still a list rather than a pack, or the Module 10 classification table is partial, or the Module 12 workflow map is described in prose rather than mapped — return to the underlying module and revise before continuing. The capstone built on weak inputs is itself weak, and the rubric grades the capstone, not the underlying intent.

**3. Do I have at least two-to-four days of focused, uninterrupted working time available for the capstone, including a real opportunity to walk away for twenty-four hours mid-process so the self-critique fallback's pause rule can do its work?** If yes, the time is ready. If no — if you are squeezing the capstone into evening fragments, or you cannot create a 24-hour pause because of an external deadline — pause the start of the capstone until the time is real. A capstone done in scattered fifteen-minute slots loses coherence; a self-critique done without a real pause loses the fresh-eyes effect; both failure modes show up in the rubric. The certificate has no time limit. Going slowly to do the capstone well is faster than going quickly to do it twice.

If the three answers are *yes*, *yes*, and *yes*, you are ready. The capstone will not be easy, but it will be doable, and the work you produce will be worth showing.
