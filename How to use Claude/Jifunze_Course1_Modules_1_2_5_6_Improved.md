## Core lesson

### A practical definition
AI refers to computer systems designed to perform tasks that involve pattern recognition, prediction, classification, generation, retrieval, or decision support, based on data and instructions. That definition is more useful than dramatic claims about machines that "think" because it ties AI to specific kinds of work — work you can name, evaluate, and review.

What that definition rules out: the idea that AI is one homogeneous thing. AI is a category of tools, in the same way "transport" is a category. A bicycle, a matatu, and an airplane are all transport, but they are not interchangeable. AI tools that classify images, generate text, retrieve documents, and predict prices are all "AI," but they have different strengths, weaknesses, and risks.

### What AI does well
Modern AI tools, especially the text-based assistants you are likely using during this course, are reliably useful for a set of tasks: drafting first versions of writing, restructuring messy notes into cleaner formats, comparing options, summarizing material you provide, brainstorming angles or examples, simplifying explanations, rewriting for a different audience, and extracting tasks or themes from text.

Notice the pattern: AI is strongest when it transforms or works with material that already exists, especially material you supply. The grounding helps the system stay on task and reduces the chance it invents facts.

### What AI does not do well
The same tools are weaker — sometimes dangerously so — at other tasks. They are not reliable as a source of truth, especially for current events, specific statistics, or claims that depend on recent or local information. They are not consistent decision-makers in high-stakes contexts, including medical, legal, financial, or disciplinary decisions. They are not aware of context they have not been told. They cannot take responsibility, because responsibility belongs to the person or organization that uses the output.

A grounded user holds two ideas at once: this tool is genuinely useful for some things, and it is genuinely unreliable for others.

### Five common AI task types
Different AI tasks carry different levels of risk, and learning to name them helps you predict how careful you should be.

**Prediction.** Estimating a likely value or category. Examples: predicting the next word in a sentence, predicting whether a transaction is fraud, predicting which email is spam.

**Generation.** Producing new content. Examples: drafting an email, writing a paragraph, generating an image from a description.

**Retrieval.** Surfacing information that exists. Examples: finding a section in a document, looking up a definition, returning a stored answer.

**Transformation.** Converting content from one form to another. Examples: summarizing an article, translating a paragraph, turning notes into a checklist.

**Reasoning support.** Helping you think through a problem. Examples: comparing two options, structuring an argument, surfacing tradeoffs you may have missed.

A single task often combines several of these. Asking AI to "summarize this report and recommend next steps" mixes transformation (summarizing) with reasoning support (recommending). Knowing which is which helps you check the right things.

### Five misconceptions to correct

**Misconception 1: AI is basically a human brain in software.** AI can imitate language and pattern behavior usefully, but that does not mean it thinks, understands, or intends in the way a human does.

**Misconception 2: AI either knows everything or knows nothing.** AI can be strong on some tasks and weak on others. Capability is uneven, not absolute.

**Misconception 3: If AI sounds confident, it must be certain.** Confident output can still contain errors, missing context, or weak reasoning. Confidence is a feature of how the system writes; it is not evidence of correctness.

**Misconception 4: AI is only relevant to technical careers.** Useful AI applications now run across writing, learning, planning, communication, operations, analysis, and decision support. Many of the highest-leverage uses are non-technical.

**Misconception 5: AI replaces the need to think.** Used badly, AI can weaken thinking by encouraging passive acceptance. Used well, it can support deeper thinking by helping you compare, refine, and structure ideas.

## Fully written worked examples

### Worked example 1: An AI explanation that sounds correct but contains errors

**Prompt given to AI:** "What's the best way to study for a final exam in two weeks?"

**AI output:**
> The best strategy is to spend the first week reviewing all your notes, then the second week doing practice questions. Studies have consistently shown that students who use this approach score 27% higher on average. You should also study for at least eight hours per day in the final three days before the exam — this "cramming" phase is essential for moving information into long-term memory. Lastly, avoid sleep the night before the exam so the material stays fresh in your mind.

**What sounds correct.** The output is structured (first week, second week, final three days), uses a specific number (27%), and gives concrete advice. It reads like a competent study guide.

**What is wrong.**
- The "27% higher on average" figure has no source. Specific-looking statistics are one of the things AI invents most readily.
- The claim that cramming "moves information into long-term memory" contradicts well-established education research. Spaced practice and retrieval improve long-term retention; cramming is associated with short-term recall and worse retention later.
- The advice to skip sleep before an exam is harmful. Sleep deprivation impairs the cognitive functions exams test. This is closer to dangerous than to wrong.

**What the learner should notice.** The output is fluent, structured, and confident, and substantial parts of it are wrong or harmful. Fluency is not evidence. When AI gives advice in an area where you can quickly check against credible sources or general knowledge, do that check before acting on it.

### Worked example 2: AI as transformation support

**Input — messy meeting notes a learner pastes in:**
> meeting was about Q3 budget. Janet said marketing needs more — current is 800K shillings, wants 1.2M. Peter pushed back, said sales not justifying. Joyce mentioned the website redesign is dragging — needs separate decision. Action items? unclear. James asked for follow-up by Friday. We didn't decide on the budget. Need another meeting maybe.

**Prompt to AI:** "Turn these meeting notes into a structured summary with decisions, open questions, and action items. Mark anything that wasn't clearly decided as 'Open'."

**AI output:**
> **Decisions:** None reached this meeting.
>
> **Discussion points:**
> - Marketing budget request: increase from KES 800,000 to KES 1.2M (Janet)
> - Pushback from Peter, who noted sales did not justify the higher figure
> - Website redesign timeline raised by Joyce as a separate concern
>
> **Open questions:**
> - Whether to approve the marketing budget increase
> - How to handle the website redesign timeline
>
> **Action items:**
> - James: follow up by Friday (purpose unclear from notes)
> - Group: schedule another meeting to decide on the budget

**What the learner should notice.** This is one of AI's strongest uses. The input had real content, and the output preserved it without invention — it even flagged "purpose unclear" rather than guessing. Transformation tasks where you supply the source material are typically lower-risk than tasks where AI generates content from nothing.

### Worked example 3: AI as classification support

**Input — eight short customer feedback entries:**
1. App keeps crashing when I try to send money.
2. Customer service waited forty minutes to respond.
3. Love the new interface, much cleaner than before.
4. I can't find where to update my phone number.
5. Charges are higher than they were last month.
6. Took three days to verify my account.
7. The dark mode is great.
8. Why is the app so slow on Saturday afternoons?

**Prompt to AI:** "Group these comments into 3-4 categories and give each category a short name."

**AI output:**
> - **Technical performance** (1, 8): App crashes and slow performance.
> - **Account and support** (2, 4, 6): Long waits for customer service and verification, hard to find account settings.
> - **Pricing** (5): Higher charges than expected.
> - **Positive feedback on UI** (3, 7): Users like the new interface and dark mode.

**What the learner should notice.** Classification works well when items are short and the categories are not subtle. A human reviewer would still consider whether item 8 ("slow on Saturdays") really fits "technical performance" or might be a capacity issue worth its own category. Classification is a starting point for analysis, not the conclusion.

### Worked example 4: When AI is asked to overreach

**Prompt given to AI:** "Based on the following text messages between an employee and a manager, decide whether the employee should be terminated."

**Why this prompt is the wrong shape.**
- The text messages may be incomplete; the AI does not have the full context.
- Termination is a high-stakes decision involving HR policy, employment law, contract terms, and human relationships.
- The AI has no access to the employee's history, contract, or company policy.
- The AI is not accountable for the consequences of the decision; the human who acts on it is.

**Better way to frame the same task.** Ask AI to summarize the tone of the messages, identify factual claims that can be verified, and suggest questions a manager should investigate further. Keep the decision human.

**What the learner should notice.** Capability is not the same as authority. Even when AI produces a confident answer, the type of decision matters. Some decisions stay human regardless of how good the AI seems.

## Core lesson

### Why polished output fools people
Humans tend to overvalue fluency. Structured paragraphs, professional tone, and confident phrasing create a feeling of reliability — especially when the reader is tired, rushed, or hoping the answer is right. AI is good at producing fluency. That is part of what makes it useful, and part of what makes it risky.

A useful habit: notice the difference between *presentation quality* (how the output reads) and *content quality* (whether the content holds up). AI can do very well on the first while quietly failing on the second.

### Eight myths you should be able to correct

**Myth 1: If AI sounds smart, it is probably correct.** Reality: AI can produce highly fluent language that feels authoritative while still being incomplete, fabricated, oversimplified, or wrong.

**Myth 2: AI knows everything.** Reality: AI may lack current information, local context, or access to the exact facts needed. A capable answer is not proof of complete knowledge.

**Myth 3: AI replaces thinking.** Reality: Used badly, AI weakens thinking. Used well, it supports thinking by helping you compare, refine, and structure ideas.

**Myth 4: AI is only for programmers or technical people.** Reality: Some uses are technical, but many of the highest-value uses are accessible to non-technical learners, teachers, founders, administrators, and creators.

**Myth 5: AI is either completely neutral or completely biased.** Reality: Neither. AI outputs can reflect skewed framing, missing context, and uneven representation. That does not make every output useless; it makes careful review necessary. (See the bias section below for how to check.)

**Myth 6: If AI saves time, the result is good enough.** Reality: Time saved on drafting can be lost later if the work is inaccurate, inappropriate, or non-compliant.

**Myth 7: AI can take responsibility for mistakes.** Reality: Responsibility stays with the person, team, or institution that acts on the output.

**Myth 8: Using AI is automatically cheating.** Reality: This depends on rules, context, disclosure expectations, and the nature of the task. AI use can be acceptable, valuable, or inappropriate depending on how it is used and whether it replaces required human effort dishonestly.

### Bias in AI outputs — three types you can name and check
Bias is not a single thing. It is several things, and each is checkable.

**Training-data bias.** AI's outputs reflect what is overrepresented in the data the system was trained on. If most CV examples in the training data are from a Western corporate context, AI's first-draft CV advice will default to Western corporate conventions. If most "leadership" examples are male, AI's leadership descriptions may quietly assume male defaults. This is not because the AI dislikes other contexts — it is because the patterns it learned are skewed.

*Learner check:* Ask yourself, *whose context is overrepresented in this output?* Look for cultural, geographic, or demographic defaults that the AI did not name. If you are writing for a Kenyan audience and the output reads as if you are writing for a New York office, that is a sign the AI defaulted to its training-data skew.

**Framing bias.** AI presents one perspective as the default. If you ask "what's the best way to manage a team?" the AI will give one answer, and the framing assumes there is one best way. If you ask "should this policy be allowed?" the AI may default to the framing of one cultural or political tradition.

*Learner check:* Ask yourself, *what assumption is built into the question itself?* Reframe the question to test whether the answer changes. If you also ask "what are three different cultural views on managing a team?" you will see what was hidden in the original framing.

**Presentation bias.** How information is ordered, emphasized, or qualified affects how it lands. AI may put the recommendation it weights most heavily first, or may use stronger language for some options than others.

*Learner check:* Ask yourself, *what is first? what is emphasized? what is hedged?* Reverse the order yourself and see if your sense of the right answer changes. If it does, the order was doing some of the persuading.

A short way to remember the three: *training-data bias* is about whose voice is in the data, *framing bias* is about what the question assumes, and *presentation bias* is about how the answer is shaped. You will see these again in Module 6 (where source bias matters for verification) and Module 9 (where accountability for biased output remains human).

### Common failure modes
Beyond bias, AI has predictable failure modes worth naming:
- Invented facts or citations.
- Confident overclaiming.
- Missing important context.
- Vague or generic advice.
- Weak reasoning hidden inside smooth phrasing.
- Misunderstanding ambiguous prompts.
- Flattening nuance in sensitive topics.
- Poor fit for local policy, law, culture, or organization.
- Inconsistent answers across similar prompts.

Notice that none of these are signs the AI is broken. They are normal behavior of the tool. Knowing them turns review from "what if something is wrong?" into "where would this kind of thing go wrong?"

### The T-R-E-J review model
A simple review you can run on any AI output before you use it.

- **Task** — What is the AI actually helping with?
- **Risk** — What happens if this is wrong?
- **Evidence** — What can be checked against a source, policy, document, or expert?
- **Judgment** — What still depends on human context, ethics, accountability, or decision-making?

As risk rises, the verification effort should rise too. A low-stakes brainstorm is different from a public statement, a medical recommendation, a financial claim, a disciplinary message, or a legal sentence.

## Fully written worked examples

### Worked example 1: Fluent but flawed

**Prompt to AI:** "Write a short paragraph explaining why remote work always increases productivity."

**AI output:**
> Remote work always increases productivity because employees face fewer distractions, avoid commuting, and work in more comfortable environments. Studies consistently prove that remote teams outperform office-based teams in every industry, making remote work the best solution for all companies.

**What sounds strong.** Smooth flow, confident tone, broad professional-sounding claim, no spelling errors.

**What is wrong.**
- Absolute language ("always," "every industry," "best solution for all companies").
- Claims evidence ("studies consistently prove") without showing any.
- Ignores differences across roles, management practices, industries, and home conditions.
- Sounds complete while oversimplifying a contested issue.

**Improved reviewed version:**
> Remote work can improve productivity in some situations by reducing commute time and offering more flexibility, but outcomes vary by role, team practices, management quality, and home environment. A more reliable conclusion would compare contexts rather than assume one result for every organization.

**What the learner should notice.** Polished language can hide weak logic and unsupported claims. The improvement is not just in tone; it is in honesty about what the evidence does and does not support.

### Worked example 2: Useful support, but not final

**Prompt to AI:** "Turn these messy meeting notes into a clean summary with action items."

This is a strong AI use case. The AI is helping organize material humans already produced. It can save time by structuring loose notes into something more usable.

**What still needs human review:**
- Whether decisions were captured accurately.
- Whether action owners are assigned correctly.
- Whether sensitive or unresolved issues were softened too much.
- Whether the tone fits the audience.
- Whether tentative discussion was made to sound final.

**What the learner should notice.** AI often adds real value when it transforms human-supplied material that can be checked easily. The risk is not zero — it is just lower than tasks where the AI has to invent content.

### Worked example 3: A bias example you can name

**Prompt to AI:** "Give me three traits of a strong leader."

**AI output:**
> A strong leader is decisive, confident, and willing to take charge. Strong leaders make difficult calls quickly, maintain composure under pressure, and inspire others through their authority and vision.

**What is wrong with this answer.**
- *Training-data bias:* The traits listed (decisive, confident, takes charge, authority) are heavily associated with one cultural style of leadership — often called "command and control." Other widely-studied leadership styles are absent: collaborative leadership, servant leadership, consensus-based leadership, leadership through quiet expertise.
- *Framing bias:* The question assumed there is one description of a strong leader. The output went along with that framing.
- *Presentation bias:* All listed traits are individual and assertive. Relational traits — listening, building trust, reflecting before deciding — are missing.

**Reframed prompt that surfaces what was hidden:** "Give me three traits of a strong leader, drawing from at least two different cultural or organizational traditions."

**What the learner should notice.** Bias in AI is not always obvious. Often it shows up as a default that feels normal until you ask what is missing. The check is to reframe the question and see what changes.

### Worked example 4: High-stakes, sensitive context

**Prompt to AI:** "Draft a public response to a customer complaint involving possible harm."

**Why caution is needed.** Even if the draft sounds professional, the stakes are too high for blind use. A public response can create legal, reputational, ethical, or operational consequences.

**What human review must consider:**
- Factual accuracy of the claim and the response.
- Admission language that could create legal exposure.
- Company policy and what may or may not be said publicly.
- Empathy and appropriateness of tone.
- Escalation obligations (insurance, legal, regulator notification).
- Brand voice.

**What the learner should notice.** A polished draft is not the same as an acceptable draft. Some contexts require a kind of review the AI cannot do.

## Module purpose
Most learners improve their prompts once and then plateau. They either accept the first usable response, regenerate randomly, or blame the model when the real issue is workflow. ## Core lesson

### Iteration as a normal workflow
Strong AI users rarely stop at the first draft when the task matters. The first answer typically reveals what your original prompt left underdefined: the audience may not be specified, the tone may be off, the format may not match what you need, or the AI may have included content that should be excluded.

Revision often addresses one of these:
- Tone mismatch.
- Missing context.
- Weak structure.
- Generic content.
- Audience misfit.
- Too much or too little detail.
- Unsupported claims.
- Weak actionability.

A useful habit: name the weakness before naming the fix. "The output is too formal for a peer audience" leads to a sharper revision than "I want it to sound better."

### When to revise versus when to start over
Revision is right when the output has a usable core and one or two specific weaknesses you can name. Start over when:
- You realize the original prompt was misframed (you asked for the wrong thing).
- The output is so far off that revising would cost more than restarting.
- The conversation has accumulated context that is now confusing the AI.
- You are about to revise the prompt for the fourth or fifth time on the same problem — that is a sign the prompt needs to be redesigned, not patched.

Knowing when to start over is part of the skill. Endless revision can be its own form of waste.

### Output comparison as a diagnostic tool
When you have two or three outputs from different prompt versions, compare them on more than style. Look at:
- Clarity.
- Audience fit.
- Structural usefulness.
- Factual caution.
- Risk control.
- Actionability.

Without comparison, learners often overvalue whichever answer came last. A side-by-side check protects against that.

### Revision moves
Common moves you can make to a prompt:
- Clarify the audience.
- Add structure to the request.
- Narrow the task.
- Add source boundaries (use only the notes below).
- Add exclusions (do not include X).
- Request options instead of one answer.
- Define tone more precisely.
- Require shorter or more usable formatting.
- Ask the AI to flag missing information rather than guess.

Each move should respond to an observed weakness, not be added because more words feel safer. Adding instruction without diagnosis is a different kind of waste.

### Prompt chains for multi-step work
When a task contains multiple distinct jobs, a single prompt often hides them. Splitting the task across stages gives you better control. A typical chain:
1. Summarize source material.
2. Extract key points.
3. Draft output for a specific audience.
4. Review against constraints.
5. Tighten for final use.

Each step has its own prompt. Each step's output becomes input for the next. The advantage is that you can inspect each stage before moving forward, and you can rerun a single stage without redoing the whole task.

### Reviewable workflow design
Generation and review are different jobs. A reviewable workflow separates them:
- Prompt for first draft.
- Prompt for structure cleanup.
- Prompt for identifying uncertainty or weak areas.
- Human check for facts, fit, tone, and consequences.
- Final revision or approval.

Reviewable workflows matter most when outputs affect other people — customers, learners, colleagues, regulators. The goal is not bureaucracy. The goal is that you can see, before you send, what was generated and what was checked.

## Fully written worked examples

### Worked example 1: Email revision workflow

**Task.** Drafting an email to customers about a one-day delivery delay.

**Version 1 prompt:**
> Write an email to customers about a delivery delay.

**Likely result.** Polite but vague. Possibly too defensive or too casual. May promise things the business cannot deliver.

**Version 2 prompt:**
> Write a short customer email explaining that deliveries will arrive one day late. Keep the tone calm and professional, apologize briefly, and end with a clear reassurance that orders are still being processed.

**Improvement.** The output is now controlled in tone and structure.

**Version 3 prompt:**
> Write a short customer email explaining that deliveries will arrive one day late. Keep the tone calm and professional, apologize briefly, reassure customers that their orders are still being processed, and do not promise compensation or same-day resolution unless explicitly stated.

**Improvement.** The result is now safer too. The exclusion ("do not promise compensation or same-day resolution") prevents the AI from making commitments the business has not authorized.

**What the learner should notice.** Each revision improves something specific. Version 2 fixes tone and structure. Version 3 fixes risk control. Both moves come from naming the weakness.

### Worked example 2: Study workflow chain (with the actual prompts)

**Task.** Turning lesson notes into revision support for a learner studying for an exam.

**Step 1 — summarize the lesson notes in plain language.**
> Read the notes below. Summarize the lesson in plain language in 200 words or fewer. Use the same level of detail a beginner would need. Do not add facts that are not in the notes.
>
> [paste notes]

**Step 2 — identify likely misconceptions from the same notes.**
> Based on the notes below, list three to five misconceptions a learner is likely to have about this topic. For each misconception, write one sentence stating the misconception and one sentence correcting it.
>
> [paste notes]

**Step 3 — generate short quiz questions.**
> Based on the notes below, write five short-answer quiz questions that test understanding of the main ideas. Avoid yes/no questions. Avoid questions about facts that are not in the notes.
>
> [paste notes]

**Step 4 — rewrite the explanation for a beginner learner.**
> Rewrite the summary you produced in Step 1 for a learner who has never seen this material before. Use everyday language and one concrete example for each main idea. Keep it under 250 words.

**What the learner should notice.** A single large prompt asking for "summary, misconceptions, questions, and beginner explanation" works less well than four focused prompts. Each step has a clear, narrow job. You can inspect the misconceptions before generating questions, and you can rerun the beginner rewrite without redoing the misconceptions.

### Worked example 3: Weak iteration habit

A user writes a vague prompt, dislikes the answer, and clicks regenerate four times without changing anything.

**Why this is weak practice.**
- No diagnosis of what is wrong.
- No increased control over the output.
- No learning from the previous attempts.
- No reason to expect the next answer to be more usable than the last.

**What the learner should notice.** Regeneration without diagnosis produces motion without improvement. Closing the gap between attempts requires naming what was wrong with the previous output and changing the prompt to address it.

### Worked example 4: Revision based on reviewability

**Task.** Drafting an internal staff update.

The first answer sounds good but mixes confirmed facts with assumptions. The user revises the prompt:

**Revised prompt:**
> Use only the facts provided in the notes below. If information is missing, state what still needs confirmation rather than filling the gap. Mark any sentence based on inference with "(to confirm)".

**What the learner should notice.** Revision can improve trustworthiness, not just clarity. The "(to confirm)" instruction makes the staff update easier to review because the reviewer can see exactly which sentences still need verification.

## Module purpose

## Learner outcomes
By the end of this module, you should be able to:

## Core lesson

### Why evidence matters
AI often predicts plausible language patterns. That means it can produce text that resembles informed writing without actually performing reliable evidence judgment on its own. The danger is not that AI lies — it is that AI is fluent, and fluency can substitute for reliability in the reader's mind.

The basic move: separate *sounding informed* from *being supported*. Sounding informed is a writing achievement. Being supported is a verification achievement.

### Three types of hallucination, named
Hallucination is wider than fake citations. Three types are worth distinguishing.

**Invented citations and statistics.** The AI generates a reference, study name, author, or specific number that does not exist or that does not say what the AI claims. This is the type most people learn about first. The check is to look up the source. If you cannot find it, treat the claim as unsupported regardless of how authoritative the citation looks.

**Hallucinated reasoning.** The AI produces a confident chain of reasoning that does not actually hold up when traced step-by-step. The phrasing sounds logical, but the inferences depend on assumptions the AI did not state, or contain hidden contradictions. The check is to walk through the reasoning yourself: does each step actually follow from the previous one? Where does the conclusion sneak in?

**Hallucinated procedural steps.** The AI gives instructions for how to do something — set up a tool, file a form, take a regulatory action — that include steps that do not exist or are wrong. The check is to compare the instructions against the actual tool, the actual form, or the actual regulation. AI gives plausible-looking procedures, especially for systems it has trained on but cannot currently see.

A useful summary: AI can invent *what was said* (citations and stats), *how it follows* (reasoning), and *what to do* (procedural steps). All three are common. The check is similar in shape — go look — but the targets differ.

### Source-bounded prompting
A practical move that reduces hallucination on a wide class of tasks. Instead of asking the AI to draw from its training, you give it the source material and bound it.

Examples of source-bounding language:
- *Use only the notes below.*
- *Base the answer only on the provided document.*
- *Do not invent sources or data.*
- *If information is missing, state what is unclear rather than filling the gap.*
- *Distinguish confirmed facts from likely interpretation.*

Source-bounding is especially useful for summarization, policy explanation, note transformation, drafting from supplied materials, and reviewable internal work.

### How to actually verify
Naming verification is the easy part. Doing it well is a skill.

**Search literacy.** When you check a claim, search for the most specific phrasing the AI used. If the AI quoted a study, search for the study's title in quotes. If the AI gave a statistic, search for the statistic plus a likely source name. If nothing comes up, that is a signal the claim may be invented.

**Primary-source preference.** When you can, go to the source itself, not to a summary of the source. AI summaries of policies, laws, or research papers can drift. The original document — the regulation, the published paper, the official report — is what you should rely on for anything that matters.

**Paywalled or hard-to-access material.** When you cannot reach the primary source, two reasonable moves: search for a credible secondary source that quotes the original (a major newspaper, a peer-reviewed review article, an official summary), or treat the claim as unverified and either remove it or label it accordingly. Do not use AI's confidence as a substitute for access.

**When verification cost exceeds claim value.** Not every sentence needs the same verification effort. The C-S-V-E model exists because verification should be proportional to risk. For a low-stakes brainstorm, light checking is enough. For a public communication, a financial figure, or a policy claim, verification effort should rise.

### The C-S-V-E model
A simple verification workflow.

- **Claim** — What specific statement matters most?
- **Source** — What source, document, policy, or evidence should support it?
- **Verify** — Does the source actually say that?
- **Escalate** — If the claim is high-stakes, unclear, or unsupported, who or what should review it next?

Apply C-S-V-E to the claims that matter, not to every sentence.

### When to escalate
Some uses should not rely on AI alone. Examples:
- Medical guidance with possible patient harm.
- Legal language with binding effect.
- Disciplinary or HR decisions affecting a person's standing.
- Formal policy interpretation.
- Public-facing claims involving safety, money, or harm.

Escalation is not a failure mode. It is part of responsible use. The skill is recognizing it before the output is sent.

## Fully written worked examples

### Worked example 1: A fabricated-sounding statistic in a business brief

**AI-style sentence:**
> Studies show that 78% of customers permanently switch brands after a single delayed order.

**Why it feels persuasive.** Specific number, "studies show" framing, plausible enough to be repeated.

**What is wrong.**
- No source given.
- The figure may be exaggerated or invented.
- The precision (78%, not "most") creates false trust.

**What the learner should notice.** Specific numbers without sources should *increase* caution, not reduce it. If you cannot find the study with a focused search, do not quote the figure.

### Worked example 2: A citation that looks real but cannot be verified

**AI-style sentence:**
> According to Johnson and Rivera's 2022 *Journal of Consumer Trust* study, apology speed predicts repurchase behavior more than refund value.

**Why this is risky.** Named authors, named journal, scholarly structure. A reader skimming for a brief or report would not stop to verify.

**What the learner should do.** Search for the study in academic databases or via the journal's website. If neither the journal nor the study appears, do not repeat the citation. Rewrite the claim more cautiously, or remove it.

### Worked example 3: A strong source-bounded summary

**Prompt:**
> Using only the meeting notes below, summarize confirmed decisions, action items, and open questions. Do not add assumptions. If information is unclear, label it as unresolved.

**Why this is strong.** The source boundary is explicit. Overreach is controlled. The prompt structures the output. Invented certainty is prevented.

**What the learner should notice.** Strong grounding often comes from narrowing the source universe. The AI is a much more reliable summarizer when it cannot drift outside the supplied material.

### Worked example 4: Summary drift

A learner asks AI to summarize a lesson but does not paste the lesson. The AI produces a polished explanation that includes claims not in the original.

**What the learner should notice.** Even summaries can become partly synthetic when the source is not provided. The fix is to provide the actual material and bound the AI to it.

### Worked example 5: Hallucinated reasoning (new)

**Prompt:**
> Should a small Kenyan business choose to be VAT-registered if its annual turnover is below the registration threshold?

**AI output:**
> Yes, registering for VAT below the threshold is generally beneficial because it allows the business to claim input VAT, signal credibility to corporate clients, and avoid surprise registration penalties later. Most accountants recommend voluntary registration for any business above KES 3 million in revenue, since the input VAT recovery typically exceeds the additional compliance cost.

**Why it sounds reasonable.** Confident structure, specific numbers, plausible reasoning trail.

**What the learner should check.** Walk the reasoning step by step. The "claim input VAT" benefit only applies if the business has significant taxable purchases — for service businesses with low input costs, this benefit may be small. The "avoid surprise registration penalties" claim assumes a regulatory regime that may or may not match Kenya's actual VAT rules. The "KES 3 million" threshold for voluntary registration is presented as an accountants' rule of thumb but no source is given. Each step in the reasoning depends on an unstated assumption.

**What the learner should notice.** Reasoning hallucination is harder to spot than citation hallucination because there is no specific reference to look up. The check is the same in shape — go look — but you are checking the assumptions, not the citations. For tax advice that affects a business, the next step is a qualified tax professional, not the AI.

### Worked example 6: Hallucinated procedural steps (new)

**Prompt:**
> Walk me through how to apply for a Kenyan Personal Identification Number (PIN) on the iTax portal.

**AI output (excerpt):**
> 1. Go to the iTax portal at itax.kra.go.ke and click "New PIN Registration."
> 2. Select "Individual" as the taxpayer type.
> 3. Fill in your details and upload a copy of your national ID and a passport-sized photo.
> 4. Submit the form. You will receive your PIN by email within 24 hours.

**Why it sounds reasonable.** Numbered steps, plausible URL, specific actions.

**What is wrong.** Step 3's "passport-sized photo" upload requirement may not match the current iTax process. Step 4's "PIN by email within 24 hours" timing may be wrong. Procedures change. AI does not know what changed last week.

**What the learner should do.** Use the AI output as a starting outline, then go to the actual iTax portal or the KRA's current guidance and verify each step before acting. For procedural tasks involving regulators, banks, or institutions, AI is a sketch, not a specification.

- The repeated sixteen-times "Intervene only if the learner shows overtrust…" paragraph.
- The "Premium completion upgrade" header and content block.
- The repeated "Self-learner success path" / "Evidence of completion" / "Automated/self-paced checkpoint recommendation" / "Minimal facilitator intervention points" five-block tail.
- The "Validation and quality assurance checklist" and "Validation check" sections.
- The phrase "It is aligned to the same premium authoring standard."
- Any sentence whose only function is to compliment the course on itself.

The Misconceptions, Differentiation, and Facilitator-intervention guidance is moved to a separate `Course1_Facilitator_Guide.md`. The Validation Checklist content is moved to a separate `Course1_QA_Checklist.md`. The per-module checkpoint specifications are moved to `Course1_Assessment_Standards.md`.

## 9. What employability and pathway language should look like
Each module includes one explicit pathway connection — a sentence or short paragraph that names which Jifunze pathways the module's artifact most directly supports (Digital Work Starter, AI Productivity Professional, Remote Work and Freelancing, Small Business and Entrepreneurship, Junior Tech Builder). The language is concrete: *"This artifact is a usable proof point for the Digital Work Starter pathway, where learners need to demonstrate they can apply AI safely in office and operations roles."* Avoid claims about getting jobs, earning a specific income, or being certified to a level the course does not actually certify. The course produces evidence the learner can use; it does not guarantee outcomes.

## 10. What ready-for-Cursor-mapping means
A module is ready for platform mapping when:

- It can be split into the eight platform lesson blocks (overview, concept, guided example, practice, reflection, checkpoint, artifact upload, remediation) without rewriting the core teaching.
- Every checkpoint question has a structure suitable for UI conversion (clear stem, defined response format, machine-checkable or rubric-checkable answer).
- The portfolio artifact has a defined upload format (.pdf, .docx, .md) and a filename convention.
- The transition into the next module is a single short paragraph that fits as a closing UI block.
- The remediation guidance is specific enough to power an automated "review section X" link rather than a generic "study more" prompt.
- The module's text contains no facilitator-only instructions, no "ask the group," and no embedded references to in-person delivery.

When a module meets all of items 1 through 10, it has reached the benchmark. The first four modules above are the reference. Every subsequent rewrite, improvement, or polish target should be checked against this list before it is considered done.
