# Course 1 Rewrite Plan

*AI Essentials — Repair-and-upgrade plan based on the Course 1 audit. No lesson content is rewritten in this document. Each work order specifies what must be produced; production happens after this plan is approved.*

---

## 1. Course-level repair strategy

Course 1 has a strong half (Modules 1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 at the level of depth Jifunze should ship) and a thin half (Modules 3, 4, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 written as outlines, not lessons). The rewrite strategy is therefore not a fresh start — it is **leveling**: bring the thin modules up to the depth of the strong modules without disturbing what already works.

The strategy has six components:

**Level the depth.** Every module reaches the same authoring standard already visible in Module 2: full worked examples with actual prompts and outputs shown, supplied scenarios for every practice activity, six written checkpoint questions plus an applied scenario, and a model answer guide a self-learner can use without a facilitator.

**Repair the seam at Module 9.** Module 9 currently introduces itself as the course finale ("This concluding module consolidates the course… Reconnect modules 1–9 into one operating model"). It will be reframed as the responsibility bridge into the applications arc. The synthesis function moves to the Capstone (Module 16).

**Reposition Privacy earlier.** Privacy and operational boundaries (currently Module 15) belong before high-volume application work, not after. Privacy moves to position 10 so learners build safety discipline before they research, plan workflows, support decisions, and build reusable systems.

**Fold missing topics into existing modules where possible.** Bias/fairness folds into Module 2 (which already addresses it as a single myth) and Module 9. AI agents fold into Module 12 (Workflows + Agents). AI for job search threads through Modules 7, 8, and 15, plus a dedicated section in the course-level pathway guide. This avoids inflating the course past 16 modules and keeps integration tight.

**Strip the boilerplate tail and replace it with module-specific transitions.** The repeated five-block tail at the end of every module ("Premium completion upgrade," "Self-learner success path," etc., plus the verbatim 16× "Intervene only if the learner…" paragraph) gets removed from learner-facing content and either relocated to a separate facilitator/QA guide or replaced with a forward-pointing transition into the next module.

**Add the missing course-level scaffolding.** A self-learner guide, a tool stance with a beginner setup walkthrough, a certificate-readiness page, a portfolio guide, a pathway map, a responsible-AI disclosure note, and a capstone preparation guide. These are the seven course-level documents currently missing.

The result: a course that flows from foundational mental model → prompting craft → verification → applied communication and learning → responsibility and privacy → applied work → capstone, where every module produces a portfolio artifact that the learner can map back to a Jifunze pathway.

**Why Module 2 is renamed.** The current title ("AI Myths, Reality, and Responsible Judgment") doesn't signal that bias and fairness are addressed. Bias is currently one of eight myths and deserves equal weight. The renamed module covers myths, reality, **bias as a distinct topic**, and judgment.

**Why Privacy moves to position 10.** A learner who reaches Module 11 (research) without privacy discipline will paste sensitive material into an AI tool while working through research practice activities. Safety habits should precede application volume. M10 also functions as the cleanest entry point into the applications arc because it forces learners to think about inputs before they think about outputs.

**Why Module 12 absorbs Agents instead of becoming its own module.** Agents in 2026 are essentially automated multi-step workflows. Treating them as a separate module would mean teaching the same concepts twice. Folding agents into the workflow module preserves the 16-module count and lets learners see the progression: manual workflow → templated workflow → reusable workflow → agentic workflow. The agent-readiness portion of the artifact gives learners a credible, current capability to demonstrate.

**Why bias is folded into Module 2 rather than getting its own module.** A standalone bias module pulls bias out of the judgment frame where it belongs. Bias is a judgment issue, not a separate doctrine. The audit found bias under-treated; the fix is more depth in Module 2 plus reinforcement threads in Module 6 (source bias) and Module 9 (accountability for bias). Adding a 17th module would break the count and create an artificial silo.

**Why job-search content is threaded, not modularized.** Job-search writing is communication (M7). Studying for an interview or skill assessment is learning (M8). Building a personal AI toolkit usable on the job is reusable systems (M15). Applying AI safely in a workplace is privacy (M10). The natural placement is integration across modules, with a dedicated "AI for Job Search" section in the course-level pathway guide (see Section 7).

**Why Module 9 stays at position 9 instead of moving.** Moving Module 9 disrupts more modules than reframing it. The fix is wording: Module 9 stops claiming to be the finale and instead positions itself as the responsibility checkpoint between foundational use (Modules 1–8) and applied use (Modules 10–15). The Capstone takes over the synthesis function.

### Module 3 — Prompts as Control (Rewrite)

**What is missing now.** The teaching parts each have 2–6 lines of body text. The four worked examples are listed by title only ("From 'Write a message about late assignments' to a specific audience-aware email") with no prompts or outputs shown. The four practice activities are named without instructions. The checkpoint is a single line: "Knowledge check plus an applied rewrite of: 'Write something to customers about a delay.'"

**What must be written.** Five teaching parts at 200–350 words each, each with at least one inline mini-example. The T-C-C-F-A model expanded with a one-paragraph definition per element and an inline weak-vs-strong demonstration of each.

**Required worked examples (4, fully written).**
1. Late-assignment email to students — show the weak prompt, the AI output, the analysis, the strengthened prompt with audience and constraint, the new output, the side-by-side comparison.
2. Lesson summary versus revision-bullet-plus-quiz prompt — show both versions and the learner-facing reasoning for the difference.
3. Generic business message versus context-rich WhatsApp customer-delay message — show the prompt context block (sender, customer relationship, what they ordered, the delay reason), the two outputs, and review notes.
4. Performance review feedback with evidence boundaries — show the weak prompt, the failure mode it produces (invented examples), the strengthened prompt with explicit evidence sources, and the corrected output.

**Required practice tasks (4, with supplied materials).**
1. Prompt improvement lab — supply 4 weak prompts; learner produces 3 graded improvements per prompt, each with one specific T-C-C-F-A element strengthened.
2. Output comparison challenge — supply 2 prompt-output pairs; learner identifies which prompt change produced which output difference.
3. Context vs no-context exercise — supply one task; learner runs it twice with their AI tool, once with no context and once with full context, and submits both outputs plus a one-paragraph analysis.
4. Constraint design drill — supply 3 tasks; learner adds at least 3 well-chosen constraints to each and explains the choice.

**Required checkpoint (6 questions + applied scenario, with model answers).** Three knowledge questions on the T-C-C-F-A model, two scenario questions identifying which element is missing in a weak prompt, one scenario on when more detail starts to clutter, and one applied rewrite of the customer-delay prompt with a model answer that shows the engineered version, the reasoning, and what a reviewer would still check.

**Portfolio artifact.** Before/after prompt rewrite (one example, fully developed) plus a reusable prompt contract template the learner adapts to one task they actually do at work or in study.

**Transition into next module.** A 2–3 sentence forward-pointer: Module 3 introduced the prompt as the control surface; Module 4 takes the same control surface and engineers it deliberately, adding role, output design, and review cues to the T-C-C-F-A foundation.

### Module 4 — Prompt Engineering — Structure, Constraints, and Output Design (Rewrite)

**What is missing now.** Same disease as Module 3. R-T-C-C-O-R model named without depth. Four worked example titles without bodies. Four practice activities named without specifications. Checkpoint is one line.

**What must be written.** Five teaching parts. The R-T-C-C-O-R model expanded into a one-paragraph definition per element. A clear teaching of when prompt detail becomes clutter (currently named as a learning outcome but never taught). Output design taught as a first-class skill: tables, checklists, summaries, briefs, and step-by-step outputs each with a worked instance.

**Required worked examples (4, fully written).**
1. Study aid prompt redesign — supply messy lecture notes, show a basic prompt, show the engineered prompt with role, output format, and review cue, and show the resulting study aid.
2. Customer communication about a price increase — show the engineered prompt with explicit constraints (no compensation language, no apology language, professional but warm tone), and the output.
3. Review-ready feedback from performance notes — show the engineered prompt that includes evidence-source rules and an explicit "flag missing evidence" instruction, and the output.
4. Cluttered prompt → clean engineered version — show a 400-word over-specified prompt that produces worse output than a 150-word focused one, and explain why.

**Required practice tasks (4, with supplied materials).**
1. Prompt engineering makeover — supply 3 weak prompts; learner produces an engineered version of each using R-T-C-C-O-R with documented reasoning.
2. Output design lab — supply one source document; learner generates four different outputs (table, checklist, brief, step-by-step) from the same source using prompts that differ only in output design.
3. Constraint selection drill — supply 5 tasks; learner picks the 3 most useful constraints for each and justifies the choice.
4. Reusable prompt pack creation — learner builds 3 reusable prompt patterns (summarization, rewrite-for-audience, compare-options) and tests each against a fresh scenario.

**Required checkpoint.** Three knowledge questions on R-T-C-C-O-R, two on when constraints stop helping, one applied engineering of a weak prompt, with model answers showing the reasoning trail.

**Portfolio artifact.** Structured prompt template (the learner's own R-T-C-C-O-R template, filled in for one real task they do) plus three reusable patterns from the prompt pack creation activity.

**Transition.** Module 4 builds the static structure; Module 5 puts it into motion with iteration and comparison.

### Module 11 — AI for Research, Analysis, and Synthesis (Rewrite)

**What is missing now.** Five teaching parts of 1–4 lines each. Four worked examples by title only. Four practice activity names without specifications. Checkpoint described, not written.

**What must be written.** Full teaching of the research-assist workflow: define question → collect sources → summarize each source separately → compare and cluster → mark uncertainties → draft synthesis → human review. The distinction between exploratory synthesis and evidence-backed conclusion taught explicitly with criteria. Anti-hallucination practice for analytical work: separating known facts, claims needing verification, assumptions, and open questions.

**Required worked examples (4, fully written).**
1. Comparing three source excerpts — supply the three excerpts; show the AI-assisted theme comparison; show what was preserved and what was flattened.
2. Turning interview notes into theme clusters — supply 5 short interview snippets; show the cluster output; show one example of an invented cluster relationship the learner must catch.
3. Weak synthesis that sounds stronger than the evidence — show the over-confident synthesis paragraph; show the corrected version with hedged language and uncertainty markers.
4. Better analytical brief with uncertainty language — show the full structure (question, scope, evidence summary, patterns, disagreements, open questions) populated with one realistic example.

**Required practice tasks (4, with supplied materials).**
1. Source-to-theme map — supply 4 short documents; learner produces a theme map with traceability back to source.
2. Question framing rewrite — supply 5 vague research questions; learner rewrites each into a scope-limited, answerable form.
3. Compare-and-contrast brief — supply 3 source excerpts; learner produces a brief with separated themes, contrasts, and uncertainties.
4. Uncertainty labeling exercise — supply one AI-generated analysis paragraph; learner labels every claim as known/needs-verification/assumption/open question.

**Required checkpoint.** Three knowledge questions on the research-assist workflow and uncertainty handling, two scenario questions on detecting weak synthesis, one applied task producing a small synthesis brief from a supplied source pack.

**Portfolio artifact.** Research scope brief, source comparison grid, and evidence-based synthesis brief — all from one real or supplied research question.

**Transition.** Module 11 produces analysis; Module 12 turns analysis into operational workflows that can be repeated and, eventually, automated.

### Module 12 — AI for Workflows, Automation, and Agents (Rewrite + expand)

**What is missing now.** Five teaching parts of 1–4 lines each. Four worked example titles, no bodies. No coverage of agents at all. No failure-point analysis, despite this being named as a Premium upgrade.

**What must be written.** Five teaching parts. Workflow design taught as: stages, sequence, dependencies, owners, review points, realistic constraints. Failure-point analysis: where workflows speed up the wrong work, where AI introduces new error modes, where review points are mis-placed. **A new agents section**: what an agent is in plain language (an AI workflow that makes its own next-step decisions), when agents are appropriate (repeatable, low-stakes, reversible tasks), when they are not (high-stakes, irreversible, ambiguous-judgment tasks), and a basic agent-readiness assessment for a learner's own task.

**Required worked examples (5, fully written — 1 added for agents).**
1. Event planning workflow — show the full stage map, the AI-assist decision table (what AI does, what humans do, what is reviewed and when), the SOP draft.
2. Monthly reporting process — show the workflow with concrete prompt stages, sample outputs, and review checkpoints.
3. Customer complaint handling flow — show the workflow with explicit escalation triggers and human-only steps.
4. Weak plan that ignores real constraints — show a plausible-looking workflow that fails when applied to a real timing or authority constraint.
5. Workflow that becomes an agent — show one of the above workflows redesigned for partial automation with explicit guardrails (what the agent can do, what requires human approval, what triggers a stop).

**Required practice tasks (4).**
1. Task breakdown lab — supply 3 vague objectives; learner produces a staged workflow for each with sequence, dependencies, and review points.
2. Dependency mapping — supply one workflow; learner identifies hidden dependencies and constraints.
3. Weak-plan diagnosis — supply 3 plans with embedded failure points; learner identifies and explains each.
4. Agent-readiness assessment — learner picks one of their own workflows and runs it through a 6-question agent-readiness check.

**Required checkpoint.** Three knowledge questions on workflow design, two on failure points, one on agent-readiness criteria, with model answers.

**Portfolio artifact.** Workflow map, AI-assist decision table, reviewable SOP, and a one-page agent-readiness assessment for one of the learner's tasks.

**Transition.** Module 12 turns work into a process; Module 13 takes process logic into the territory of judgment and decisions.

**Required checkpoint.** Three knowledge questions on the decision frame, two scenario questions on detecting framing bias, one applied decision memo task.

## 8. Employability and pathway alignment

Course 1 is foundational; every Jifunze pathway should pass through it. The alignment differs by pathway in *which* Course 1 artifacts matter most and *what comes next*.

**Digital Work Starter Pathway.** Course 1 builds the baseline: prompt skill, verification habits, communication fit, privacy discipline, responsible use. Most relevant artifacts: Module 7 audience-fit rewrites (workplace communication), Module 9 guardrail checklist (responsible use), Module 10 safety checklist (operational privacy), Module 12 workflow SOP (entry-level process work). Course 1 graduates this pathway by demonstrating they can use AI competently and safely in any digital-work setting.

**AI Productivity Professional Pathway.** Course 1 is the first proof point; this pathway extends with deeper prompt engineering, multi-agent workflows, and domain specialization. Most relevant artifacts: Modules 3–5 prompt engineering work, Module 11 synthesis brief, Module 12 workflow SOP and agent-readiness assessment, Module 15 prompt pack, Module 16 capstone. Course 1 graduates this pathway by demonstrating disciplined, reusable, agent-aware AI practice.

**Remote Work and Freelancing Pathway.** Course 1 builds the operational discipline a freelancer needs to deliver AI-assisted work credibly to clients. Most relevant artifacts: Module 7 communication rewrites including the job-search artifact (client outreach), Module 9 accountability statement (client-facing professionalism), Module 10 safety checklist (handling client data responsibly), Module 14 team agreement adapted for client-collaboration use, Module 15 prompt pack (the learner's deliverable toolkit), Module 16 capstone (showcase of an end-to-end client workflow). Course 1 graduates this pathway by producing a portfolio a freelancer can show to a first client.

**Small Business and Entrepreneurship Pathway.** Course 1 helps a founder use AI without exposing the business to risk. Most relevant artifacts: Module 7 communication (customer-facing writing), Module 9 guardrail checklist (founder accountability), Module 10 safety checklist (business data handling), Module 11 synthesis brief (market and competitor research), Module 12 workflow SOP including agent-readiness (operational efficiency), Module 13 decision memo (founder decisions). Course 1 graduates this pathway by showing the founder can run AI-assisted operations safely and credibly.

**Junior Tech Builder Pathway.** Course 1 builds the AI fluency that precedes tool-building, even though Course 1 is not a coding course. Most relevant artifacts: Modules 3–5 prompt engineering (the technical foundation for anyone integrating AI), Module 6 verification (essential for builders shipping AI features), Module 10 safety (essential for builders handling user data), Module 12 workflow SOP and agent-readiness (the design pattern for AI products), Module 15 prompt pack (the asset library a builder reuses across projects). Course 1 graduates this pathway by providing the responsible-use foundation that every later technical course assumes.

**Cross-pathway threading.** Every module's transition section names which pathway it most directly serves. The Pathway Map document gives learners a one-page view of how their work in Course 1 maps to their chosen pathway and what Course 2 in that pathway addresses next.

## 9. Capstone repair plan

Module 16 has the right structure (Appendix C rubric exists, the submission spec is clear) but cannot currently be completed by a self-learner. The repair plan has five components.

**Fully worked capstone example.** At least one — preferably two — complete capstone walkthroughs included in the module. Each shows: problem framing (user, task, constraints, success criteria, risks), workflow design (stages, AI roles, prompt stages, source boundaries, review checks, safety boundaries, audiences, approval points), the actual prompts written for each workflow stage, sample outputs, the review and verification work performed, the revision after self-critique, and a final reflection. Recommended examples: one study-workflow capstone (e.g., a learner preparing for a certification) and one communication-workflow capstone (e.g., a small-business owner setting up a customer-response process).

**Self-critique fallback.** A self-critique guide that produces what a peer or facilitator would produce. Built around the rubric: for each rubric criterion, two or three diagnostic questions the learner asks themselves, plus a "what does strong evidence look like for this criterion" reference and a "what does weak evidence look like" reference. The fallback should also include a 24-hour rule: complete the workflow, walk away for a day, then return for self-critique with fresh eyes.

**Rubric improvements.** The current Appendix C rubric (problem framing, prompt/workflow design, verification and review, safety and privacy, reusability, reflection and improvement, with weights) is structurally sound. Improvements: add an annotated example of strong and weak evidence at each criterion level, add a fail-state for each criterion (what disqualifies the work entirely vs what merely weakens it), and add a sixth criterion or merge: **transfer** — can the workflow be reused, taught, or adapted by someone else. The Module 16 Premium upgrade already names this transfer requirement; the rubric should reflect it explicitly.

**Certificate-readiness criteria.** Course 1 certificate eligibility requires: completion of all 16 module checkpoints at 80% or higher, submission of all 16 module artifacts plus the capstone, and a capstone that scores at or above the rubric's "strong evidence" threshold on every criterion. The certificate explicitly names what the learner can do (e.g., "use AI productively in writing, learning, research, decision-support, and workflow design while maintaining verification, privacy, and accountability standards") and what it does not certify (e.g., domain expertise, advanced prompt engineering, AI engineering or development).

**Portfolio packaging.** The capstone is the centerpiece of the Course 1 portfolio, but the portfolio is not just the capstone. The portfolio packaging guide (see Section 7) walks the learner through assembling all 16 module artifacts plus the capstone into a single cohesive portfolio, with a one-paragraph context note per artifact, the certificate, the disclosure statement, and a one-page learner self-introduction tied to a Jifunze pathway. Final portfolio is the artifact a learner shows to an employer, client, or admissions reviewer.

## 10. Rewrite order

The recommended sequence balances dependency (build foundations before consumers), risk (fix the highest-impact thin modules first), and momentum (alternate heavy rewrites with lighter work).

**Phase 1 — Course-level scaffolding (week 1–2).**
- Self-Learner Guide
- Tool Stance and Beginner Setup
- Certificate Readiness
- Portfolio Guide
- Pathway Map
- Disclosure Note
- Capstone Preparation Guide
- QA Checklist (relocated from per-module boilerplate)
- Facilitator Guide (relocated)
- Assessment Standards (relocated)

These are the floor everything else rests on. Until the self-learner guide and pathway map exist, the modules don't have a coherent product context to point into.

**Phase 2 — Prompt foundations rewrite (week 2–3).**
- Module 3 — Prompts as Control (rewrite)
- Module 4 — Prompt Engineering — Structure (rewrite)

These two modules are the heart of the course's promise (practical prompt skill) and currently the weakest. Fix here unblocks the rest.

**Phase 3 — Safety repositioning (week 3).**
- Module 10 — Privacy, Risk, Boundaries (rewrite + reposition from M15)

Done before the application modules so safety discipline is in place when learners do high-volume practice.

**Phase 4 — Application modules rewrite (week 3–5).**
- Module 11 — Research, Analysis, Synthesis (rewrite)
- Module 12 — Workflows, Automation, Agents (rewrite + expand)
- Module 13 — Decision Support (rewrite)

Sequenced research → workflow → decision because that is the natural learner progression: gather → process → choose.

**Phase 5 — Collaboration and reusability rewrite (week 5–6).**
- Module 14 — Teams and Organizations (rewrite)
- Module 15 — Reusable AI Systems (rewrite)

Module 15 is the bridge into the capstone, so it must be solid before Module 16 work begins.

**Phase 6 — Improve modules (week 6–7).**
- Module 7 — Writing and Communication (improve, add job-search thread)
- Module 8 — Learning and Study (improve, add upskilling and disclosure stance)
- Module 9 — Responsibility (improve, reframe — no longer finale)
- Module 16 — Capstone (improve, add worked example and self-critique)

These come late because they consume rewritten earlier modules.

**Phase 7 — Keep-module polish (week 7).**
- Modules 1, 2, 5, 6 — light-touch polish per Section 5

Done late so the polish reflects the new boilerplate-removed standard set in Phase 1.

**Phase 8 — Boilerplate removal pass (week 7–8).**
A single sweep across all 16 modules that strips the deprecated boilerplate and inserts the new transition/completion-evidence/revision-guidance pattern. Done after every module's content is finalized.

**Phase 9 — Consistency pass (week 8).**
Course-wide read for: tone consistency, repeated phrasing, terminology drift (e.g., is the same model called T-C-C-F-A or "the prompt control framework" — pick one), pathway-mapping consistency, and verification that every module's transition correctly names the next module's content.

**Phase 10 — Jifunze platform mapping (week 9).**
Convert the finalized course into platform-ready lesson blocks per Appendix A. Map artifacts into the upload blocks. Convert checkpoints into UI interactions. Validate against the QA checklist.

Total estimated effort: 8–9 weeks of focused authoring time, depending on team size and review cycles.

**Quality and repetition.**

When items 1–25 are true, Course 1 is the Jifunze model course and is ready for platform mapping. Until they are, it is not.
