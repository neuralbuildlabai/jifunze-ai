# Business Process Automation for Work — Voiceover Script

Professional narration for the Jifunze slide deck (40 slides). Target total runtime when recorded: roughly forty-five to sixty minutes at a calm instructional pace. Adjust pauses on title and transition slides so the deck does not feel rushed.

## Course introduction (before Slide 1)

**Narration:**

Welcome to Business Process Automation for Work from Jifunze. Over the next hour you will use a single case company, Bright Path Training Center, to practice how operations and small-business teams decide what to automate, what to keep human, and how to describe value without overselling. You can move the slides at your own pace. When voice tracks are available, they play alongside the deck; until then, treat this script as your narration guide. Let us begin with the title slide.

---

## Slide 1 — Title slide

**Narration:**

This course is about business process automation in real workplaces: streamlining tasks, reducing waste, and improving productivity without pretending that software fixes broken judgment. Keep your own company or team in mind as we work; Bright Path is the teaching example, not the only answer.

---

## Slide 2 — Course promise and business value

**Narration:**

By the end you should be able to read a manual workflow, spot automation-ready steps, prioritize by impact and effort, sketch a future state, and speak credibly about time, cost, risk, and change. The payoff is not buzzwords; it is clearer decisions and fewer expensive mistakes.

---

## Slide 3 — What business process automation means

**Narration:**

Business process automation means using rules, systems, and integrations so repeatable work moves with less manual handling. It is broader than buying a single tool: it is how handoffs, approvals, data capture, and follow-ups actually run day to day.

---

## Slide 4 — Automation vs digitization vs AI

**Narration:**

Digitization often means turning paper into files. Automation removes or reduces human steps in a flow. Artificial intelligence can help with judgment or pattern recognition, but many high-value wins are simple, reliable rules first. Keep those distinctions clear when you talk to vendors or leadership.

---

## Slide 5 — What should and should not be automated

**Narration:**

Good candidates are high volume, stable rules, structured inputs, and clear error paths. Poor early candidates include ambiguous exceptions, rare edge cases, and anything that needs nuanced ethics or negotiation before you have strong guardrails. Say no on purpose, not by accident.

---

## Slide 6 — The automation thinking cycle

**Narration:**

Treat automation as a cycle: observe the current state, measure workload and defects, score opportunities, design the future flow, quantify value and risk, then pilot and iterate. If you skip observation, you automate the wrong work faster.

---

## Slide 7 — Meet BrightPath Training Center

**Narration:**

Bright Path is a compact training provider mixing public courses and corporate workshops. They take enquiries, register learners, collect payments, run sessions, issue certificates, and gather feedback. Nothing here is exotic, which is why it is a useful mirror for many small teams.

---

## Slide 8 — The business problem we are solving

**Narration:**

Their problem is familiar: growth stretched manual steps. Staff time disappears into email, spreadsheets, and rework. Customers feel delays. Leadership wants efficiency without breaking trust or compliance. We will quantify that pressure before we propose fixes.

---

## Module transition — Into Module 2 (current workflow)

**Narration:**

You now have a shared vocabulary for automation. In the next module we read Bright Path’s current workflow the way an operations lead would: maps, roles, data, and hours. Pause the deck when you want to sketch your own parallel map.

---

## Slide 9 — BrightPath's current manual workflow

**Narration:**

This slide summarizes how work really moves today: enquiry through registration, payment, delivery, certification, and follow-up. Your task later is not to memorize their labels but to notice handoffs, queues, and rework loops.

---

## Slide 10 — Before workflow map

**Narration:**

The “before” map should feel busy. That is intentional. Density of arrows often correlates with coordination cost. Watch where the same document or status is recreated in more than one system.

---

## Slide 11 — Swimlane diagram: who does what

**Narration:**

Swimlanes make ownership obvious. When a step sits on a boundary between lanes, you have found a classic handoff risk. Automation often starts by tightening those boundaries, not by adding a chatbot on top.

---

## Slide 12 — Reading the workload dataset

**Narration:**

We now ground the story in numbers. Read tables slowly: volume per month, minutes per task, error rates. If a number surprises you, note why. Surprises are where leadership questions will land later.

---

## Slide 13 — Manual workload calculation

**Narration:**

This slide walks through how hours add up from simple arithmetic. The point is transparency: anyone on the team should be able to reproduce the estimate with the same assumptions. That credibility matters when you ask for budget.

---

## Slide 14 — Staff hours by task

**Narration:**

Compare tasks by hours consumed. Often one or two steps dominate. Those become your first candidates for measurement, standardization, and then automation, because savings there scale with volume.

---

## Slide 15 — Error and delay analysis

**Narration:**

Delays hurt revenue and reputation; errors hurt rework and trust. Separate frequency from severity. A rare catastrophic failure may deserve controls even if it is uncommon, while a frequent small annoyance may be cheap to fix first.

---

## Slide 16 — Bottlenecks and handoff failures

**Narration:**

Bottlenecks are where work waits. Handoff failures are where ownership blurs. Fix language first—who is responsible, what “done” means—then tools. Automation cannot clarify a role that leadership has left ambiguous.

---

## Module transition — Into Module 3 (opportunities)

**Narration:**

You understand the baseline. Next we score where automation belongs: suitability, impact, effort, and sequencing. Bring a skeptical mindset: scoring should narrow the list, not justify a shopping list.

---

## Slide 17 — How to decide if a task is automation-ready

**Narration:**

Ask whether inputs are structured, rules are stable, exceptions are manageable, and someone can monitor outcomes. If answers are mostly yes, dig deeper. If mostly no, improve the process manually before you buy software.

---

## Slide 18 — Human judgment vs automation decision tree

**Narration:**

Decision trees are training wheels for governance. They force the team to say which branches stay with people—approvals, exceptions, ethical calls—and which branches machines can execute. That clarity reduces shadow IT.

---

## Slide 19 — Automation suitability scoring

**Narration:**

Scoring turns debate into a repeatable rubric. Keep criteria few and weighted, otherwise everyone maxes every column. Revisit weights after each pilot; the rubric should learn, not fossilize.

---

## Slide 20 — Impact vs effort matrix

**Narration:**

Quick wins live high impact and lower effort. Strategic bets are high impact and higher effort. Fill-ins are low impact but easy—use sparingly. Traps are high effort and low impact; say no politely and document why.

---

## Slide 21 — Prioritizing high-value automation

**Narration:**

Prioritization is negotiation. Finance cares about cash timing; operations cares about throughput; compliance cares about evidence. Your matrix should surface one aligned short list, not three competing lists.

---

## Slide 22 — What not to automate yet

**Narration:**

Explicit “not yet” decisions protect capacity. They also prevent the team from feeling judged for ignoring shiny tools. Revisit this list after data quality and ownership improve.

---

## Slide 23 — Data quality and structured intake

**Narration:**

Garbage in still breaks automation. Forms, validations, and reference data are boring and essential. Budget time for cleanup before you promise timelines to customers.

---

## Slide 24 — Exception handling and escalation

**Narration:**

Every automated path needs a human escape hatch: what triggers review, who owns it, how fast they respond, and how you log the decision. Without that, small anomalies become emergencies.

---

## Module transition — Into Module 4 (future workflow)

**Narration:**

We move from diagnosis to design. The next slides sketch concrete automation options for Bright Path. As you listen, translate each option into language that would fit your own stack and risk appetite.

---

## Slide 25 — Automation option 1 — structured enquiry and registration forms

**Narration:**

Structured intake reduces back-and-forth email, captures data once, and feeds downstream systems cleanly. Pair the form with confirmation messaging so customers trust the handoff.

---

## Slide 26 — Automation option 2 — automated course info and reminders

**Narration:**

Reminders and predictable information cut “where do I go” questions. Keep tone human and allow opt-out where regulation requires. Automation should feel helpful, not robotic surveillance.

---

## Slide 27 — Automation option 3 — payment follow-up tracker

**Narration:**

Payment follow-up is sensitive. Automate reminders and status visibility first; keep dispute handling with trained staff. Transparency reduces anxiety for both sides.

---

## Slide 28 — Automation option 4 — attendance and certificate workflow

**Narration:**

Certificates are emotional and sometimes legal artifacts. Automate preparation and checks, but keep clear accountability for exceptions and corrections. Audit trails matter here.

---

## Slide 29 — Automation option 5 — feedback and improvement loop

**Narration:**

Feedback automation should close the loop: capture themes, route to owners, and track fixes. If feedback disappears into a dashboard nobody reads, you have added waste, not removed it.

---

## Slide 30 — After workflow map

**Narration:**

Compare the after map to the before map mentally. Fewer hops, clearer ownership, visible status. If it looks too perfect, ask what exceptions were assumed away—that is your risk register starting.

---

## Slide 31 — Automation dashboard mockup

**Narration:**

Dashboards are for decisions, not decoration. Each tile should tie to an owner and an action threshold. If a metric cannot trigger a behavior change, consider dropping it from v1.

---

## Module transition — Into Module 5 (value, risk, roadmap)

**Narration:**

We close with adult conversations about money, risk, and adoption. Numbers here are illustrative for learning; in your workplace, replace them with your own sources and sign-off from finance.

---

## Slide 32 — Time saved analysis

**Narration:**

Time saved should connect to baseline hours you already trusted. Show assumptions explicitly. Leaders will divide your headline number by two in their heads; give them the footnotes first.

---

## Slide 33 — Cost savings estimate

**Narration:**

Translate hours into labor value using conservative rates. Note what is excluded—software fees, implementation, training—so nobody confuses net and gross savings.

---

## Slide 34 — Error and delay reduction estimate

**Narration:**

Estimate fewer defects and faster cycle times, but pair benefits with controls. Savings from fewer errors only realize if you stop doing the old rework, not if people stay busy with new busywork.

---

## Slide 35 — Risk and control checklist

**Narration:**

Walk the checklist aloud in your head: access, payments, data privacy, business continuity, vendor lock-in. Controls are features of good automation, not paperwork afterthoughts.

---

## Slide 36 — Change management for automation

**Narration:**

People support what they help design. Communicate intent, train on exceptions, celebrate early wins, and be honest about job impacts. Patronizing change slides create resistance faster than any bot.

---

## Slide 37 — 30-60-90 day implementation roadmap

**Narration:**

Thirty days stabilize measurement and quick wins; sixty days expand integrations and governance; ninety days harden monitoring and continuous improvement. Adjust horizons for your organization’s maturity.

---

## Slide 38 — Executive recommendation for BrightPath

**Narration:**

Executives want a crisp recommendation: what to pilot first, expected impact, risks, and what you need from them—budget, time, or policy decisions. Practice delivering this slide in under two minutes.

---

**Narration:**

Your practice lab asks you to redesign a manual workflow you actually know. Export the thinking: current map, pains, prioritized automation angle, future sketch, and one risk you will monitor. Quality beats length.
