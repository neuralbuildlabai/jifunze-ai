## Lesson 1: Agile in Plain Language

**Duration:** 5 minutes

**Objective**
Help the learner explain in one sentence what Agile is and why teams adopted it. Establish that Agile is a *way of working*, not a tool or a brand.

**Key ideas**
- Traditional planning assumes you can see the whole project from the start.
- Agile assumes you will learn things during the work that change the plan.
- Short cycles + frequent feedback + small, finished pieces of work.
- Agile is the *philosophy*. Scrum is one specific *recipe* that follows it.

**Example scenario**
A small bakery wants to launch online ordering. The traditional plan: spend three months designing the perfect site, then launch. The agile plan: ship a one-page menu with a phone-order link in week one, learn what customers actually do, then add features.

**Learner action**
In the workbook, write down one project they have worked on (or want to start) and answer: *Which assumptions did we make at the start that turned out to be wrong?*

**Expected outcome**
The learner can describe Agile in their own words and recognise that "discovering things during the work" is normal, not a sign of poor planning.

## Lesson 2: Scrum and Sprints

**Duration:** 5 minutes

**Objective**
Introduce Scrum as a specific Agile framework built around fixed-length cycles called *sprints*. Set the expectation that the rest of the course is about how to run one of those cycles end-to-end.

**Key ideas**
- A sprint is a fixed time-box (most teams use 1–4 weeks; this course uses 2 weeks).
- Each sprint produces something usable, even if small.
- Sprints repeat. The team keeps improving.
- Inside a sprint there are: a goal, a backlog of work, daily check-ins, a demo, and a reflection.

**Example scenario**
A salon wants a website to take bookings. The team agrees on a two-week sprint with one goal: "Customers can request an appointment online." Anything beyond that — gift cards, loyalty points — waits for the next sprint.

**Learner action**
Open the workbook to the *Sprint length picker* prompt and decide whether their first sprint will be 1, 2, or 3 weeks, and why.

**Expected outcome**
The learner can describe a sprint as a short, repeatable cycle that delivers a usable result, and has chosen a sprint length for their own project.

## Lesson 3: Scrum Roles and Responsibilities

**Duration:** 6 minutes

**Objective**
Introduce the three Scrum *accountabilities* (the Scrum Guide's word for roles) without certification jargon. The learner should be able to look at any task and know who owns the decision.

**Key ideas**
- **Product Owner** decides *what* the team builds and in what order. Owns the product backlog.
- **Scrum Master** helps the team work well together, removes blockers, protects the process. Not a manager.
- **Developers** are everyone doing the building. In a non-software setting, "developers" means whoever does the work — a designer, a baker, a volunteer, a marketer.
- One person can wear two hats on a tiny team, but the *decisions* still need clear ownership.

**Example scenario**
At a community food drive: the organizer is acting as Product Owner (deciding which neighbourhoods to cover first). One volunteer coordinator is acting as Scrum Master (making sure the team has van keys, route maps, and time to talk). The volunteers loading and delivering food are the Developers.

**Learner action**
Workbook prompt: *For my project, who plays each role? If it's just me, which decisions am I making with which hat on?*

**Expected outcome**
The learner can name the three accountabilities, knows what each one decides, and has assigned them on paper for their own project.

## Lesson 4: Scrum Events

**Duration:** 7 minutes

**Objective**
Walk through the five Scrum events in the order they happen during a sprint, and explain what question each one answers.

**Key ideas**
- **Sprint** — the container that holds everything else.
- **Sprint Planning** — *What can we finish, and how?* (start of sprint)
- **Daily Scrum** — *Are we still on track for the sprint goal?* (15 min, every day)
- **Sprint Review** — *What did we actually deliver, and what did people think?* (end of sprint)
- **Sprint Retrospective** — *How can we work better next sprint?* (end of sprint, after the review)
- Every event has a clear purpose. Skipping events is the most common reason "Scrum doesn't work."

**Example scenario**
A school is organizing a careers week. Monday morning planning: the team picks the goal "All speakers confirmed and a printed schedule ready by Friday." Each morning at 9:00 they spend 10 minutes checking blockers (one speaker hasn't replied, one room is double-booked). Friday afternoon they walk the head teacher through the schedule (review). Friday end-of-day they spend 15 minutes asking what to do differently next time (retrospective).

**Learner action**
Workbook prompt: *Sketch a calendar for your first sprint with all five events on it.*

**Expected outcome**
The learner can name all five events, knows the purpose of each, and has them placed on a calendar for their own first sprint.

## Lesson 5: Backlogs, Task Boards, and Definition of Done

**Duration:** 8 minutes

**Objective**
Cover the three working tools (the Scrum Guide's word for these is *artifacts*) that hold the team's plan: the product backlog, the sprint backlog, and the increment. Add the *task board* as the practical day-to-day view, and the *Definition of Done* as the team's quality bar.

**Key ideas**
- **Product backlog** — every idea, request, or improvement, ordered by priority. Owned by the Product Owner.
- **User story** — a short sentence in the form *As a [persona], I want [something] so that [benefit]*. Helps the team focus on the user, not the feature.
- **Acceptance criteria** — short, testable conditions that say "this is finished correctly."
- **Sprint backlog** — the slice of the product backlog the team has committed to this sprint.
- **Task board** — a simple grid (To Do / In Progress / Done) that shows the state of every item right now.
- **Definition of Done** — the checklist that decides whether a piece of work is really finished, not just "almost done."

**Example scenario**
A small business owner is preparing a Mother's Day marketing campaign. Their product backlog has 18 ideas. The Product Owner picks 6 for this sprint. Each item gets a short user story and acceptance criteria — for example: *As a returning customer, I want to receive a personalised email so I feel remembered.* Acceptance criteria: email goes only to customers who bought in the last year, includes their first name, links to the Mother's Day landing page. Definition of Done for the sprint: copy proofread, image checked on mobile, email scheduled in the sending tool.

**Learner action**
Workbook prompts back to back: write one user story with acceptance criteria, then write three lines of a Definition of Done that fit their project.

**Expected outcome**
The learner can build a small product backlog, write a user story with acceptance criteria, choose a sprint backlog, and articulate a Definition of Done.

## Lesson 6: Mini Project — Plan Your First Sprint

**Duration:** 10 minutes

**Objective**
Pull every piece of the course together. The learner produces a complete first-sprint plan for a real project of their own.

**Key ideas**
- Pick a real project, not a hypothetical one.
- Keep the sprint short and the goal narrow.
- Finish each artifact before moving to the next: product goal → backlog → sprint goal → sprint backlog → task board → Definition of Done → review plan → retrospective questions.
- It is normal for the first sprint plan to feel "too small." That is correct.

**Example scenario**
The learner picks one of: the salon booking website, the school careers week, the small-business marketing campaign, or a software/product improvement. They follow the workbook step by step and end with a one-page plan they could hand to a teammate.

**Learner action**
Complete every section of the *Mini Project* part of the workbook. Use the *Mini-project submission checklist* on the last page to confirm nothing is missing.

**Expected outcome**
The learner has a complete, realistic first-sprint plan on paper. They could start running the sprint tomorrow.

## Final Quiz

**Duration:** 5 minutes

**Objective**
Confirm understanding of the core ideas and surface any gaps.

**Format**
15 questions: multiple choice, scenario, short answer, sequencing.

**Pass mark**
80% (12 of 15) recommended for "course complete."

**Note for learners**
The quiz is not a Scrum certification. It is a self-check that the workshop's ideas have landed.

---

## Completion Activity

**Duration:** 5 minutes

**Objective**
Move the learner from "I understand the ideas" to "I am committed to running my first sprint."

**Activity**
The learner picks a sprint start date, shares their one-page sprint plan with at least one other person (a teammate, friend, or community), and books their Sprint Review meeting on the calendar before the workshop ends.

**Expected outcome**
The learner leaves with a real, dated commitment to run a real first sprint. The workshop ends with action, not just knowledge.

## Glossary alignment

This course uses plain language throughout. The official Scrum Guide vocabulary maps as follows:

| Plain-language term used in this course | Scrum Guide term |
|---|---|
| Roles | Accountabilities |
| Meetings / ceremonies | Events |
| Working tools (backlog, task board, etc.) | Artifacts |
| Promises tied to each artifact | Commitments (Product Goal, Sprint Goal, Definition of Done) |

Learners who later move to certification material will recognise the underlying ideas immediately.
