# Facilitator Notes — Scrum Made Simple: Plan Your First Sprint

This micro-course is primarily self-paced. These notes are for facilitators who want to deliver it as a live workshop or as part of a longer team session.

## Who these notes are for

- Trainers running this as a paid public workshop
- Team leads running it as an internal onboarding session
- Community organisers using it to bring volunteers up to speed
- Course operators at Jifunze running it as a cohort kickoff

You do not need to be a certified Scrum Master to facilitate. You do need to have run the course yourself end to end, including the mini project, before you teach it.

---

## Format A: 45-minute live workshop

Use this when time is tight and people are showing up to "learn what Scrum is."

| Time | Block | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 – 0:03 | Welcome | Greet the room. State the promise: by the end, each person leaves with a sprint plan for a real project. Ask everyone to silently pick a real project to use throughout. |
| 0:03 – 0:08 | Lesson 1 | Run the script for Lesson 1. Skip the workbook reflection. |
| 0:08 – 0:13 | Lesson 2 | Run Lesson 2. Quick group hands: who picked 1 week, 2 weeks, 3 weeks. |
| 0:13 – 0:19 | Lesson 3 | Run Lesson 3. One-minute pair share: "Which role would be hardest for you?" |
| 0:19 – 0:26 | Lesson 4 | Run Lesson 4. Have everyone open a calendar app and block their five events live. |
| 0:26 – 0:34 | Lesson 5 | Run Lesson 5. Have learners write one user story + acceptance criteria + Definition of Done draft on screen-sharable sticky notes. |
| 0:34 – 0:42 | Mini project | Walk the workbook prompts in order. Keep the room silent and writing. Don't let anyone "ask one quick question" — it derails everyone. |
| 0:42 – 0:45 | Wrap and commit | Each person says out loud, in 10 seconds: their sprint goal and their sprint start date. End on time. |

**Key facilitation moves**

- Do not let any single lesson run long. The compounding effect is that the mini project gets cut, which is the only part that produces a real outcome.
- Speak less than the script — let learners write more.
- If someone has questions about advanced topics (scaled Scrum, story points, Kanban), park them on a "next session" board and keep going.

## Format B: 90-minute team session

Use this when you have a real team in the room and you want them to leave with a sprint plan they will *actually run together*.

| Time | Block | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 – 0:05 | Frame the session | Confirm the team's project. Confirm the sprint length. Confirm that the plan they leave with is the plan they will start running on the agreed date. |
| 0:05 – 0:25 | Lessons 1–3 (group teach) | Walk the script. Pause for the workbook reflections. Pair-share the role exercise. |
| 0:25 – 0:40 | Lesson 4 (calendar live) | Pull up the team's shared calendar. Block all five events for the next two sprints. Get everyone to accept on the spot. |
| 0:40 – 1:00 | Lesson 5 (artifacts) | Walk the artifacts. Then split the room: Product Owner drafts the product goal and persona; Developers draft 5–10 user stories on sticky notes. Reconvene; the PO orders the stickies. |
| 1:00 – 1:25 | Mini project (team) | Set the sprint goal as a group. Pick the sprint backlog as a group. Set the Definition of Done as a group. Lay out the task board. |
| 1:25 – 1:30 | Wrap and commit | Read the mini-project submission checklist out loud. Tick each item. Confirm the start date. End. |

**Key facilitation moves**

- For Lesson 5, write on the wall, not on screens. Sticky notes on a wall make ordering and reshuffling visible. Take a photo before everyone leaves.
- Watch for one person dominating the prioritisation. The Product Owner has the final call, but the team should be heard.
- The Definition of Done is the most-skipped artifact in real teams. Spend the time. If you only get one extra agreement during the session, make it the DoD.

## Common learner misconceptions

These come up almost every time. Address them when you hear them.

**"Scrum is just stand-up meetings."**
No. Stand-up (the Daily Scrum) is one of five events. The events without backlogs, artifacts, and a Definition of Done are just meetings on a calendar.

**"The Scrum Master is the project manager."**
No. The Scrum Master does not assign work, does not own the schedule, does not chase status. They help the team work well, remove blockers, and protect the process. A team can have both a Scrum Master and a project manager — they are different jobs.

**"The Product Owner is the customer."**
No. The Product Owner *represents* the customer's interests inside the team. They make decisions about what to build and in what order. The customer might give feedback during the Sprint Review, but the customer does not own the backlog.

**"You need a tool like Jira to do Scrum."**
No. Sticky notes on a wall, a spreadsheet, a Trello board, or a Notion page all work. The tool follows the team's process, not the other way around.

**"Sprints are deadlines."**
No. A sprint is a time-box, not a deadline. The team commits to a sprint goal, which is broader and softer than a deadline. If a specific user story isn't finished, the unfinished work goes back to the product backlog and gets re-prioritised. The sprint still ends on time.

**"User stories are technical specifications."**
No. A user story is a short sentence that captures who the work is for and why. The technical detail goes in the discussion, the acceptance criteria, and the team's working notes — not in the user story itself.

**"You can plan a whole project in a single sprint."**
No. A sprint produces *one usable thing*. A whole project takes many sprints. If a learner is trying to fit a quarter's worth of work into one sprint, gently shrink their sprint goal until it is honestly achievable.

**"The retrospective is just complaining."**
A bad retrospective is. A good retrospective ends with one or two specific commitments that change behaviour next sprint. If you are facilitating, do not let the room leave without those commitments written down.

## Suggested discussion questions

Use these to spark conversation in a live session, in cohort chat, or as homework.

1. Pick a project from your past that went badly. Which Scrum event would have caught the problem earliest, and why?

2. If you were the Product Owner of your favourite app, what would be the top three items on the product backlog this sprint?

3. Imagine your team has six people and they each pick one user story to work on alone. What is the most likely failure mode of this sprint?

4. Your sprint review is tomorrow and one user story is at 80%. What do you do?

5. When is it appropriate to *cancel* a sprint, and who should make that call?

6. Your team's Daily Scrum has been creeping up to 30 minutes. What is the underlying problem, and what is one specific change you could try?

7. A stakeholder shows up mid-sprint and asks the team to add a "small" extra item. How do you handle it without sounding bureaucratic?

8. You inherit a backlog of 200 items that nobody has touched in a year. What do you do first?

## Signs that a sprint goal is too broad

Look for any of these in a learner's worksheet and gently push back:

- The goal contains the word *and* more than once. ("Launch the website **and** train the team **and** prepare the marketing.")
- The goal uses a vague verb like *improve*, *enhance*, *work on*, *make progress*.
- The goal refers to a process, not an outcome. ("Hold weekly meetings about marketing.")
- You cannot picture a single moment where you stand in front of someone and say "yes, we did it."
- The goal would still be true at the end of the sprint even if the team did almost nothing.
- The goal would also be a good *quarterly* goal. (If yes, it is too big for a sprint.)

If the goal fails any of these tests, ask the learner: "What is the smallest version of this that would still be useful?" Help them shrink until it fits.

## How to review learner worksheets

When you are reviewing someone's mini-project plan, scan in this order:

1. **Sprint goal — single sentence?** If not, it is the most important thing to fix.
2. **Sprint backlog — does each item help achieve the sprint goal?** If something doesn't, ask why it's there.
3. **Sprint backlog size — realistic?** If the team has six people and ten items, that's probably fine. If the team has two people and ten items, that's probably not fine. Ask: "Have you done this much in two weeks before?"
4. **Definition of Done — at least five items, and specific?** Check for vague words like *good*, *quality*, *clean*. Push for testable phrases.
5. **Daily Scrum — has a time and a place?** If not, it will not happen.
6. **Sprint Review — has a real audience?** If the audience is "the team," that's not a review, that's a status meeting. Push for at least one external person.
7. **Sprint Retrospective — booked for the same day as the review?** If it slips by even a day, most teams skip it.

Give feedback in the form of one specific suggestion, not a list of criticisms. "Tighten this sprint goal — try removing the word *and*" lands better than "your sprint goal is too broad."

## How to support non-technical learners

Most of the language and most of the available material online assumes a software team. Here is how to make Scrum land for everyone else.

**Translate the word *Developers* every time.** Every time the script or workbook says "Developers," explicitly translate: "in your case, that means the people doing the building — the writer, the volunteer driver, the designer, the baker." Do this until the room stops needing the translation.

**Use the four worked examples — and pick the one closest to the room.** The script has a salon, a school, a marketing campaign, and a software improvement. If you are facilitating for community organisers, lean on the food drive. If you are facilitating for retail owners, lean on the marketing campaign. Reuse the same example through Lessons 4 and 5 so the room sees the whole loop in one context.

**Skip the words *artifact* and *accountability* when you can.** They are correct words but they are also intimidating. Use *working tool* and *role* in conversation, and only mention the official terms once, with a brief "you'll see this word in the Scrum Guide."

**Do not use the words *velocity*, *story points*, *epics*, or *grooming* in this workshop.** These are advanced topics, and skipping them is a feature, not a bug. If a learner asks, say "great question, that's in the next-level material — for now, ignore it."

**Show, don't define.** When you describe the task board, do not just describe it — draw it on a whiteboard, or share a real screen with three columns and live-move a card. Two minutes of demonstration beats five minutes of explanation.

**Lower the bar for the first sprint.** Most non-technical learners want to do too much in their first sprint. Encourage a deliberately tiny goal. The point of sprint one is not to deliver everything — it is to learn the loop.

**End every workshop with a public commitment.** Each learner says, out loud, their sprint goal and their start date. Public commitments dramatically increase the chance that the learner actually runs the sprint after they leave the room.

## Timing tips for facilitators

- The narration script targets ~40 minutes of speaking. If you are facilitating live, plan to take roughly 1.5× that time once you add Q&A and pauses for writing.
- Do not improvise on Lesson 5 (the artifacts lesson). It is the densest lesson and the easiest to overrun. Stick to the script.
- The mini project is the irreducible part of the workshop. If you are running short on time, cut earlier lessons or skip some workbook reflections — but never cut the mini project.

## After the workshop

A few things you can do as a facilitator to multiply the impact:

- **Send a one-page recap email** with the slide visual of the Scrum cycle, the mini-project checklist, and the date of the next session.
- **Offer to review one sprint plan per learner** within five working days. Most learners will take you up on it; the feedback you give now shapes how they actually run their first sprint.
- **Schedule an optional "first sprint review" check-in** two weeks after the workshop. Learners who know there is a check-in are dramatically more likely to actually run the sprint.
- **Capture one anonymised plan as a worked example** for future cohorts (with the learner's permission).
