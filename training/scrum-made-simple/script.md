# Voiceover Script — Scrum Made Simple: Plan Your First Sprint

**Target runtime:** 38–42 minutes at a relaxed conversational pace (≈140 words per minute).
**Tone:** Warm, professional, beginner-friendly. Like a patient colleague walking you through it for the first time.
**Pause cues:** `[PAUSE — workbook]` marks places where the learner stops to write. The voiceover pauses for 2 seconds and then continues.
**Section markers:** `[L1]`, `[L2]`, etc. align to the lessons in `course-outline.md` and the slide ranges in `slides/slide-outline.md`.

## Cold open (0:00 — 1:00)

Welcome. If you have ever joined a project that started with a beautiful plan and then quietly fell apart somewhere in week three — this workshop is for you. If you have ever heard the words *Agile* or *Scrum* and quietly nodded while having no idea what they meant — this is also for you.

Over the next forty minutes or so, I am going to take Scrum apart in plain language and put it back together. We will skip the certification jargon, we will use real examples — a salon, a school event, a small marketing campaign, a small product improvement — and by the end you will have done the most important thing: you will have planned your own first sprint, on paper, for a real project of your own.

You will need three things. The workbook that comes with this course, a pen, and one project in mind that you genuinely want to move forward. It can be tiny. A side project, a community event, a launch you keep putting off. Pick the smallest real thing you have, not a hypothetical.

Ready? Let's start with the bigger picture.

## [L1] Lesson 1 — Agile in plain language (1:00 — 6:00)

For most of the twentieth century, the way you ran a project looked like this. You wrote a long plan at the start. You estimated everything. You drew a Gantt chart. Then you executed the plan, in order, and you delivered at the end. That style of work has a name — it is sometimes called *waterfall*, because each phase pours into the next.

Waterfall works really well when the world is predictable. Building a bridge. Putting up a warehouse. Manufacturing a car part. The requirements do not change much during the work, so a long up-front plan holds up.

The problem is that most modern work is not like that. You start a project, and somewhere around week three you discover something you did not know in week one. A customer asks for something different. A supplier changes a price. A regulation moves. A piece of technology you assumed would work, doesn't. And the long plan starts to feel like a museum piece.

In the late nineties and early two-thousands, a group of software practitioners noticed this and wrote what they called the *Agile Manifesto*. The manifesto is short. It says: people and conversations matter more than rigid processes; working things matter more than long documents; responding to change matters more than sticking to a plan. Agile, in plain words, is a way of working that assumes you will learn things during the work, and that the plan should adapt as you learn.

That is the whole idea. Agile is not a tool. It is not a piece of software. It is not a brand. It is a *philosophy* about how to work when you cannot predict everything up front.

Let's make that concrete. Imagine a small bakery. The owner wants to launch online ordering. The waterfall version of that project is: spend three months designing the perfect website, get every payment method working, build a loyalty program, then launch. The agile version is: this week, put up a one-page menu with a phone-order link. Watch what customers actually do. Next week, add the most-requested feature. The agile version is in the hands of customers ten weeks earlier and is shaped by what they actually wanted, not what someone guessed they would want.

Now, here is where the names start to matter. Agile is the philosophy. *Scrum* is one specific recipe that follows it. There are other recipes — Kanban is one, Extreme Programming is another, but in this workshop we are focused on Scrum because it is the most widely used and the easiest to learn first.

Before we move on, take a moment.

[PAUSE — workbook] Open your workbook to the *Lesson 1 reflection*. Write down one project you have worked on, or want to start. Then write down one thing about that project that you were sure of at the start, that turned out to be wrong. Take thirty seconds.

Welcome back. The thing you just wrote is the reason agile exists. The work changed underneath you. That is normal. Now let's look at the recipe that handles it best.

## [L2] Lesson 2 — Scrum and sprints (6:00 — 11:00)

Scrum is built around a very simple idea: instead of doing one giant project, you do a series of short cycles, each one ending in something usable. Each of those short cycles is called a *sprint*.

A sprint has a fixed length. Most teams pick somewhere between one and four weeks. In this workshop we are going to use two weeks as our default, because two weeks is long enough to get something real done and short enough that you do not lose your way. You can pick a different length later — just keep it consistent so the team builds a rhythm.

Inside a sprint, five things happen. There is a *goal* for the sprint — a single sentence that says what we will achieve. There is a *plan* — the slice of work the team has chosen to deliver. There are short daily *check-ins* to keep everyone aligned. At the end, there is a *review* where the team shows what they actually delivered. And there is a *retrospective* where the team asks how they can work better next time.

That whole pattern repeats. Sprint after sprint. The team gets faster, sharper, and more confident, because they are running the same loop over and over instead of running one giant project once.

Let's anchor that with an example. A small salon owner — let's call her Amani — wants a website where clients can book appointments online. The waterfall version of that project would mean three months of planning, designing, and building before the first client can use it. The Scrum version says: in our first two-week sprint, we will deliver one thing — a customer can request an appointment online and the salon receives that request. That is it. No gift cards. No loyalty points. No staff calendar integration. Just one usable thing, in the hands of customers, in two weeks.

In the next sprint, Amani might add the staff calendar. In the sprint after that, automatic reminders. In each sprint, she ships something usable, learns from real client behaviour, and decides what to build next based on what she actually saw — not what she imagined ten weeks ago.

That is Scrum. A drumbeat of short, finished cycles.

[PAUSE — workbook] Turn to the *Sprint length picker*. Decide whether your first sprint will be one, two, or three weeks. Two weeks is a safe default. Pick one and write it down before we continue.

If you picked something other than two weeks, that is fine. Just remember that the rest of the workshop assumes a two-week pattern, so adjust the dates in your own plan accordingly.

## [L3] Lesson 3 — Scrum roles and responsibilities (11:00 — 17:00)

Scrum has three roles. The official term in the Scrum Guide is *accountabilities*, which is a fancier word for "who is responsible for what." We will use the word *role* in this workshop because it is clearer for beginners — but if you ever read the Scrum Guide later, look for the word "accountabilities" and you'll know it means the same thing.

The first role is the *Product Owner*. This person decides what the team builds, and in what order. They own the list of work — the backlog — and they make sure that the most valuable thing is always being worked on next. The Product Owner is not a project manager. They are not a manager of people. They are the single voice that says "this matters more than that, here is why, this is what we do next."

The second role is the *Scrum Master*. This is the most misunderstood role. The Scrum Master is not the team's boss. They do not assign work. They do not chase status. Their job is to help the team work well together. They remove blockers — for example, if the team is stuck waiting on access to a tool, the Scrum Master goes and gets the access. They protect the team's process — they make sure the meetings happen, they keep them short, they make sure the team is not being pulled in twelve directions at once. A good Scrum Master makes themselves slowly less necessary as the team gets better.

The third role is the *Developers*. In the Scrum Guide, that word is used for everyone doing the actual building, no matter what their job title is. On a software team, developers are engineers. On a marketing team, the developers are the writers, designers, and channel managers. On a community project, the developers are the volunteers doing the work. Don't get hung up on the word — wherever you see "developers" in Scrum, read it as "the people building the thing this sprint."

These three roles together are called the *Scrum team*. A Scrum team is small — usually no more than ten people, often just three to seven. Small enough that a single conversation can include everyone.

Let's make this concrete with an example. Picture a community food drive. The neighbourhood has agreed to deliver food parcels to families in three nearby blocks over two weeks. There is one organizer who decides which families get what and in what order — she is acting as the Product Owner. There is a volunteer coordinator who makes sure the team has the van keys, the route maps, the time to talk each morning, and a clear way to flag problems — she is acting as the Scrum Master. And there is a group of volunteers who do the loading and the delivering — they are the Developers.

Now, on a tiny team — a one-person side project, a two-person startup — one person ends up wearing more than one hat. That is normal and acceptable. The thing that matters is that *each decision* still has clear ownership. When a backlog choice has to be made, the person making it should know they are wearing the Product Owner hat. When a process question comes up, they should know they are wearing the Scrum Master hat. The hats are about decision ownership, not job titles.

[PAUSE — workbook] Open the workbook to *Roles for my project*. Write down the name of one real or imagined person for each role. If it is just you, write your name three times — but next to each, write the kind of decision you'll be making with that hat on. Take a minute.

Good. Now we'll look at how the team works together inside a sprint.

## [L4] Lesson 4 — Scrum events (17:00 — 24:00)

Scrum has five events. The Scrum Guide calls them *events*. You will sometimes hear them called *ceremonies* or *meetings* — they all mean the same thing. They exist for one reason: to give the team predictable moments to plan, sync, show, and reflect, so the team is not constantly inventing how to coordinate.

The first event is the *Sprint* itself. The sprint is the container that holds everything else. From the moment a sprint begins to the moment it ends — usually two weeks later — the team is focused on the goal of that sprint. The sprint is the heartbeat of Scrum.

The second event is *Sprint Planning*. This happens at the start of every sprint. The team and the Product Owner sit down together and answer two questions. *What can we finish in this sprint?* And *how will we go about it?* The output of Sprint Planning is two things: a sprint goal — one clear sentence — and a sprint backlog — the slice of work the team is committing to.

The third event is the *Daily Scrum*. This is a fifteen-minute meeting that happens every working day at the same time. The team uses it to check whether they are still on track for the sprint goal. The classic version of this meeting has each person answer three questions: *what did I do yesterday, what will I do today, and what is in my way.* Some teams prefer a more conversational version — that's fine. The point is the same: do we still have a clear path to the goal? If we don't, what needs to change?

The fourth event is the *Sprint Review*. This happens near the end of the sprint. The team shows what they delivered to the people who care about it — customers, stakeholders, sponsors, the rest of the company. The review is not a status meeting. It is a chance to put real, finished work in front of real people and get real feedback. That feedback flows back into the product backlog and changes what the team builds next.

The fifth event is the *Sprint Retrospective*. This is the team's private conversation, usually right after the review. The team asks three questions: *what went well, what didn't, and what will we change next sprint?* The retrospective ends with one or two specific commitments — small things the team will do differently. Over many sprints, those small improvements compound into a much stronger team.

Let's anchor that with an example. A school is organizing a careers week — speakers from local industries coming in to talk to year ten students. The school's small organising team decides to run it as a two-week sprint. On Monday morning of the first week, they hold sprint planning and agree on a goal: *all speakers confirmed and a printed schedule ready by Friday of week two*. They write a short sprint backlog: contact each speaker, confirm rooms, design the schedule poster, brief the volunteer hosts.

Each morning at nine, they spend ten minutes on a daily scrum. On Tuesday, the daily scrum surfaces a problem — one speaker hasn't replied. The team decides who will follow up by lunchtime. On Friday of week two, they walk the head teacher through the finished schedule — that is the sprint review. The head teacher asks for the names of the volunteer hosts to be added to the printed schedule. That feedback gets noted and added to the backlog for the next sprint. Then, end-of-day Friday, the team spends fifteen minutes asking what worked and what didn't. They notice that the daily scrum drifted to thirty minutes by the end of the week. Their commitment for next sprint: stand up, no chairs, hard stop at fifteen minutes.

That whole pattern fits inside two weeks. It is small, predictable, and learnable.

One last thing about events. Skipping events is the most common reason teams say "Scrum doesn't work for us." If you skip Sprint Planning, you start the sprint without a clear goal. If you skip the Daily Scrum, problems hide for days. If you skip the Review, you lose your feedback loop. If you skip the Retrospective, you stop improving. The events are not bureaucracy — they are the engine.

[PAUSE — workbook] Turn to *Sprint calendar* in the workbook. For your first sprint, write down the dates for sprint planning, the daily scrum slot, the sprint review, and the sprint retrospective. Block them in your real calendar before the end of this workshop.

## [L5] Lesson 5 — Backlogs, task boards, and Definition of Done (24:00 — 32:00)

We have covered the people and the meetings. Now we cover the working tools — the things the team actually looks at every day. The Scrum Guide calls these *artifacts*. You can think of them as the team's working documents.

There are three main artifacts.

The first is the *Product Backlog*. This is the master list of every idea, every request, every improvement, every bug. It is ordered by priority. The Product Owner owns the order. The most important item is always at the top. The backlog is alive — it grows when new ideas come in, it shrinks when the team finishes things or removes ideas they no longer believe in.

Most items on a product backlog are written as *user stories*. A user story is a short sentence in a specific shape: *As a [type of person], I want [something], so that [benefit].* The shape forces the team to think about who the work is for and why it matters, not just what it is. For example: *As a returning customer, I want to receive a personalised email at Mother's Day, so that I feel remembered.* That is much more useful than the bare phrase "Mother's Day email," because it tells you who, what, and why.

Each user story should also have *acceptance criteria* — short, testable conditions that say "this is finished correctly." For our Mother's Day email, the acceptance criteria might be: it goes only to customers who bought in the last twelve months; it includes their first name; it links to the Mother's Day landing page; it sends on the morning of the Saturday before Mother's Day. When the team can tick all of those, the story is acceptable. When they can't, it isn't done — it doesn't matter how close it feels.

The second artifact is the *Sprint Backlog*. This is the slice of the product backlog the team has chosen to commit to in this sprint. It is much shorter than the product backlog. For a two-week sprint, you might pick five to twelve items, depending on size. The sprint backlog is the team's plan for the sprint. It is owned by the Developers — the people doing the work — because they are the ones who know how much they can realistically finish.

The third artifact is the *Increment*. This is the actual usable result the team produced this sprint. In a software team, the increment is a working version of the product. In a marketing team, the increment might be the campaign, ready to ship. In a community team, the increment might be the food parcels, packed and delivered. The increment is what you can show in the sprint review. It is the *evidence* that the sprint produced something real.

Three artifacts. Product backlog, sprint backlog, increment. The Scrum Guide pairs each of those with a *commitment*: the product backlog has the Product Goal, the sprint backlog has the Sprint Goal, and the increment has the Definition of Done. We'll talk about the Sprint Goal and Definition of Done in a moment.

Day to day, the team looks at the *task board*. The task board is not technically an artifact in the Scrum Guide, but every working Scrum team uses one. It is the simplest possible view of where every item is right now. The classic task board has three columns: *To Do*, *In Progress*, and *Done*. Every item from the sprint backlog is a card. Cards move left to right as work progresses. You can put it on a wall with sticky notes, in a spreadsheet, or in a tool like Trello or a Jira board. The format does not matter — what matters is that any team member can glance at the board and see the state of the sprint.

A useful upgrade is to add a fourth column: *Blocked*. Anything the team can't move forward on goes there, with a short note saying why. The Scrum Master watches the Blocked column carefully — those are the things they need to clear.

Now — the *Sprint Goal*. The sprint goal is one sentence. It is not a list. It is one clear thing the team is trying to achieve this sprint. A good sprint goal sounds like *Customers can request an appointment online* or *All speakers confirmed and a printed schedule ready by Friday* or *Mother's Day email is in customers' inboxes by Saturday morning*. A bad sprint goal sounds like *Make progress on the website* or *Improve marketing*. The test is simple: at the end of the sprint, can you point at something and say, yes, we achieved that? Or no, we did not? If the answer is fuzzy, the goal is fuzzy.

Finally — the *Definition of Done*. The Definition of Done is the team's checklist for "really finished." It is the same for every item in the sprint. For a marketing campaign, your Definition of Done might say: copy proofread by a second person, design checked on mobile and desktop, scheduled in the sending tool, links tested. For a software story, it might say: code reviewed, tests passing, deployed to staging, demo recorded. For a community project, it might say: parcel packed, route confirmed, recipient contacted, photo taken.

Without a Definition of Done, "done" means whatever the person who finished the task thinks it means. With a Definition of Done, "done" means the same thing for the whole team.

Let's anchor everything we just covered with a single example. A small business owner — let's call him Daniel — runs a homeware shop and is preparing a Mother's Day marketing campaign. Daniel has eighteen ideas in his product backlog, ranging from "discount code" to "in-store flowers" to "Instagram giveaway." He picks six items for the first two-week sprint, written as user stories with acceptance criteria. He sets a sprint goal: *Mother's Day email and landing page live by Saturday morning.* He sets a Definition of Done that includes proofreading, mobile check, link testing. He sets up a simple task board with columns To Do, In Progress, Blocked, and Done. Daily at nine he and his small team spend ten minutes at the board. By the Friday of week two, the email is live, he reviews the result with his retail team, and they spend fifteen minutes asking what to do differently before the next sprint starts on Monday.

That is the whole engine.

[PAUSE — workbook] Now turn to the workbook and do three things in a row. One: write a single user story for your project in the *As a / I want / So that* form. Two: write three or four lines of acceptance criteria for that story. Three: write three lines of a Definition of Done that fit your project. Take three minutes. Pause the workshop if you need to.

When you come back, we will pull all of this together into your first real sprint plan.

## [L6] Lesson 6 — Mini project: plan your first sprint (32:00 — 42:00)

This is the moment everything we have done becomes useful. We are going to walk together through your first sprint plan, end to end. By the end of this lesson, you will have a one-page document that you could hand to a teammate tomorrow.

We will go in this order: product goal, persona, product backlog, sprint goal, sprint backlog, task board, Definition of Done, sprint review plan, sprint retrospective questions.

Step one. Open your workbook to the *Product Goal* worksheet. Your product goal is the bigger objective your sprints are working toward. It is broader than a sprint goal, narrower than a company mission. It is the *what we are ultimately trying to achieve with this product or project*. For Amani's salon, it might be: *Clients can manage their entire relationship with the salon online — booking, paying, and receiving reminders.* For Daniel's homeware shop, it might be: *Mother's Day generates 30 percent more revenue than last year.* For the food drive, it might be: *Every family in our three-block area has reliable weekly food support for the next six months.* Write yours now.

[PAUSE — workbook]

Step two. Persona. Open the persona worksheet. Write down one sentence about the person your work is really for. Their name, their situation, what they are trying to do, what gets in their way. The persona is not market research — it is a single human you can picture clearly when you make decisions. If your project serves several types of people, pick the most important one for now.

[PAUSE — workbook]

Step three. Product backlog. Turn to the worksheet with ten slots. Write between five and ten items. Each one in user-story form. Each one short. Don't worry about getting it perfect — the backlog is alive, you will keep changing it. Take three minutes.

[PAUSE — workbook]

Step four. Sprint goal. Look at your backlog. Pick the one outcome that, if delivered in the next two weeks, would matter most. Write it as a single sentence on the *Sprint Goal* worksheet. Test it: at the end of the sprint, will you be able to point at something and say "yes, we did that," or "no, we did not"? If the answer is fuzzy, sharpen the sentence.

[PAUSE — workbook]

Step five. Sprint backlog. Look back at your product backlog and pick the items that contribute to your sprint goal. Move them to the *Sprint Backlog* worksheet. Be honest about how much your team can do in two weeks — most beginners overcommit. It is much better to under-commit and finish than over-commit and miss.

[PAUSE — workbook]

Step six. Task board. Open the *Task Board* worksheet. Write each sprint backlog item as a card in the To Do column. You will move them across as the sprint runs. If you are using a digital tool — Trello, Notion, a spreadsheet — set up the same three or four columns there.

[PAUSE — workbook]

Step seven. Definition of Done. You wrote a draft of this earlier. Now look at it again with your sprint goal in mind. Is it specific enough that two people would agree on whether something is done? Is it short enough to remember? Adjust it.

[PAUSE — workbook]

Step eight. Sprint review plan. Turn to the *Sprint Review Plan* worksheet. Write down: who will be in the room, what you will show them, how long the meeting will be, and one specific question you want their feedback on.

[PAUSE — workbook]

Step nine. Sprint retrospective questions. The retrospective is short and structured. Write down the three questions you will ask the team at the end of the sprint. The classic three are *what went well, what didn't, what will we change next sprint*. Use those, or write your own. Then commit to writing the answers down somewhere your team can see them.

[PAUSE — workbook]

Beautiful. Take a breath. You now have a complete first-sprint plan. Compare it against the *Mini-project submission checklist* at the end of the workbook. Tick each item. If anything is missing, fill it in now.

If your plan looks small, that is correct. First sprint plans should look small. You are not trying to deliver everything — you are trying to deliver one usable thing in two weeks, and learn enough from it to plan the next sprint better.

## Wrap-up and what's next (42:00 — 44:00)

You have just done what most people who talk about Agile and Scrum never actually do. You planned a real sprint, for a real project, on paper. That puts you ahead of most rooms.

A few honest reminders before you go.

First, this is not a Scrum certification. If you want to pursue certification, look at the Scrum Guide at scrumguides.org and an accredited training provider. The plain-language terms in this workshop map cleanly to the official ones, so you will recognise everything.

Second, your first sprint will not be perfect. That is the entire point of the retrospective. Run the sprint, learn from it, change one thing, run the next one. The teams that get good at Scrum are not the ones that get it right the first time — they are the ones that keep adjusting.

Third, do not let perfect get in the way of starting. The smallest team — even a team of one — can run a sprint. The smallest project can have a sprint plan. If you wait until you have the right team, the right tools, the right project, you will wait forever.

Now there are three things I want you to do before the workshop ends. One: go to the quiz and take it. It is fifteen short questions and it is designed to surface anything that didn't quite land. Two: pick a real start date for your first sprint and write it on your sprint plan. Three: tell at least one other person what your sprint goal is and when your sprint review is. The act of saying it out loud makes it real.

Thank you for working through this with me. Go and run a great sprint.
