# Voiceover Script (per-slide format) — Scrum Made Simple

This is the **same content** as `script.md`, re-cut by slide rather than by lesson, so the voiceover artist can record cleanly slide by slide.

**Conventions**

- Each slide section starts with `## Slide N — [title]`.
- Inside each slide:
  - `> Slide on screen for ~XX seconds` — the on-screen budget.
  - The narration in plain text. Read this aloud as written.
  - `[BREATH]` — a quarter-second pause, not a full silence.
  - `[PAUSE — 2s, workbook badge]` — a full 2-second silence; the editor will drop in the on-screen badge.

**Pacing target:** ~140 words per minute. The slide-time budgets in this script were calculated from word counts at that rate.

Lesson one. Agile in plain language.

---

## Slide 5 — The way projects used to be planned

> Slide on screen for ~32 seconds.

For most of the twentieth century, the way you ran a project looked like this. You wrote a long plan at the start. You estimated everything. You drew a Gantt chart. Then you executed the plan, in order, and you delivered at the end. That style of work has a name — it is sometimes called *waterfall*, because each phase pours into the next.

Waterfall works really well when the world is predictable. Building a bridge. Putting up a warehouse. Manufacturing a car part. The requirements do not change much during the work, so a long up-front plan holds up.

---

## Slide 6 — Then reality intervenes

> Slide on screen for ~28 seconds.

The problem is that most modern work is not like that. You start a project, and somewhere around week three you discover something you didn't know in week one. A customer asks for something different. A supplier changes a price. A regulation moves. A piece of technology you assumed would work, doesn't. And the long plan starts to feel like a museum piece.

---

## Slide 7 — Agile, in one sentence

> Slide on screen for ~30 seconds.

In the late nineties and early two-thousands, a group of software practitioners noticed this and wrote what they called the *Agile Manifesto*. The manifesto is short. It says: people and conversations matter more than rigid processes; working things matter more than long documents; responding to change matters more than sticking to a plan.

[BREATH]

Agile, in plain words, is a way of working that assumes you will learn things during the work, and that the plan should adapt as you learn.

---

## Slide 8 — Why it matters: feedback arrives sooner

> Slide on screen for ~25 seconds.

That is the whole idea. Agile is not a tool. It is not a piece of software. It is not a brand. It is a *philosophy* about how to work when you cannot predict everything up front.

[BREATH]

This chart shows the practical difference. Traditional planning typically gathers customer feedback once — at the end. Agile gathers feedback every couple of weeks, all the way through. That is why agile teams are so much harder to surprise.

---

## Slide 9 — Agile is the philosophy. Scrum is one recipe.

> Slide on screen for ~22 seconds.

Now, here is where the names start to matter. Agile is the philosophy. *Scrum* is one specific recipe that follows it. There are other recipes — Kanban is one, Extreme Programming is another — but in this workshop we are focused on Scrum because it is the most widely used and the easiest to learn first.

Lesson two. Scrum and sprints.

## Slide 12 — A sprint is a fixed time-box

> Slide on screen for ~30 seconds.

Scrum is built around a very simple idea: instead of doing one giant project, you do a series of short cycles, each one ending in something usable. Each of those short cycles is called a *sprint*.

[BREATH]

A sprint has a fixed length. Most teams pick somewhere between one and four weeks. In this workshop we are going to use two weeks as our default, because two weeks is long enough to get something real done, and short enough that you do not lose your way.

---

## Slide 13 — The Scrum cycle

> Slide on screen for ~50 seconds.

Inside a sprint, five things happen. There is a *goal* for the sprint. There is a *plan* — the slice of work the team has chosen to deliver. There are short daily *check-ins* to keep everyone aligned. At the end, there is a *review* where the team shows what they actually delivered. And there is a *retrospective* where the team asks how they can work better next time.

[BREATH]

That whole pattern repeats. Sprint after sprint. The team gets faster, sharper, and more confident, because they are running the same loop over and over instead of running one giant project once.

---

## Slide 14 — Worked example: Amani's salon

> Slide on screen for ~40 seconds.

Let's anchor that with an example. A small salon owner — let's call her Amani — wants a website where clients can book appointments online. The Scrum version says: in our first two-week sprint, we will deliver one thing — a customer can request an appointment online and the salon receives that request. That is it. No gift cards. No loyalty points. No staff calendar integration. Just one usable thing, in the hands of customers, in two weeks.

In the next sprint, Amani might add the staff calendar. In the sprint after that, automatic reminders. In each sprint, she ships something usable, learns from real client behaviour, and decides what to build next based on what she actually saw.

## Slide 17 — Three roles. Three clear jobs.

> Slide on screen for ~70 seconds.

Scrum has three roles. The official term in the Scrum Guide is *accountabilities*, which is a fancier word for "who is responsible for what." We will use the word *role* because it is clearer for beginners.

[BREATH]

The first role is the *Product Owner*. This person decides what the team builds, and in what order. They own the list of work — the backlog — and they make sure that the most valuable thing is always being worked on next.

[BREATH]

The second role is the *Scrum Master*. The Scrum Master is not the team's boss. They do not assign work. Their job is to help the team work well together — they remove blockers, they protect the team's process, they keep the meetings short.

[BREATH]

The third role is the *Developers*. In the Scrum Guide, that word is used for everyone doing the actual building. On a marketing team, the developers are the writers, designers, and channel managers. On a community project, the developers are the volunteers doing the work. Wherever you see "developers" in Scrum, read it as "the people building the thing this sprint."

---

## Slide 18 — Worked example: a community food drive

> Slide on screen for ~40 seconds.

Let's make this concrete. Picture a community food drive. The neighbourhood has agreed to deliver food parcels to families in three nearby blocks over two weeks.

[BREATH]

There is one organizer who decides which families get what and in what order — she is acting as the Product Owner. There is a volunteer coordinator who makes sure the team has the van keys, the route maps, the time to talk each morning, and a clear way to flag problems — she is acting as the Scrum Master. And there is a group of volunteers who do the loading and the delivering — they are the Developers.

---

## Slide 19 — Wearing more than one hat

> Slide on screen for ~30 seconds.

On a tiny team — a one-person side project, a two-person startup — one person ends up wearing more than one hat. That is normal and acceptable. The thing that matters is that *each decision* still has clear ownership. When a backlog choice has to be made, the person making it should know they are wearing the Product Owner hat. When a process question comes up, they should know they are wearing the Scrum Master hat.

## Slide 22 — Five events. Five questions answered.

> Slide on screen for ~80 seconds.

Scrum has five events. They exist to give the team predictable moments to plan, sync, show, and reflect, so the team is not constantly inventing how to coordinate.

[BREATH]

The first is the *Sprint* itself — the container for everything else.

[BREATH]

The second is *Sprint Planning*, at the start of every sprint. It answers two questions. *What can we finish in this sprint?* And *how will we go about it?*

[BREATH]

The third is the *Daily Scrum* — fifteen minutes, every working day, same time. It answers one question: *are we still on track for the sprint goal?*

[BREATH]

The fourth is the *Sprint Review*, near the end of the sprint. The team shows what they delivered to the people who care about it. It is not a status meeting — it is a chance to put real, finished work in front of real people and get real feedback.

[BREATH]

The fifth is the *Sprint Retrospective*, usually right after the review. The team asks: what went well, what didn't, what will we change next sprint? It ends with one or two specific commitments.

---

## Slide 23 — Worked example: a school's careers week

> Slide on screen for ~70 seconds.

Let's anchor that with an example. A school is organizing a careers week — speakers from local industries coming in to talk to year ten students. They run it as a two-week sprint. On Monday morning of the first week, they hold sprint planning and agree on a goal: *all speakers confirmed and a printed schedule ready by Friday of week two*.

[BREATH]

Each morning at nine, they spend ten minutes on a daily scrum. On Tuesday, the daily scrum surfaces a problem — one speaker hasn't replied. The team decides who will follow up by lunchtime.

[BREATH]

On Friday of week two, they walk the head teacher through the finished schedule — that is the sprint review. The head teacher asks for the names of the volunteer hosts to be added. That feedback gets noted for the next sprint. Then, end-of-day Friday, the team spends fifteen minutes asking what worked and what didn't. They notice the daily scrum drifted to thirty minutes. Their commitment for next sprint: stand up, no chairs, hard stop at fifteen minutes.

---

## Slide 24 — Skipping events is the most common failure

> Slide on screen for ~35 seconds.

One last thing about events. Skipping events is the most common reason teams say "Scrum doesn't work for us." If you skip Sprint Planning, you start the sprint without a clear goal. If you skip the Daily Scrum, problems hide for days. If you skip the Review, you lose your feedback loop. If you skip the Retrospective, you stop improving. The events are not bureaucracy — they are the engine.

Lesson five. Backlogs, task boards, and Definition of Done.

## Slide 27 — Three working tools

> Slide on screen for ~50 seconds.

We have covered the people and the meetings. Now the working tools. There are three.

[BREATH]

The first is the *Product Backlog*. This is the master list of every idea, every request, every improvement. It is ordered by priority. The Product Owner owns the order.

[BREATH]

The second is the *Sprint Backlog*. This is the slice of the product backlog the team has chosen to commit to in this sprint. It is owned by the people doing the work, because they are the ones who know how much they can realistically finish.

[BREATH]

The third is the *Increment* — the actual usable result the team produced this sprint. The increment is what you can show in the sprint review.

---

## Slide 28 — From product backlog to sprint backlog

> Slide on screen for ~30 seconds.

This is what the flow looks like. The full product backlog lives on the left. The team picks the items that fit the sprint goal and the sprint length, and only those items move to the sprint backlog. Most items stay on the product backlog. That is normal — the backlog is meant to be longer than any single sprint can finish.

---

## Slide 29 — A user story has a shape

> Slide on screen for ~30 seconds.

Most items on a product backlog are written as *user stories*. A user story is a short sentence in this shape: *As a [type of person], I want [something], so that [benefit].* The shape forces the team to think about who the work is for and why it matters, not just what it is.

[BREATH]

For example: *As a returning customer, I want to receive a personalised email at Mother's Day, so that I feel remembered.*

---

## Slide 30 — Acceptance criteria

> Slide on screen for ~30 seconds.

Each user story should also have *acceptance criteria* — short, testable conditions that say "this is finished correctly."

[BREATH]

For our Mother's Day email, the acceptance criteria might be: it goes only to customers who bought in the last twelve months; it includes their first name; it links to the Mother's Day landing page; it sends on the morning of the Saturday before Mother's Day. When the team can tick all of those, the story is acceptable. When they can't, it isn't done — it doesn't matter how close it feels.

---

## Slide 31 — The task board

> Slide on screen for ~75 seconds (includes the screen-recorded segment).

Day to day, the team looks at the *task board*. It is the simplest possible view of where every item is right now. Three columns: To Do, In Progress, and Done. Every item from the sprint backlog is a card. Cards move left to right as work progresses.

[BREATH]

A useful upgrade is to add a fourth column: Blocked. Anything the team can't move forward on goes there, with a short note saying why.

[BREATH — pause for screen recording cut]

You can put it on a wall with sticky notes, in a spreadsheet, or in a tool like Trello. The format does not matter — what matters is that any team member can glance at the board and see the state of the sprint.

---

## Slide 32 — Definition of Done

> Slide on screen for ~50 seconds.

Finally — the *Definition of Done*. The Definition of Done is the team's checklist for "really finished." It is the same for every item in the sprint.

[BREATH]

For a marketing campaign, your Definition of Done might say: copy proofread by a second person, design checked on mobile and desktop, scheduled in the sending tool, links tested. For a community project, it might say: parcel packed, route confirmed, recipient contacted, photo taken.

[BREATH]

Without a Definition of Done, "done" means whatever the person who finished the task thinks it means. With a Definition of Done, "done" means the same thing for the whole team.

---

## Slide 33 — What progress can look like

> Slide on screen for ~35 seconds.

This chart is what a healthy sprint looks like as it progresses. The horizontal axis is the working days of the sprint. The vertical axis is the work remaining. As the team finishes items, the line drops. The dashed line shows the ideal pace. The solid line shows what real teams actually look like — mostly tracking the ideal, with the occasional bump as new scope is discovered.

---

## Slide 34 — Worked example: Daniel's Mother's Day campaign

> Slide on screen for ~50 seconds.

Let's anchor everything we just covered with one example. A small business owner — let's call him Daniel — runs a homeware shop and is preparing a Mother's Day marketing campaign. Daniel has eighteen ideas in his product backlog. He picks six items for the first two-week sprint, written as user stories with acceptance criteria. He sets a sprint goal: *Mother's Day email and landing page live by Saturday morning.* He sets a Definition of Done that includes proofreading, mobile check, link testing. He sets up a simple task board. By the Friday of week two, the email is live, he reviews the result with his retail team, and they spend fifteen minutes asking what to do differently before the next sprint starts.

## Slide 37 — Your first sprint plan — 9 steps

> Slide on screen for ~40 seconds.

This is the moment everything we have done becomes useful. We are going to walk together through your first sprint plan, end to end. By the end of this lesson, you will have a one-page document that you could hand to a teammate tomorrow.

## Slide 38 — Walking each step

> Slide on screen for ~5 minutes (with workbook pauses interleaved).

Step one. Open your workbook to the *Product Goal* worksheet. Your product goal is the bigger objective your sprints are working toward. Write yours now.

[PAUSE — 2s, workbook badge]

Step two. Persona. Write down one sentence about the person your work is really for. Their name, their situation, what they are trying to do, what gets in their way.

[PAUSE — 2s, workbook badge]

Step three. Product backlog. Write between five and ten items. Each one in user-story form. Each one short.

[PAUSE — 2s, workbook badge]

Step four. Sprint goal. Look at your backlog. Pick the one outcome that, if delivered in the next two weeks, would matter most. Write it as a single sentence.

[PAUSE — 2s, workbook badge]

Step five. Sprint backlog. Pick the items that contribute to your sprint goal. Be honest about how much your team can do in two weeks.

[PAUSE — 2s, workbook badge]

Step six. Task board. Write each sprint backlog item as a card in the To Do column.

[PAUSE — 2s, workbook badge]

Step seven. Definition of Done. Look at your earlier draft and adjust it now that you have a sprint goal in mind.

[PAUSE — 2s, workbook badge]

Step eight. Sprint review plan. Write down: who will be in the room, what you will show them, how long the meeting will be, and one specific question you want their feedback on.

[PAUSE — 2s, workbook badge]

Step nine. Sprint retrospective questions. Write down the three questions you will ask the team. The classic three are *what went well, what didn't, what will we change next sprint*.

[PAUSE — 2s, workbook badge]

Beautiful. Take a breath. You now have a complete first-sprint plan. Compare it against the *Mini-project submission checklist* at the end of the workbook. Tick each item. If anything is missing, fill it in now.

## Slide 40 — Three honest reminders

> Slide on screen for ~50 seconds.

A few honest reminders before you go.

[BREATH]

First, this is not a Scrum certification. If you want certification, look at the Scrum Guide at scrumguides.org and an accredited training provider. The plain-language terms in this workshop map cleanly to the official ones, so you will recognise everything.
